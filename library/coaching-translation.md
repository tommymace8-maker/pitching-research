## 0. How to Use This Document

Each entry follows one shape:

> **SCIENCE** — the finding, its population, and its evidence grade.
> **STRESS COST** — mandatory. Every velocity cue states what it costs the elbow/shoulder, or states plainly that the cost is unknown.
> **CUE** — what I actually say out loud. Short, external-focus, feelable.
> **DRILL** — the constraint that makes the cue happen without me having to say it.
> **FAILURE ON VIDEO** — what I look for at 240 fps to know it didn't take.

Evidence grades match the library: **[ESTABLISHED] / [EMERGING] / [FOLKLORE]**, plus **`[OFF-POPULATION]`** for youth/unselected-HS samples.

## 1. The Four Standing Rules

**Rule 1 — No velocity cue without its stress cost.**
Within a single athlete, ball velocity and elbow varus torque are locked at **R² = 0.957** (Slowik et al., 2019, n=64 pro). You cannot sell this athlete free velocity. What you *can* sell him is a better exchange rate: elite arms at the same velocity differ by **28% in normalized elbow torque** (Fleisig et al., 2025, n=523).

**Rule 2 — Everything is decided by foot contact.**
Arm cocking is ~100–150 ms. Arm acceleration is ~30–50 ms. Nothing inside those windows is consciously coachable in real time. If a fix has to happen after front foot strike, it is not a fix — it is a wish. Build it into the stride or build it into a constraint.

**Rule 3 — Coach the position, measure the trend, never the norm.**
Population norms tell you the bounds of the possible. They do not tell you where *this* arm should live. Track him against himself: his ER strength, his total rotational motion, his elbow extension, his release consistency.

**Rule 4 — If the pitcher can't feel it, it isn't a cue.**
"Reduce shoulder abduction at foot contact by 8°" is a lab finding. "At front foot strike, I want your throwing elbow at shoulder height, not above it" is a cue. Science stated at a precision the athlete cannot act on is science you have wasted.

## 3. Deep Position #1: Foot Plant → Max External Rotation

### 3.1 Why this window

**FC→MER is ~100–150 ms and it contains peak elbow varus torque, peak shoulder internal-rotation torque, and peak shoulder horizontal-adduction torque.** It is the injury-generating phase for the UCL and the anterior shoulder. It is also, per Rule 2, almost entirely un-coachable *while it is happening.* Everything below is therefore either (a) a stride-phase change that determines what the window looks like, or (b) a capacity change that determines whether the window is survivable.

---

### 3.2 The Eight Interrogation Questions, Answered

---

#### Q1. At foot plant, what is actually resisting the valgus load, and in what order does it arrive?

**ANSWER — sourced to anatomy (§2.9, §1.3) and biomechanics (§4.4).**
The lab reports a *net* internal varus torque near 95–100 N·m, peaking near MER. That net number is the sum of three contributors, modeled at roughly a third each: the **UCL anterior bundle**, the **flexor-pronator mass** (FCU primary, FDS secondary, pronator teres contributory), and the **radiocapitellar/osseous articulation** under compression. Morrey & An's cadaveric 54%-of-varus-restraint figure for the UCL is a *static, isolated-ligament* number and almost certainly overstates the ligament's dynamic share.

Order of arrival: osseous compression is passive and instantaneous with the valgus moment. Flexor-pronator EMG rises **from cocking through acceleration** — i.e., it is ramping *into* the peak, not sitting pre-loaded. The UCL takes whatever is left, and it takes it last.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Your ligament is the backup. Your forearm is the starter. When the forearm is tired, the ligament plays the whole inning."*
**And the drill is:** grip-and-forearm endurance as a *terminal* block, not a warm-up block — heavy-ball holds, towel/rice-bucket work, and pronated carries programmed on his highest-volume days so the forearm is trained in the fatigue state it actually fails in. Then a **grip-cued bullpen**: five pitches where he deliberately increases index/middle-finger pressure into the ball. FDS contraction of the index and middle fingers measurably narrows medial joint gapping under valgus stress (ultrasound/shear-wave elastography). It is the only voluntary act inside this window that changes the load-sharing.

---

#### Q2. Why does shoulder abduction at foot contact raise elbow torque without raising velocity?

**ANSWER — biomechanics §6.2/§6.4 (Fleisig et al., 2025, n=523: 425 pro + 98 D1).**
Shoulder abduction at FC is one of four variables that show up as **positive contributors to normalized elbow varus torque and are not velocity predictors.** Mechanism, stated as hypothesis not fact: an over-abducted humerus at FC arrives at the cocking phase with the forearm more vertical relative to the trunk's rotation plane, which lengthens the effective valgus moment arm through the ball-and-forearm mass as the trunk rotates underneath it. Pro norm is **82–85°** at FC; the ranges pool 78–95°.

**Honest caveat:** this is a cross-sectional multivariate association from a model explaining 40% of torque variance. **No intervention trial exists.** It is the best-supported hypothesis available, not a proven fix.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"At front foot strike I want your throwing elbow level with your shoulder — not above it. Ear-high elbow is a tax you're paying for nothing."*
**And the drill is:** **wall-slot drill into a rocker throw.** Set him with his back a fist's width from a wall in the FC position; the elbow that touches the wall first is over-abducted. Then rocker throws with the same feel at 60–70% into a net, 240 fps camera on the closed side, checked at the FC frame only.

---

#### Q3. Why does elbow flexion at foot contact raise torque, and is it actually changeable?

**ANSWER — biomechanics §6.2; anatomy §2.9.**
Same regression, same list of "free" reductions. Pro norm at FC is **93–101°**, and max elbow flexion at MER is **~89–90°** — i.e., the elbow arrives at FC roughly where it will be at peak load and stays there. Mechanically, more flexion at FC puts more of the forearm-plus-ball mass further from the long axis about which the trunk is rotating it, and the elbow flexion torque required to hold it is itself elevated in pros (3.8–4.1 %BW×BH).

**Where I push back on my colleagues:** of the four "free" reductions, this is the one I am least willing to coach directly. Elbow flexion at FC is not an independent dial — it is the arm path. Change it and you change hand position, arm slot, forearm alignment, and probably pitch shape. **The reduction is only "free" in a regression, not in a bullpen.** See open-disputes.md, Dispute #2.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing directly. I never cue elbow flexion angle.
**And the drill is:** if his elbow flexion at FC is an outlier high, I change it *upstream* via **implement moment of inertia** — a larger, lighter implement (medicine-ball-sized plyo, javelin-style throw) forces a longer, less-collapsed forearm without a single word about the elbow.

---

#### Q4. What actually creates "layback," and how much of the 165–180° is the glenohumeral joint?

**ANSWER — biomechanics §3.2; anatomy §2.6, §2.8, §4.1, §4.3.**
The 165–180° MER figure is **not** glenohumeral external rotation. It is an apparent composite of: true GH ER (limited by the anterior band of the IGHL and the subscapularis checkrein), **scapulothoracic** retraction/posterior tilt/upward rotation, **thoracic** extension and rotation, trunk hyperextension, and elbow/forearm contributions. True GH ER is substantially less.

Two elite-specific facts govern how much a given athlete has: **humeral retrotorsion** rotated his entire arc posteriorly during adolescence and is now bone — locked, non-modifiable; and **acquired anterior capsular laxity** from years of maximal ER has already occurred to some degree in any 85+ arm.

**Therefore:** more MER can come from thoracic extension and scapular posterior tilt (adaptable, cheap) or from the anterior capsule (already-deformed tissue, expensive). **The angle looks identical on video.** This is the single most important thing on this page.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"I never want more layback. I want more room to lay back into — that's your mid-back and your shoulder blade, not your shoulder."*
**And the drill is:** thoracic extension/rotation work over a roller and **serratus + lower-trap upward-rotation work** as standing prep before every high-intent day, then a **wall-slide-to-throw** so he feels the scapula posteriorly tilt before the humerus externally rotates. What I do **not** do is stretch a lax elite arm into more ER.

---

#### Q5. What is happening at the lead leg between FC and MER, and does anything the athlete does in that window matter?

**ANSWER — biomechanics §3.5, §4.6; anatomy §2.2.**
The lead knee is eccentrically loaded and then, in higher-velocity pitchers, **extends** — knee extension FP→BR correlates with velocity at r = 0.29 raw, and max front-knee extension angular velocity at r = 0.25 between subjects / 0.20 within. Lead-leg vertical GRF is r = 0.44 raw but drops to **r = 0.23 once bodyweight is controlled** — roughly 5% of variance. The block **does not** work by driving pelvis rotation (pelvis rotational velocity gain vs velo, **r = −0.07**). It works by decelerating the center of mass and converting linear momentum.

**And it costs.** Max knee extension velocity during arm acceleration is a *positive* contributor to normalized elbow varus torque. The block is a real velocity lever and a real stress lever simultaneously.

Can he do anything about it *within* the 100–150 ms? No. He can only arrive with the leg prepared. **This is a capacity problem wearing a mechanics costume:** eccentric quad strength and rate of force development determine whether the knee extends or keeps flexing, and block quality is one of the first things to degrade under fatigue.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Front leg's job is to stop your body, not to spin it. Head travels out over the front foot and then stops — like you hit a wall with your chest, not like you turned a corner."*
**And the drill is:** **step-back / walking-windup throws onto a firm front leg** (raises incoming momentum so the leg must do more), paired in the weight room with heavy eccentric split squats and drop-landings to single-leg stick. And I say the price out loud: *"This adds velo and it adds elbow load. We're spending, not saving."*

#### Q6. Where in this window does the flexor-pronator mass turn on, and can he change it?

**ANSWER — anatomy §2.9, §2.10.**
FCU and FDS EMG rises **from cocking through acceleration** and provides measurable **stress shielding** of the UCL in competitive pitchers. Grip force is not incidental: FDS contraction of the index and middle finger slips reduces medial joint gapping under valgus stress. That means the one thing the athlete controls in the last 150 ms — **how hard he holds the baseball** — is directly coupled to the load-sharing that keeps his ligament intact.

**[EMERGING]:** whether targeted flexor-pronator training reduces UCL injury *incidence*. Mechanism is solid; the outcome trial does not exist. I am coaching this off mechanism and saying so.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Late in an outing, when your forearm goes soft, the ball gets loose in your hand and your ligament starts working overtime. Grip is arm care."*
**And the drill is:** grip dynamometer reading **before and after** every bullpen and every start, logged. It is a $60 tool. A within-athlete drop of a consistent magnitude, appearing earlier in successive outings, is my best available field proxy for the load-sharing failure the anatomy file describes — and see open-disputes.md Dispute #5, because I do not have validation for that threshold and neither does anyone else.

#### Q7. Is the "trunk before pelvis" failure decided before or after foot plant?

**ANSWER — biomechanics §5.2, §5.3; anatomy §2.3, §2.5.**
Before. Trunk-rotating-early ("flying open") raises torques (Aguinaldo & Escamilla, 2019), and trunk *position at FC* — trunk rotation at foot contact — is a velocity predictor in professionals (β = 0.289). But the anatomical causes sit further upstream: restricted **lead-hip internal rotation** means the pelvis cannot rotate over the planted femur, so rotation gets stolen from the lumbar spine (which offers only ~2° per segment, ~10–13° total) or the thorax simply opens early. Restricted **thoracic rotation** does the same thing from above. And a lead foot landing open leaks rotation before the window even starts.

**Crucial nuance, and this is where I break with the standard teaching:** "fix the sequence" is not a real instruction (§9). What *is* real is a specific, common, upstream failure — chest opening at or before foot strike — with identifiable anatomical causes you can screen.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Your front hip and your chest have to arrive in that order. Belt buckle first, chest second. If your chest gets there with your foot, you've already spent it."*
**And the drill is:** **closed-side 240 fps video, single frame at FC** — chest square to the plate at FC is the failure. If it is present, I screen lead-hip IR and seated thoracic rotation before I touch the delivery, because if the hip is a bony block (FAI morphology is over-represented in baseball players) no drill will fix it and stretching a bony block accomplishes nothing. Drill: **hip-hinge med-ball rotational throws into a wall from a split stance**, with the constraint that the front hip must clear before the ball leaves.

#### Q8. What tissue is at risk in THIS window, versus after ball release?

**ANSWER — anatomy §2.8, §5.1, §5.2; biomechanics §4.3.**
Different phases, different failures, and coaches conflate them constantly.

| Window | Dominant load | Tissue at risk | Symptom timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FC→MER (this one)** | **Torque** — elbow varus ~95–100 N·m; shoulder IR torque 4.9–5.2 %BW×BH | UCL anterior bundle; anterior band of the IGHL; posterosuperior labrum + articular-side cuff (internal impingement); biceps anchor (peel-back) | Medial elbow pain *at MER*; posterosuperior shoulder pain *in late cocking*; a clunk at MER |
| **BR→MIR (deceleration)** | **Force** — shoulder distraction **113.9–114.2 %BW** (~1,060 N for a 95 kg arm) in 30–50 ms | Posterior cuff (infraspinatus, teres minor); scapular stabilizers; biceps anchor | Diffuse shoulder ache *in follow-through* |

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Where it hurts in the delivery tells me what's failing. Pain when you're laid back is a different problem than pain after you let go. I need to know which one — every time."*
**And the drill is:** not a drill, a habit — the question gets asked after every outing and the answer gets written down. Medial elbow at MER, posterior elbow at release, anterior or posterosuperior shoulder at MER: **stop throwing, ⚠ REFER.** Diffuse ache in follow-through that resolves: that is a deceleration-capacity gap, and it gets programmed (posterior cuff eccentrics, plyo catches, reverse-throw decel work), not rested.

### 3.3 Window Summary Card

> **Foot Plant → MER, in one paragraph for the athlete:**
> This is 100–150 milliseconds. It's the hardest thing your elbow will ever do and you cannot steer it while it's happening. Everything about it was decided by the time your front foot hit — how long your stride was, where your elbow was, whether your chest had already opened. What you *can* control is showing up with a forearm that isn't tired, a mid-back that gives your arm somewhere to go, and a front leg strong enough to stop your body. That's the whole job.

**The three video frames I actually check:** (1) FC — chest closed? elbow at shoulder height? front foot angle? (2) MER — is layback coming from the mid-back or from the shoulder alone? (3) BR — did the front knee extend or collapse?

## 4. Effort-Level Dosing — the newest actionable finding

**SCIENCE.** Fleisig's 2026 review proposes **varying effort instead of max effort on every pitch** as a UCL-damage reduction strategy that may also improve performance by disrupting hitter timing (*Sports Biomechanics*, 2026). The supporting kinetics are messier than the proposal implies, and the three primary studies disagree:

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiegen et al., 2023** (*AJSM*) | 10 HS pitchers `[OFF-POPULATION]` | At 75% perceived effort: **81% of max elbow stress, 90% of max velocity.** Per 25% effort reduction: **−13% stress, −7.5% velocity** |
| **Wolf et al., 2025** (*OJSM*, KinaTrax) | 19 collegiate | Only elbow varus torque differed by effort, and only 100% vs 60%: **92.5 → 73.2 N·m (P = .017).** Kinematics unchanged across effort levels |
| **Hyeamang et al., 2026** (*OJSM*) | 38 HS + **24 professional** | At 50% RPE, HS pitchers actually threw **86% of max velocity, 75% of max varus torque, 80% cumulative torque.** Pros were markedly more consistent at submaximal efforts than HS |

**THE SYNTHESIS I'LL DEFEND.** Effort reduction buys torque reduction, but **sublinearly and inefficiently.** Asking for 25% less effort buys ~13–19% less stress and costs ~7.5–10% of velocity. Asking for **half** effort buys only ~20–25% less stress. There is no dosing regime here that produces a large stress reduction at a small velocity cost.

**STRESS COST, stated the other direction:** the cost of *not* doing it is that every pitch is a max-torque pitch. The cost of doing it badly is worse — a pitcher who believes he is at 60% and is actually at 86% velocity has exposed healing tissue to more load than the plan assumed.

**CUE:** *"You don't have three gears, you have about one and a half. When I say 70, I don't trust your 70 and neither should you — we're putting a number on it."*

**DRILL:** every submaximal throwing day is **radar-gated, not feel-gated.** Athlete gets a target velocity band (not a target effort percentage), and the gun is visible to him. This is the practical fix for the Fiegen/Hyeamang finding: perceived effort is an unreliable instrument, measured velocity is not. `[EMERGING — this is my inference from the effort literature, not a tested protocol.]`

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** the tell is not velocity, it is **kinematic drift** — a pitcher "taking something off" who has actually just gotten sloppier. Wolf found kinematics preserved at 60% in collegiate arms; Hyeamang found HS pitchers highly variable at submaximal efforts. If his 70% bullpen looks mechanically different from his 100% bullpen at the FC frame, he isn't throwing at 70%, he's throwing a different pitch.

## 5. Arm Slot: the one lever with published velocity AND stress numbers

**SCIENCE — [EMERGING], but converging from four independent directions.**
1. **Cross-sectional pro data:** professionals sit at a *lower* average slot than high schoolers (58 ± 14° vs 50 ± 11°, where a higher number = more sidearm in the ASMI convention). In professionals, every +10° of slot (lower/more sidearm) associates with **−0.1 %BW×BH in both elbow varus and shoulder IR torque** (Escamilla/Fleisig, 2023).
2. **OpenBiomechanics collegiate data:** n = 66 elite college pitchers; ~**4.23 N·m more elbow varus torque per 10° of higher (more overhand) slot**, with lower slots showing greater varus-torque *efficiency* and IR-torque efficiency — **at consistent velocity across slot groups** (*Sports Biomechanics*, 2024/25).
3. **MLB field data:** release points are ~2 inches lower than 2016; average arm angle down 1.41° since 2020; pitchers dropping slot 2°+ gained **+2.14 run value** and **+18.3 rpm** on the four-seam (Driveline/Sawchik, March 2026).
4. **Torque:** ~4% less elbow varus torque per 10° of slot reduction.

**STRESS COST — and here it runs the right way, which is rare:** lower slot appears to cost **~0.15 mph on average** and buy **~4% elbow varus torque per 10°.** That is the only lever in this entire document where the velocity cost is trivial and the stress reduction is real.

**WHO IT DOESN'T WORK FOR — say this part:** it requires enough wrist mobility (radial deviation) to hold spin efficiency at the new slot; Paul Skenes tried it and reverted. And the relationship **inverts at the HS level** — in high schoolers, lower slots associated with *higher* elbow flexion torque. This is a professional/elite-collegiate finding. For an 85 mph high schooler it is unproven.

**CUE:** *"I'm not lowering your arm. I'm going to let your chest tilt a little less and see where your hand comes out."* (Slot is largely a downstream product of trunk lateral tilt — chasing the hand directly produces a manufactured, uncomfortable slot.)

**DRILL:** slot exploration off flat ground, **five throws each at his slot, slightly under, and clearly under**, with Rapsodo or Trackman on (one system only — never mix). Judge on: spin efficiency held? shape improved? velocity within 1 mph? If all three, it lives.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** a "lowered slot" that is actually increased trunk lateral tilt at BR — which is a **positive contributor to elbow varus torque.** He got the hand lower by leaning, not by changing arm path, and he has paid for it twice.

## 6. Command and Variability — repeatability is not the goal

**SCIENCE — [EMERGING], and genuinely new.** Two 2025–26 findings overturn the standard "repeat your delivery" model.

**Driveline, *The Interaction of Biomechanics and Command* (Feb 2026)** — 27 pitchers (college → MLB), 270 throws, Intended Zone Tracker + Launchpad. Multiple biomechanical features correlated |r| > 0.5 with miss distance. But the PCA was dominated by *negative* correlations, and the authors' conclusion was that **"adjustability was at least as important as repeatability."** Repeatability mattered specifically at **foot plant** (trunk position, shoulder preparation angles). Adjustability mattered *before* FC (angular velocities at peak knee height) and *after* (shoulder horizontal abduction into MER, pronation rate into release).

**Bloebaum (Driveline), uncontrolled-manifold analysis, SportRxiv preprint** — **43,650 game-speed pitches, 2,052 pitchers**, markerless capture. Higher-velocity pitchers show lower absolute joint variability *and* a higher UCM synergy index (partial ρ = 0.22, p < 10⁻⁴⁷ at release) — variability confined to task-preserving directions. **Skill is not less variability. It is better-organized variability.** Convergence is **segment-staggered**: trunk and pelvis reach their variability minimum *before* foot plant; the shoulder only at MER.

> **⚠️ CORRECTED 2026-08-13.** This paragraph originally cited Bloebaum's ρ = 0.22 inside a command section, which read as if the synergy index predicted **command**. **It does not — ρ = 0.22 is an association with PITCH VELOCITY.** Bloebaum's outcome variable is velocity throughout; the paper contains no accuracy or location measure. The release point appears in his methods as the **performance variable being protected**, not as an outcome being predicted — which is why the misreading is easy. The paper still belongs here, because *how* variability is structured is exactly the repeatability/adjustability question. But **the command evidence in this section is the Driveline command study above (n = 27) and Kusafuka 2025 (n = 14) — not Bloebaum.** See `stuff-and-command.md` §8.2. *If a future cycle finds the old framing elsewhere, it is this error propagating; do not re-import it.*

**This still validates Rule 2 from a new angle:** the delivery converges at foot plant and stays adjustable on both sides of it. Note the convergence is a *velocity* finding and the foot-plant repeatability is a *command* finding — they agree, from two independent datasets, which is why the cue below survives.

**CUE:** *"Different every time on the way there, same every time when the foot lands."*

**DRILL:** **variable leg-lift / variable-tempo bullpens converging to one FC position.** Five pitches: slow lift, fast lift, no lift, slide-step, exaggerated drift — all required to arrive at the same front-foot position and same trunk angle at FC. This is a constraint that trains exactly the repeatability/adjustability split the Driveline data describes. Layer in **differential command work** (varying ball size and weight — tennis ball, softball, weighted command ball) to raise learning rate.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** stack the FC frames from five varied-tempo pitches. If the trunk angle and front-foot position scatter, he is repeating the *wind-up* and improvising the *pitch* — exactly backwards.

## 7. Constraint Design Heuristics

From Brice Crider, Driveline (Feb 2025). This is the cleanest published framework for *why* a constraint works, and it lets me design a drill instead of borrowing one.

| Lever | What it does | Coaching use |
|---|---|---|
| **Implement mass** | Heavier = longer force application, lower peak force → teaches position and relaxation. Lighter = higher peak force → *magnifies* sequencing errors at speed | Heavy for organization; light for exposure |
| **Moment of inertia (size/shape)** | Larger implements demand prolonged external rotation before IR. Linear implements (javelin) force center-of-mass management along the pull line | The fix for Q3 (elbow flexion at FC) without cueing the elbow |
| **Center-of-mass kinematics** | Mound vs flat, displacement from target, pre-throw velocity (pulldown, drop-step, roll-in) | Manipulates rotational vs linear momentum — the lever for Q5 |
| **Distal limb kinematics** | Preset arm positions (90/90, figure-8, javelin) remove degrees of freedom | Fastest route to a new arm path; also the easiest to over-use |

**Crider's organizing principle, which I'm adopting verbatim:** *"Manipulating time is the most valuable tool you have"* — the goal is shaping the force-time curve to maximize impulse into the implement.

**STRESS COST — mine, not theirs:** none of these have published kinetics at the 85+ level except weighted implements, where the evidence is genuinely split (see idea-scouting.md #10). A constraint that changes the arm path changes the torque, and nobody has measured most of them.

## 8. Video Protocol — what you can and cannot claim from your camera

**240 fps minimum. 1/1000 s shutter minimum.** At 30 fps you get one frame in the entire acceleration phase. At 60 you get two. Both are useless for anything at or distal to the shoulder.

**Reliably measurable from 240 fps 2D:** stride length (%height, with a calibrated reference in frame), foot placement and foot angle, lead-knee flexion at FC and BR and whether it extends, approximate trunk tilts at BR, the FC/MER/BR timing landmarks, and gross pelvis-leads-trunk.

**NOT measurable without 3D, and I will not pretend otherwise to an athlete:** max shoulder external rotation (composite and badly perspective-distorted), hip-shoulder separation *as a number*, any angular velocity, **and any torque or force whatsoever.**

**On the sleeve:** Motus/PULSE "Stress" underestimates lab elbow torque by ~39% (Boddy et al., 2019). It is a within-athlete workload index and a good one. Never compare it between athletes, never compare it to a published torque value, never compare it to the 32 N·m cadaveric failure figure.

**New methodological caution (Dowling, Slowik & Fleisig, *Sports Biomechanics*, Feb 2026):** sample and low-pass filter frequency choices materially change computed pitching data; published studies use 240–500 Hz sampling and filter cutoffs varying widely, with 13.4 and 18.0 Hz most common. **This is the mechanism behind the 64 vs 100 vs 120 N·m spread across labs.** Practical rule: a torque number without its sampling rate and filter cutoff is not a number.

## 11. Translations — Day 2 (added 2026-08-13)

Twelve findings from `stuff-and-command.md` and `velocity-development.md`, each translated to the standard shape, **each with a measurable check and the sample size needed to detect it.** That last part is not optional: this program has been declaring wins at sample sizes that cannot detect them.

**The realism anchor for everything below:** detecting a **2-inch** command change needs **~196 tracked declared pitches per condition** (`stuff-and-command.md` §11.4). Detecting a **1 mph** velocity change needs roughly **8 separately-dated sessions of ≥25 fastballs on one gun** (§11.13 below). **Anything declared after one bullpen is noise.**

### 11.1 Release angle — 30 cm per degree, and why that is a warning, not a target

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED].** Kusafuka et al. (2020), *Front Sports Act Living* 2:36 — 187 four-seams, R² = 0.97 vertical / 0.96 horizontal: **~30 cm of plate location per 1° of release angle**, vertically and horizontally. Pure trigonometry (`tan 1° × 54.5 ft = 11.4 in`), so **not sample-dependent.** Within-pitcher SD ±1.1° → **~13 in of 1-SD scatter from release angle alone**, which is essentially the entire observed miss.
**And the finding that makes it coachable — Kusafuka et al. (2025), *Sci Rep* 15:12300 (PMID 40210939), n = 14 × 30 balls:** **vertical correction is near-optimal in ALL skilled throwers** — the correlation between a pitcher's lag-1 autocorrelation coefficient and his **elevation**-angle SD was null (**r = −0.18, p = .46**). **Horizontal correction is not.** For **azimuth**: ACF1 vs azimuth-angle SD **r = 0.54, p = .02**, and the **"No"-correction transition probability vs azimuth-angle SD r = 0.73, p < .01.**
⚠️ **Label discipline (fixed 2026-08-13):** these are correlations **between a per-pitcher correction statistic and that pitcher's azimuth variability** — *not* autocorrelation values themselves, and *not* a "staying-in-the-same-state" probability. **The pitchers who fail to correct are the ones with the widest side-to-side scatter.** Direction of the coaching claim is unchanged.

**STRESS COST:** none. This is a measurement and attention change.

> **CUE:** *"One degree of your hand at release is a foot at the plate. You can't feel a degree, so we're not going to try. What separates good command from bad isn't how tight your misses are — it's whether you fix the side-to-side one after you make it. Everybody self-corrects up and down. Almost nobody self-corrects left and right."*

**DRILL:** declared-target pens with a **horizontal-miss ledger**. He calls glove-side or arm-side before I say anything. **On any two consecutive same-side horizontal misses, the next pitch is a deliberate over-correction to the opposite half** — not a "hit the spot" pitch, a "get to the other side" pitch. **Train the correction, not the hit.**

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** *nothing.* This one is invisible on video — which is the point, and it is exactly why release-point video is the wrong instrument (§11.11).

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** track the **lag-1 autocorrelation of horizontal miss sign** across an outing; the run-length of consecutive same-side misses should shorten. **~60–80 declared pitches gives a usable sign-run distribution** (cheaper than the ~200 needed for a 2-inch mean-miss claim, because a run-length statistic is coarser). Baseline him first — an athlete's own starting autocorrelation is the only benchmark that means anything.

⚠️ **The honest limit, and I want it stated:** there is no device in a college program that returns per-pitch release *angle* to a pitcher at a latency he can use. **The 30 cm/° number tells you what NOT to chase.** See `open-disputes.md` #16.

### 11.2 Score miss DIRECTION, not strikes

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED for the magnitudes, EMERGING for the metric].** Three independent methods converge on how far an MLB fastball misses: ~13 in from release-angle physics, **~11 in** from Driveline's Intended Zone Tracker, **~7 in elite / 10+ in poor** from Ludwig, Brill & Wyner's Bayesian intent model (arXiv:2508.19184). **One inch of fastball miss ≈ 0.3 FIP** — comparable to a one-SD increase in Stuff+, where a starter's Stuff+ SD is 12.16 points.

**STRESS COST:** none.

> **CUE:** *"Strikes tell me what the umpire thought. Inches tell me what you did. And the direction tells me whether you're scattered or you're stuck — and those are two different problems with two different fixes."*
>
> **Athlete-facing anchor:** *"The best command in the major leagues misses by about seven inches. The average big leaguer misses by about a foot. If you think you're hitting the glove, you're not — and neither is anybody else."*

**DRILL:** declare the target before every pitch; log **(inches, clock direction)**. Nothing else about the pen changes. **Declared-target-before-the-pitch is the only approach that SOLVES rather than infers the intent problem, and it requires discipline, not hardware.**

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** not on video — on the clipboard. The failure is a coach writing "17 of 25" instead of a number and a direction.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** mean miss distance in inches, by pitch type, **on a delayed retention test (≥24 h, no feedback), ideally in a game.** **~200 declared pitches per condition for a 2-inch claim.** And per Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), **in-session hit rate is not learning** — do not evaluate on it, ever.

### 11.3 Velocity variability IS a command metric

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED — arithmetic on a verified sensitivity, not an empirical command study].** Kusafuka 2020: ~20 cm of vertical location per 1 m/s of release speed. **1 mph (0.447 m/s) ≈ 9 cm ≈ 3.5 inches of vertical miss.** A pitcher whose fastball ranges 91–95 in an outing carries **~14 inches of vertical scatter from velocity alone**, before his arm has done anything wrong. **That is larger than the entire elite-to-average command gap (7 in vs 11 in).**

⚠️ **Label it honestly: this is arithmetic, not a finding.** Nobody has regressed within-outing release-speed SD against vertical miss in a real dataset. It is `stuff-and-command.md` Open Question #6 and it is an afternoon on a laptop.

**STRESS COST:** none.

> **CUE:** *"Your gun readings are a command stat. Every mile an hour you vary is three and a half inches up or down before your arm has done anything wrong. If you were 91 to 95 tonight, you weren't wild — you were inconsistent. Different problem."*

**DRILL:** the **velocity-band pen.** Not "throw 100%" and not "throw 70%" — a **2 mph target band with the gun visible to him.** Radar-gated, not feel-gated (§4). **RPE ≥ 70%**, because there is **no speed–accuracy trade-off in overarm throwing** across 40–100% of max (Urbin et al. 2012) and force-output variability actually **peaks near 60%** — so submaximal command work is simultaneously the least specific and possibly the least accurate condition available.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** the tell is not the number, it is **kinematic drift at the FC frame** between his hardest and softest pitch in the same set. If the two look mechanically different, he isn't throwing at two efforts — he's throwing two different pitches.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** compute **within-outing fastball velocity SD every outing.** *This costs nothing — the readings are already in your radar log.* **Needs ~25 fastballs per outing and ~8–10 outings before a change in an SD is believable** (the SD of an SD is a noisy statistic). **This is the single highest-value zero-cost change in the entire library and I have never heard it coached.**

### 11.4 Arm slot beats IVB roughly 2:1 as a VAA lever — and it is not free

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED — own computation, n = 6,110, R² = 0.999].**
`|VAA| = 9.069 − 1.084·(plate height, ft) + 1.055·(release height, ft) − 0.0927·(IVB, in) − 0.0630·(velocity, mph)`

| Input | Effect on VAA |
|---|---|
| **Release height** | **+1.06° steeper per foot** |
| Pitch location | −1.08° per foot higher — **location is a VAA variable** |
| IVB | only −0.093° per inch |
| Velocity | −0.063° per mph — negligible |
| Extension | **drops out entirely** once release height is in the model |

**Half a foot of release height (0.53°) ≈ 5.7 inches of IVB.** Five extra inches of ride buys 0.46°.

**⚠️ And three prices, all real:**
1. **Platoon.** Andrews (FanGraphs, 27 Nov 2024): lower arm angles perform better against **same-handed** batters; **higher** against opposite-handed.
2. **Command.** Manzi et al. (2021), *J Orthop* 27:28–33, n = 338 professionals, **velocity-matched (p = .055)**: the plus-command group sat at a **higher** arm slot (59.7° vs 54.7°, p = .009).
3. **Arsenal.** Slot changes propagate into spin efficiency and pronation tendency, not just VAA (§11.5).

**STRESS COST:** lower slot buys ~4% less elbow varus torque per 10° in pros — the one lever in this document where the stress ledger runs the right way (§5).

> **CUE:** *"You've been chasing ride. Ride is the expensive way to buy the angle you want — where your hand comes out is worth about twice as much and it's free. But it's not free against lefties, and it might cost you command. So we're testing it, not assuming it."*

**DRILL:** slot exploration off flat ground — five throws at his slot, five slightly under, five clearly under. **One device only; never mix Rapsodo and Trackman.** Judge on three gates: spin efficiency held, shape improved, velocity within 1 mph.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** a "lowered slot" that is actually **more trunk lateral tilt at BR.** He got the hand lower by leaning — which raises elbow varus torque *and* is the exact posture Manzi's n = 338 associates with worse command. **Two prices for one purchase.**

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** **VAA quoted at a STATED location** — *any VAA figure without its location is meaningless.* Shape grades in **~80 pitches** (Stuff+ stabilizes there). **Command grades in ~200–400.** ⚠️ **Do not let the fast measurement decide.** The reason this trade gets made badly is that one side of it reports in a bullpen and the other reports in two months.

### 11.5 Spin efficiency over raw spin — and it is ~65% fixed

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED for the physics; EMERGING for trainability].**
- Raw spin explains **~4%** of cross-sectional IVB variance (R² = 0.041; observed **+0.32 in per 100 rpm** vs **+0.5–0.6** theoretical for a 100%-efficient pitch). **The gap between 0.57 and 0.32 IS the efficiency problem.**
- "Diminishing returns from the lift curve" is **FOLKLORE** — C_L(S) is approximately linear above S ≈ 0.15 and every MLB fastball lives at **S ≈ 0.21–0.24.**
- **⚠️ NEW 2026-08-13 — Rosen, FanGraphs, 13 Apr 2026:** 185 pitchers with ≥25 fastballs in both 2023 and 2026 — **three-year spin-efficiency r² = 0.65.** Efficiency is roughly **two-thirds a fixed trait**, one-third movable.
- **The one documented mover bought it with a delivery change.** Emerson Hancock: arm angle **23° → 13°**, landing moved crossbody — which per Driveline's Spenser Davis *"cut off his tendency to pronate."* Four-seam efficiency fell from 99%, and that was **good** for him: it unlocked a glove-side breaking ball. 2.38 FIP, 30.6% K%. Connor White's caution: *"The arm is not one fixed lever."* And the field's own line: *"once you go supinator, it's hard to go back."*
- **Headroom check:** four-seam efficiency **p75 ≈ 95%, p90 ≈ 98%**, distribution compressed near the ceiling. **Slider median is 31%. Sweeper 51%. Cutter 47%.**

**STRESS COST:** if the efficiency change is bought with a slot change, it carries §5's and §11.4's costs. **A grip change carries none.** Know which one you're buying.

> **CUE:** *"Your spin number is close to fixed and so is how efficient you are — about two-thirds of it, over three years. And here's what nobody says: on your four-seam you're probably already near the ceiling. The league median is 91 and a half percent. The room isn't in your fastball. It's in your breaking ball, where the median is thirty-one."*

**DRILL:** ten pitches per variant, one device. **If he is a supinator sitting at 99% four-seam efficiency, stop trying to make him pronate a changeup** — build him a kick-change or a splitter and put the efficiency work into the slider/sweeper where the room is real.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** an efficiency "gain" that arrived alongside a 2 mph velocity loss, or a shape the hitter picks up earlier.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** ten pitches per variant, **then re-test COLD on a different day.** *A grip that works the day you find it and not the following week is a mood, not a pitch.* And calibrate expectations to r² = 0.65 — **a genuine efficiency change will be small, and if it's large you probably changed his delivery without meaning to.** See `open-disputes.md` #15.

### 11.6 The changeup — buy separation, kill spin, preserve arm speed

**SCIENCE [EMERGING — single-source].** Rosen, FanGraphs, 17 Jun 2025.

> ### ⚠️ READ THE CORRECTION IN `stuff-and-command.md` §3.5 BEFORE USING THIS
> The library briefly carried this finding **inverted.** **Rosen's main argued chain is: velocity DIFFERENTIAL → hitters get out in front (attack direction) → whiffs**, and he calls it strong. **What has basically no relationship to whiff rate is the changeup's ABSOLUTE velocity** — which is why a 92 mph Jax changeup and a 78 mph Gaddis changeup both post ~57% whiff. **Absolute velocity ≠ velocity separation. Do not re-import the collapsed version.**

- MLB changeup velocity separation norm: **7.6 mph** off the four-seam (IQR 6.1–8.7). Splitter: 8.2.
- Gaddis's changeup induced a **−26.1° pull-oriented attack angle** — the most extreme in baseball.
- Spin-axis similarity to the fastball and arm-angle change both showed no predictive value — **each as a single unquantified sentence, no coefficient, no n.** Real, weak. **Not "a published null."**
- **The variable Rosen thinks matters most, he could not test: arm-speed similarity.** Not publicly measurable.
- Driveline's spin target: **500–800 rpm below the fastball.** The splitter class averages **1,390 rpm** — lowest of any pitch — and posts **the best wOBA-against in baseball (.263).**

**STRESS COST — one line:** MLB pitch-tracking studies associate higher changeup velocity and greater changeup spin/movement with UCL-reconstruction odds (Mastroianni et al., *AJSM*, May 2025). Tag it and move on.

> **CUE:** *"Stop trying to make it match your fastball on the spin chart — nobody's shown that matters. Make it slower to spin, and make it identical to throw. If your arm slows down, you've just thrown a batting-practice fastball with a funny grip."*

**DRILL:** **grip changes only.** Ring-finger pressure, lateral finger tilt toward the thumb on a two-seam base, the kick-change spike, the splitter. **Never a delivery change** — a pitch-design project that requires a mechanics change is a mechanical intervention in a costume and it will cost command.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** **arm speed.** Side-by-side 240 fps of the fastball and the changeup, aligned at foot contact. If the hand is slower into MER or the finish is shorter, **the pitch is dead regardless of what the Rapsodo report says.** Arm-speed similarity is the probable hidden variable, nobody can measure it, and **your camera is the only instrument you have for it.**

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** spin and velo gap read in **~10 pitches**. Whiff rate needs far more volume than you have. **So test the actual mechanism instead: hitter timing.** Swings-ahead and foul-ball direction against live hitters — **40–60 live pitches gives a usable read on whether hitters are early**, which is the thing the whole finding turns on.

### 11.7 Stride length — ⚠️ DOWNGRADED 2026-08-13

**SCIENCE — this entry changed today and the Cue Ledger row in §2 is superseded.**

The cross-sectional finding stands: **+0.9 m/s (+2.0 mph) per 10% of body height** (B = 0.089, β = 0.25, p < .001), **no elbow-torque difference across quartiles** (p = 0.072), **n = 315 professionals** — **Manzi JE**, Dowling B, et al. (2021), *J Sports Sci* 39(23):2658–2664, PMID 34240663. ⚠️ *First author is Manzi, not Dowling — corrected 2026-08-13. **Cross-sectional/observational**, which is the whole point of what follows.*

**But it has been experimentally manipulated by two independent groups and neither found a gain:**
- **Ramsey DK, Crotin RL, White S (2014), *Hum Mov Sci* 38:185–196** (PMID 25457417) — n = 19, randomized crossover, **±25%** of self-selected stride: **hand and ball velocity equivalent across conditions.**
- **Matsuda et al. (2025), *Front Sports Act Living*** (PMID 40264933) — n = 20 college, **±20%**: under 72.7 · **normal 75.8** · over 72.7 mph. **His own stride was significantly faster than both. Lengthening made them slower.**
- Normalization disputed twice, and the two critiques disagree with each other: Solomito 2023 (n = 99, %BH null) and Yanagisawa 2020 (n = 18, **%BH r = 0.36, p = .15** — while *absolute* stride length was r = 0.55, p = .02).

⚠️ **The Buffalo cohort is ONE experiment reported across seven papers** (PMIDs 25457417, 26707678, 23917472, 25804970, 29578381, 37109515, plus *Life* 15(9):1440). **Count it once.**

> **A long stride is a MARKER of a pitcher who throws hard, not a LEVER that makes one.**

> **CUE — retired:** *"get your stride out toward 80% of your height."* **I am not saying this anymore.** Cueing a longer stride is precisely the experiment that failed, twice.
>
> **What replaces it:** nothing said to the athlete. A **short stride becomes a question for me, not an instruction for him** — *what is stopping it?*

**DRILL — constraint, never cue.** Screen the candidate constraints: **drive-leg ankle dorsiflexion** (a 4-week resistance-band ankle-mobility + balance block on D1 pitchers was run and defended at Univ. of Nevada, Reno in spring 2026 with pre/post stride length — **results unpublished; go get the thesis**); **incoming momentum** (COM velocity *before* peak knee height is the only COM window associated with velocity in 332 in-game D1 pitchers — Zappa 2026); **trail-leg eccentric strength**; **lead-hip abduction capacity**. Then use tasks in which a longer stride is the *solution*: **step-back and walking-windup throws**, mound-to-flat contrast.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** a longer stride achieved by **falling** — front side collapsing, trunk tilting further forward at release. He bought extension and a lower release height, not arm speed, and the forward tilt is the posture the command data penalizes.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING — and this is the whole methodological point:**
A longer stride does three things at once: **lengthens stride, raises extension, lowers release height.** All three change the *pitch* without changing the *arm*.

> **Report RELEASE velocity, never perceived/effective velocity.** `PV = release velocity × 54.17 / (60.5 − extension)` — extension is baked in **by construction.** Evaluate a stride project on perceived velocity and you will "detect" a gain that is pure geometry. **Release velocity is the only clean outcome.**

Track all four simultaneously: stride %BH (fixed camera, calibrated reference in frame, same landmark, ≥10 pitches averaged), release velocity, extension, release height. **If release velocity is flat and extension rose, you moved his release point. Say so.**

⚠️ **Measurement trap:** single-camera markerless stride error is **5.75% of body height**, and the change worth 2 mph is **10% of body height.** *The instrument error is more than half the effect.* **Establish his own test-retest error before claiming anything. If you can't measure his stride to better than ~2% BH repeatably, you cannot run this project.**

**One finding worth keeping:** the *shorter* stride cut mean heart rate **11.1 bpm** across an 80-pitch simulated game at no velocity cost. **For a starter, stride length may be a stamina lever rather than a velocity lever.**

### 11.8 Pelvis open at foot contact — and the distinction coaches get wrong

**SCIENCE [EMERGING].** Dowling et al. (2022), *OJSM*, **n = 157 professionals**: open-pelvis vs closed-pelvis at FC = **39.1 vs 38.4 m/s (87.5 vs 85.9 mph), p = .029 — ~1.6 mph**, with longer stride, greater lead-knee flexion, faster peak knee-extension velocity, and **no significant difference in elbow varus torque.**

⚠️ **My honest read: every co-travelling variable in that list is itself a velocity correlate.** "Open pelvis at foot contact" may be a *label* for "the delivery that did everything else right." It is a between-group cross-sectional split, so **the within-athlete effect of moving it is unknown and could be zero** — which is precisely the error that just cost us the stride-length recommendation (§11.7).

**STRESS COST:** none documented.

> **CUE:** *"Belt buckle can be early. Chest cannot. When your foot lands, your hips should already be turning toward the plate — your shoulders shouldn't be."*
>
> **This is the whole point and it is the thing that gets confused: an open PELVIS is not an open CHEST.** §3.2 Q7's failure mode — chest square at foot strike — is still a failure. The pelvis arriving early is not.

**DRILL:** hip-hinge med-ball rotational throws from a split stance, with the constraint that **the front hip must clear before the ball leaves.** If the pelvis is closed at FC, **screen lead-hip internal rotation and trail-hip extension before touching the delivery** — FAI morphology is over-represented in baseball players and a bony block does not respond to a drill.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** two of them, and most coaches screen only one line instead of two. (a) He "opens the pelvis" by **landing with an open front foot**, which leaks rotation before the window even starts. (b) He opens the chest along with it — the trunk-before-pelvis failure that raises torque.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** **pelvis-minus-shoulder angle at FC**, same camera position, ≥10 pitches, **tracked as a within-athlete trend only.** ⚠️ **Absolute thresholds do NOT transfer across capture systems** (Lerch et al. 2025) — do not import a number out of a paper and hold an athlete to it.

### 11.9 Absolute impulse — not per-kilogram, not jump height

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED].**

| Metric family | Predicts velocity? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **CMJ concentric impulse (absolute)** | **Yes — strongest** | r = **0.71**, n = 19 D1 (Sakurai 2024) |
| **Absolute peak power** | **Yes** | r = 0.43–0.68 across three samples, D1 ×2 + MiLB |
| **Body mass / lean mass** | **Yes** | r = **0.58** / **0.52** (King 2025) |
| **Drop-jump RSI** | **Yes — the only jump quality surviving into pros** | R² = **0.30**, p = .005, n = 53 pro (Wong 2023) |
| Power **÷ body mass** | **No** | r = 0.19 **NS** |
| **Jump height** | **No** | r = **0.07 NS** |
| Sparta Score | No | r = −0.06 NS |

**Momentum is mass × velocity. Divide power by body mass and you delete the term carrying the signal. Measure jump height and you are measuring a mass-normalized quantity in disguise.**

**STRESS COST:** none in the weight room. (Velocity itself is not free — Oyama's positive trunk-strength trial also raised elbow varus moment.)

> **CUE:** *"Nobody ever got a hitter out with a vertical jump. Your mass is part of the machine, not a penalty you carry. We're training total force and total power — the number on the plate, not the number divided by your bodyweight, and not how high you got off the ground."*

**DRILL:** force-plate battery — **squat jump ABSOLUTE peak power (watts)**, CMJ **RSI-modified**, 10-to-5 hop **RSI**, **IMTP net peak force.** Plus the **lateral-to-medial bound** (the one field test that correlated with throwing velocity across all four throwing conditions and in both RH and LH throwers — Lehman 2013) and the **stride-leg unilateral CMJ** (r ≥ 0.65, Lis 2023). **Do not coach unilateral asymmetry toward zero** — pitchers jump significantly higher on the stride leg and that asymmetry is directional and normal.

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** not video — the testing report. An athlete who "added two inches to his vertical" moved a variable with **r = 0.07.** An athlete who added 12 lb of lean mass and improved his drop-jump RSI moved the two with the best evidence in the literature.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** absolute peak power, IMTP net peak force, RSI, and lean mass — **same device, every 8 weeks**, plotted against his own release velocity.
⚠️ **The constraint that stops you cheating: the entire battery predicts velocity at R² = 0.54 with ±2.7 mph mean absolute error — larger than a realistic annual gain. NEVER validate a strength block against a predicted velocity.** Validate the strength block against the strength quality. Velocity is a separate readout on a slower, noisier clock (§11.13).

### 11.10 Extension — the cheapest "mph" available

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED — formula recovered empirically across 6,109 pitches, implied reference distance SD only 0.216 ft].**

> **Perceived Velocity = Release Velocity × 54.17 / (60.5 − Extension)**

**≈1.7 mph per foot at 94 mph; ~1.55 at 85 mph; ~1.83 at 100.** It is **multiplicative, so harder throwers get more from the same gain.** MLB 2024 four-seam mean extension **6.51 ft**; p95 7.30; **~7.5 ft is a practical ceiling**, not a tail you can push. Extension ≈ **1.04× pitcher height**.

**Two qualifications the enthusiasts skip:** (1) extension is mostly anthropometry plus stride length — **and stride length just got demoted**, so this is not an independent lever either; (2) **extension lowers release height and flattens VAA** — good for a four-seam at the top, **bad for a curveball.**

**STRESS COST:** unknown. Nobody has measured kinetics against extension directly.

> **CUE:** *"Some of the five miles an hour you want, you can buy without throwing any harder. Six inches further out front is worth about three-quarters of a mile an hour to the hitter's eye. It won't show on the gun. It'll show in his swing."*

**DRILL:** not a drill — a measurement plus the momentum work in §11.7. **And chase it globally, never per-pitch:** extension is nearly constant across pitch types league-wide (6.35–6.54 ft).

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** "extension" bought by **falling toward the plate at release** — more forward trunk flexion, which the n = 338 command data penalizes and which is a downstream marker of a block that already worked, not a lever.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** Trackman/Hawk-Eye reports it directly; **20–30 pitches gives a stable mean.** League SD is 0.45 ft, so **a 0.3 ft change is a real move.** ⚠️ **Report release velocity separately from perceived velocity, always — otherwise you cannot tell an arm-speed gain from a geometry gain**, and geometry is what you just bought.

### 11.11 Release-point variability does not predict walks — and I was wrong for twenty years

**SCIENCE [ESTABLISHED as a null, n = 344 MLB].** Wakamiya et al. (2024), *Front Sports Act Living* 6:1447665 — 344 MLB pitchers, 300,884 four-seams, 2021–2023.

| Outcome | Model R² | Release-point term |
|---|---|---|
| xFIP | 0.207 | RPX variability β = +0.161, p = .002 (2nd predictor) |
| K/9 | 0.345 | β = −0.122, p = .006 |
| HR/9 | 0.072 | β = +0.168, p = .002 |
| **BB/9** | **0.011** | **Not significant. Only velocity entered** |

> **Release-point consistency is a DECEPTION variable, not a command variable.** It relates to strikeouts and home runs — what the hitter reads out of the hand — and has essentially no relationship to walk rate. **Exactly what §11.1 predicts:** release *position* moves location ~1:1 against a ~30 cm/° *angular* term. It cannot carry command because command does not live there.

**My honest answer as a coach: it matches what I actually see, and it contradicts what I have been saying.** The wild pitcher is not the one with a scattered release plot — he is the one whose misses run in the same direction. The visibly scattered guys are frequently the deceptive ones. **What the study cannot see:** it is season-aggregated, starters only, and pools intentional release differences across locations and pitch types with unintentional ones. **A release point that moves in the 6th when it didn't in the 1st is still a fatigue signal**, and that is a different claim.

> **CUE:** *"I'm not chasing your release point around a screen anymore. In three hundred and forty-four big leaguers it explains one percent of walk rate. What it predicts is strikeouts and homers — which means it's about what the hitter sees, not about where the ball goes."*

**DRILL — split by goal, and never conflate them again:**
- **For deception:** train **horizontal release-ANGLE overlap** between fastball and breaking ball. Rosen's Kirby Corollary — HRA overlap predicts swings at **59% accuracy vs Stuff+'s 54%**; slider Stuff+ vs called-strike% **r² = 0.001**, release-angle overlap **r² = 0.12–0.20.** It is a **static property of the delivery, trainable in a bullpen.**
- **For command:** the miss-direction ledger (§11.1–11.2).

**FAILURE ON VIDEO:** a coach stacking release-point plots and calling it command work.

**HOW WE KNOW IT'S WORKING:** deception — HRA overlap from Trackman across **~100 pitches of each type**. Command — the miss ledger, ~200 declared pitches.

### 11.12 Dispute #12 in the bullpen — velocity-optimal or command-optimal?

**SCIENCE:** the two literatures collide at the same landmark in the same population at the same velocity. See `open-disputes.md` #12.

**I have to pick for a real athlete, so here is how I pick:**

| Athlete | Pick | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **18–21, needs velocity to be seen at all** | **Velocity-optimal** | A 21-year-old at 88 with plus command is org-filler. At 94 with 12-inch command he gets drafted and then gets three years of professional instruction. **The market prices velocity as an option on future command, and it is right to** |
| **22–25, already 93+, in pro ball, not getting outs** | **Command-optimal** | He is past the window — expected year-over-year change is negative above 93. And **1 inch ≈ 0.3 FIP ≈ a full SD of Stuff+**, more than any pitch-design project will deliver |
| **88–90 college arm, average command** | **Neither** | **Buy extension, buy slot, buy the missing pitch.** All three are cheaper than either horn, and none requires resolving the dispute |

> **CUE — to be said out loud before you push, every time:** *"What I'm about to ask you for buys velocity and it costs command. That might be the right trade for you right now. It is not automatically the right trade, and I'm not making it silently."*

⚠️ **And the caveat in writing: the dispute may be a mirage.** The velocity side rests on trunk flexion at BR, β = 1.829, implying **+8.3 mph per 10°** — an effect size that is almost certainly non-causal, and which this library already labeled *"a marker of a good delivery, not a lever."* **The stride-length collapse (§11.7) makes that reading more likely, not less** — it is the same error, one variable over. **What certainly exists is a bad cue that two literatures independently flag. Whether a real trade sits underneath it is unproven.**

### 11.13 How long before you can believe a VELOCITY change

The command side of this library has its power analysis (§11.4 of `stuff-and-command.md`: **~196 declared pitches to detect 2 inches**). The velocity side has never had one. Here it is.

Assume a **between-session mean-fastball-velocity SD of ~1.0 mph.** ⚠️ **This is an assumption — measure your athlete's own.** You need his *session-to-session* variation, not his within-outing SD. Paired, α = .05, 80% power:

| Change to detect | d | Sessions needed per condition |
|---|---|---|
| **0.5 mph** | 0.5 | **~31** — *you cannot detect this in a season. Stop trying* |
| **1.0 mph** | 1.0 | **~8 separately-dated sessions**, ≥25 fastballs each |
| **2.0 mph** | 2.0 | **~2–3 sessions** |

**Consequences, plainly:**
- **A velocity intervention needs roughly eight dated sessions on ONE gun before a 1 mph claim is anything but noise.** That is a whole fall.
- **It is still an order of magnitude cheaper than the command equivalent**, which is exactly the measurement-convenience asymmetry that made velocity development a commercial industry and command development a hobby. Know that you are being steered by it.
- **Never validate against a predicted velocity** — capacity models carry ±2.7 mph error, larger than the effect.
- **One gun, one position, one operator.** A radar change mid-project ends the project.
