## 1. The headline

**The rotational medicine ball throw is the most widely performed "velocity exercise" in college baseball and it has no intervention evidence in baseball pitching whatsoever.** The practice rests on a single cross-sectional coefficient — **r = 0.62** between rotational med-ball throw velocity (RMBTV) and pitching velocity in **15 NCAA Division III pitchers** (Taniyama 2021) — and three facts about that coefficient are decisive:

1. **In its own paper it fits the SWING better than the PITCH** (bat speed r = 0.65, p = 0.003; pitch velocity r = 0.62, p = 0.02). The authors' stated mechanism is long-axis rotation shared with hitting. **The pitching industry adopted a hitting test** (F-748).
2. **Its 95% confidence interval on R² runs from 2.5% to 73.8%** (DERIVED, F-749). "Explains 38% of the variance" is the midpoint of an interval from trivial to dominant.
3. **Corrected for restriction of range to an 85+ staff it predicts r ≈ 0.30–0.44** (DERIVED, F-750) — and the only higher-velocity dataset in existence, Tread Athletics' unpublished NAIA data, reports **r ≈ 0.30–0.44**.

**Verdict: MARKER, and a contaminated one.** Keep the throw as cheap, well-tolerated ballistic trunk work. Stop calling it a velocity driver.

### 2.3 The accuracy result — Dispute #27's eleventh venue, and the first one that isn't empty

**Two independent interventions raised velocity and measured accuracy. Both found nothing** (F-753):
- Raeder 2015: throwing velocity **+14%**, throwing precision **unaffected**.
- F-034: peak pitch velocity significantly up, **control and accuracy unchanged in both groups**.

Ten prior venues of Dispute #27 recorded the *absence* of accuracy outcomes. This is the first where the measurement exists, and velocity and accuracy **dissociated**. That is a stronger statement than the absence was — and it is the direct answer to "does rotational power buy command." No.

## 3. What the test measures — the construct problem (F-756)

Four documented properties, jointly incompatible with "it measures rotational power":

1. **It does not correlate with measured trunk strength.** Sell 2015: rotational toss ICCs **0.835–0.909** but **"not related to isokinetic strength."** A test named for core strength that does not track core strength.
2. **It is dominated by body mass.** Overhead variant vs body mass **r = 0.78**; partialling body mass dropped its strength correlations to 0.59 / 0.72; **partialling body mass AND gender made them non-significant**; power expressed *relative* to body mass or lean mass vs throw distance **r = 0.27 / 0.28, non-significant**; adolescent handball **%body fat alone explained 37%** while height and mass did not predict significantly. A 2026 *Scientific Reports* paper: throw distance is **"directly confounded by body mass... systematically biasing performance rankings and talent identification protocols."**
3. **It fits the swing better than the pitch** (§2.1).
4. **Jumps failed in the same sample where it passed** (§2.1).

**The two readings, and they point opposite ways.**
- **(a) Rotational specificity is real.** Plane and time domain match the pitch; jumps don't; that is why only the throw passed.
- **(b) The test passes because it IS a throw.** A max-intent throw by a selected thrower shares task, intent, skill *and body size* with pitching. The correlation is then a tautology about thrower-ness and size, not a measurement of a trainable quality.

🚨 **No reported analysis distinguishes them.** The deciding analysis is **one partial correlation controlling for body mass**, on data Taniyama's authors already hold. **It is the cheapest decisive item in the topic** (F-751). → **Dispute #84.**

MDC₉₅ = 1.96 × √2 × SEM. On a throw of a few metres, **68 cm is roughly a 10–14% change** before one athlete's improvement is distinguishable from error. **And no reliability figure of any kind exists for the velocity-measured rotational throw in any baseball sample** — nineteenth entry in the registry's MDC pattern.

**Mitigation the program can apply today:** 3 max-intent reps with the best kept, fixed surface and warm-up, and **compute the program's own test–retest SD from two sessions a week apart.** That single number is worth more here than anything in the literature.

## 5. The accounting question (F-757, Dispute #85)

**Zero workload, soreness, range-of-motion or tissue-cost outcomes exist across the entire med-ball training literature.** The outcome columns are velocity, bat speed, isokinetic strength, torso strength and throw distance.

The only cost information retrievable is coaching-blog consensus: **introduce high-velocity rotational throws late, not early, in the offseason** — obliques and posterior shoulder.

**The structural parallel is Dispute #79** (is a plyo-ball throw a plyometric or just a throw?), and the med-ball case is cleaner: a **2.72 kg implement, max intent, the same long-axis rotation, from the same hip-to-trunk sequence, by the same athlete, on the same day as his throwing.** If it is throwing, the comparison arm for any med-ball block is **equal-volume throwing**, not "no med balls."

**What costs nothing and does not require evidence: put the throws on the throwing sheet.**

## 6. What a college program can and cannot do

### 6.1 Cannot: test the training block (F-758, DERIVED)

Two-arm, α = .05, power 0.80, true between-arm difference **1.0 mph**:

| 85+ between-subject SD | d | n per arm | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0 mph | 0.50 | 63 | **126** |
| 2.5 mph | 0.40 | 99 | **198** |
| 3.0 mph | 0.33 | 142 | **284** |

A staff is ~15. ⚠️ The SD is a **corpus assumption** — no roster-level or start-to-start fastball SD has ever been published for an 85+ arm. **Fifth independent arrival of the detection wall** (F-506, F-476, F-623, F-681).

### 6.2 Can: audit the marker (F-759)

**Per pitcher, once.** (a) 6-lb rotational MB throw velocity, 3 max-intent reps, best kept, fixed surface and warm-up. (b) Body mass to 0.1 kg the same morning. (c) Current best fastball **from the existing radar log** — not a fresh pen, because a visible gun is worth **+7.9–12.9%** of peak output (F-698/F-706).

**Three analyses.** (1) RMBTV × best fastball across the staff. (2) **The same, with body mass partialled out — the one that matters.** (3) Within athlete across the block: Δ RMBTV × Δ fastball.

**Pre-registered prediction (F-750):** raw r ≈ 0.30–0.44; partialling body mass pulls it toward zero.

**The power statement, both halves.** At n = 15, **r_crit (df = 13) = 0.5139**. The staff **can falsify the claim as marketed** (r = 0.62) and **cannot confirm the restriction-corrected version** (r ≈ 0.35 needs n ≈ 62). ⚠️ **A null at n = 15 is therefore not evidence of no relationship** — it is evidence that the published coefficient does not reproduce here, which is precisely the claim coaches are acting on. At n = 22, r_crit = 0.4133.

## 7. Honest reading for a coach

**Say this:** *"The med ball is cheap ballistic trunk work. There is no evidence it adds a mile an hour to a baseball, because nobody has ever run that study. The number we test is partly a measure of how big and explosive you are. It goes on the throwing sheet. And it is a velocity bet, not a command bet — the two times anyone checked, command didn't come along."*

**Do not say:** "explains 38% of velocity," "r = 0.62," "medicine ball training adds 6.4%," "it builds rotational power," or "core training raises velocity by g = 0.70."

**Keep it because:** the time domain and movement plane are right, the mechanism (F-065, F-067: the trunk is the generator, the arm a transfer element) is the best-supported mechanism in velocity training, athletes tolerate it, and the one above-floor trial that contains it was positive on peak velocity.

**Do not substitute it for:** the interventions with above-floor evidence — F-033 (lumbopelvic-hip strength, 8 weeks, ball speed up), F-034's full package, and the strength work in `strength-training-velocity.md`.

**For a two-way player:** this is genuinely his exercise, and the evidence is better for the swing than for the mound.
