## 2. The two trials that exist, in full

### 2.1 McEvoy & Newton (1998) — the only baseball one (F-777)

*J Strength Cond Res* 12(4):216–221. **n = 9 training vs 9 control, professional, age 24 ± 4.** 10-week
pre-season, 3 sessions per 2 weeks. **Explosive squat jumps + bench throws at ~30–50% 1RM.**
**Throwing speed +2.0 ± 1.5% (p ≤ .05); control "no change."**

**Four disqualifiers:**
1. **Lower body not isolated** — squat jumps co-administered with bench throws.
2. **Outcome is a THROW, not a PITCH** — mixed professional squad, mound status unknown.
3. **Baseline velocity unretrievable** — the floor cannot be checked.
4. **Within-arm significance pattern** — "treatment improved, control didn't" = two tests, not an
   interaction (F-779).

**+2.0 ± 1.5% at 90 mph = +1.8 mph, SD 1.35 mph, across nine men.** Several gained nothing.

### 2.2 Makni et al. (2024) — the only sprint-training one, in any sport (F-778)

*Biology of Sport* 41(4):293–304, PMID 39416498. **n = 27 male handball players, age 14.4**, randomised to
**belt-resisted sprint (9) / vest-resisted sprint (8) / normal sprint (8)**, 6 weeks in-season.

- Belt group improved **all** tests (moderate-to-large).
- Vest group improved sprint, standing long jump, five-jump test, **throw velocity** (small-to-large).
- **The unresisted-sprint control improved change-of-direction ONLY.**
- ⭐ **Change in throw velocity correlated with CHANGE in sprint time and jump ability, r = 0.40–0.69,
  p < 0.01** — a within-athlete change-score association, the evidence class F-740 says does not exist
  anywhere. At n = 27 its CI is roughly ±0.3.

**Triple mismatch: age 14.4, handball, 425 g ball (Dispute #85).** Magnitudes reported as within-arm
qualitative labels, so the abstract's between-arm implication is not a between-group effect size.

**The one genuinely useful thing it establishes: the design is runnable.** Three arms, six weeks, resisted
vs unresisted vs nothing — and it has never been done in baseball.

## 3. What does not exist (F-776)

1. **Zero sled / resisted-sprint trials with a throwing outcome in baseball, any level.** The one baseball
   sled study (Podium 17(1):177–195, 2022, n = 10) measured 60-yard time and squat strength only.
2. **Zero lower-body-only resistance trials with a pitch-velocity outcome, any population.**
3. **Zero Olympic-lifting or trap-bar trials with a throwing outcome in baseball.**
4. **Zero lower-extremity-only plyometric trials with a velocity outcome in baseball.** F-722 (Carter 2007,
   +2.00 mph, n = 24 D1) is upper-extremity.
5. **The field's own taxonomy has no lower-body category** — Fredriksen & van den Tillaar (2024),
   *Sports Med Open*, sorts 17 studies into weighted balls / forearm loading / pulley devices /
   resistance bands.

**⚠️ PARTIAL CORRECTION TO F-038.** F-038 claimed no intervention study exists for sprint training with a
pitch-velocity outcome. **For sprint training that is now false as an absolute** — one trial exists (F-778),
in 14-year-old handball players. For Olympic lifts in baseball it still stands. **Same lesson as the F-042
correction: this corpus's gap claims are search-failure claims until somebody searches again.**

## 4. Why the gap exists (F-782)

- The archetypal "general resistance training" program in the baseball velocity literature contains
  **21 exercises: 17 upper-body, 4 lower-body** (as described in *JSCR* 15(1):148–156, 2001).
- Of this corpus's four controlled non-implement trials with a positive velocity outcome — **F-033**
  (lumbopelvic-hip), **F-034** (rotational med-ball/tubing), **F-037** (Throwers Ten / Keiser / upper plyo),
  **F-722** (upper plyo) — **not one is a lower-body program.**
- The research tradition grew out of rehabilitation and arm care, where the shoulder is the organ of
  interest.

**The warning this carries for the corpus itself:** if exercise selection in the literature is biased, then
the marker/lever ledger inherits that bias. The reason lower-body variables appear here only as markers
(F-003, F-004, F-006, F-009, F-012) may be a property of what was *programmed*, not of what works.

## 5. The marker side, and the reliability problem that may undercut all of it

| Finding | Test | Result | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| F-006 | drop jump / RSI | **R² = 0.30, RSI p = .002** | n = 53 professional |
| F-003 | CMJ concentric impulse (absolute) | r = 0.71; peak power r = 0.68 | n = 19 D1 |
| F-004 | bilateral jump **height** | r = 0.07 NS — **underpowered null**, CI [−0.28, +0.40] | n = 33 D1 |
| F-005 | power **per kg** | r = 0.19 NS vs absolute r = 0.43 (p = .014) | n = 33 D1 |
| F-009 | **lateral-to-medial jump** | **R² = 0.322**; only test surviving across all four throw conditions, both hands | n = 42 mixed college |
| F-012 | 10 m / 30 m sprint ratio | t = 2.98, p = .004; model adj. R² = 0.230 | n = 64 tryout, **~79.5 mph** |
| F-781 ⚠️ | CMJ force-plate metrics | "no strong correlation"; **ICC = 0.36** × 2 metrics | n = 23 D2, **conference abstract** |

**F-781's arithmetic, source-independent:** ICC = 0.36 → SEM = 0.80 SD → **MDC₉₅ = 2.22 SD.** A metric whose
minimal detectable change exceeds two between-athlete SDs cannot track an individual. **If that survives
peer review, Peak Relative Braking Force and Net Positive Impulse are unusable for monitoring.** Hold it
loosely: 10 s rest between reps makes it a *within-session* estimate, the abstract conflates ICC with
correlation, and n = 23 with no tables.

**Restriction of range (F-750), fifth landing, directionally:** F-009's R² = 0.322 (r ≈ 0.567) in a mixed
college sample → predicted **r ≈ 0.30–0.44 (R² ≈ 0.09–0.19)** on an 85+ staff → a D2 staff reporting "no
strong correlation" is consistent with that. **Weak instance** — abstract, n = 23.

## 6. The momentum confound (F-783) — the cleanest thing in the topic, and it needs no source

A Japanese study (**n = 31 college pitchers**, Rapsodo, sprints 10–400 m) reports ball speed correlating
**positively with momentum and kinetic energy at 10/30/50/100 m**, **negatively with running SPEED at
200/400 m**, and positively with **body weight, BMI, lean mass, muscle mass, SMI**.

**Momentum = m·v. Kinetic energy = ½m·v². Both are mass-weighted.** The same paper reports mass correlates
with ball speed. So "sprint momentum predicts ball speed" is largely **F-001 (r = 0.58) restated with a
stopwatch attached** — and F-005's registered coaching line already said it: *"dividing power by body mass
deletes the term carrying the signal. Momentum is mass × velocity."*

**The negative 200/400 m correlation is the honest half** and points the same way: the hardest throwers were
the worst longer-distance runners, i.e. bigger and more powerful.

🚨 **The decisive measurement is unreported and it is one regression: ball speed on 10 m momentum,
controlling for body mass.** Structurally identical to Dispute #84's unreported med-ball partial.
**One regression, two disputes.**

⚠️ **Mirror warning:** mass-normalising is not the fix (F-004's correction). The fix is a partial
correlation.

## 7. Lower-limb fatigue — three studies, three answers (F-780, Dispute #89)

| Source | n | Design | Jump output | Ball speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **F-359** (Yanai 2018, PMC6028199, **source-verified**) | 18 collegiate | 117 pitches / 9 innings | **no squat-jump variable moved** | fell |
| **PMID 40815189** (2025) | 12 collegiate | 7 simulated innings | **fatigue developed** | **stable** |
| **Chang 2026** (DOI 10.1177/17479541261451409) | 20 (10 D1 / 10 D2) | 7 innings, 15 FB/inning | **CMJ fell in BOTH** | **D1 −2.9% NS; D2 −5.7% sig. from 3rd inning** |

F-460 established F-359's null could only have detected a large effect, so the contradiction may be
resolvable rather than real. **The level split is the interesting part and it is the corpus's own theme in a
new design: the higher-level arm fatigued just as much and held its velocity anyway.** No mean velocity is
retrievable for any of the three samples.

## 9. What to actually say and do

**The sentence for a staff:** *"Lower-body training is in your program because of how hard throwers are
built, not because anybody has moved a fastball with it."*

**The sentence for a pitcher:** *"Getting faster is good for your body and good for your draft profile and
I can't promise you one mile an hour for it. Nobody has ever tested it on an arm like yours."*

**Keep:** sprint work 2×/week, 6–8 × 20–30 m, full recovery, off-season — on cost/benefit grounds (cheap,
low-risk, holds athleticism), not velocity grounds. Watch for it crowding the throwing day it sits beside
(F-690, same-session interference).

**Test, don't train:** the lateral-to-medial jump (F-009). One session, 3 attempts per leg, record best.
Expect R² ≈ 0.09–0.19 in our range, so **credit nothing to it.**

**Do this week:** the between-day CMJ ICC audit (F-781). 8–10 arms, 3 CMJs, two separate days, same time of
day, same shoes, same warm-up. **Near 0.36 → stop monitoring that metric. Above 0.85 → it is usable and
Chang 2026 becomes worth acting on.**

**Standing rule, unchanged:** ~200 tracked pitches to detect a 2-inch command gain. One bullpen proves
nothing.
