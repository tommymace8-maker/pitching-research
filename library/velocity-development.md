## 0. Population Scope and Evidence Labels

**Hard floor: 85 mph.** Elite HS (showcase/D1-committed/draft-followed) → NCAA D1 → MiLB → MLB. Target 90–95+. High training age, real strength, mechanics already competent.

> **EVIDENCE LABELS**
> - **[ESTABLISHED]** — Replicated, or basic physiology not seriously disputed. Coach off this.
> - **[EMERGING]** — Real data, limited/conflicting/single-source, or effect size unsettled. Hold loosely.
> - **[FOLKLORE]** — Widely repeated, unsupported or contradicted. Stop saying it.
> - **⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH** — Underlying population is not ours. Directional only.
> - **[UNVERIFIED]** — Could not be confirmed against the primary source.

**Every citation in this document was checked against PubMed, PMC, or the publishing journal.** Items that could not be verified are named in §8 rather than quietly dropped, because the fabrications circulating in this space are specific and worth knowing by name.

> ### The claim, restated precisely
>
> > **Controlled trials at 85+ are rare, non-randomized, and largely grey literature. And there is still no published controlled trial in PROFESSIONAL pitchers with velocity as an outcome** — the professional weighted-ball literature (Sussman et al., *IJSPT*) is observational injury work, not a training trial.

> That is a weaker statement than "not one," and it is the true one. The structural argument of this document survives it — the evidence base is thin, grey, and mostly null — but **the absolutism was wrong and the absolutism was doing rhetorical work it had not earned.**

**The published training literature at or above our floor is vanishingly thin, non-randomized, and mostly grey.**

The studies closest to our population are these — note that the two best-known ones found **no significant velocity gain**:

| Study | Level | Baseline | Control | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marsh et al. 2018 (Driveline, *PeerJ*) | Collegiate + professional, age 19.9 ± 1.3 | 35.1 ± 1.8 m/s = **78.5 mph** (submaximal lab test) | **No** | **No change in velocity.** 9 of 17 gained, 8 lost |
| **Ake 2016** (M.S. thesis, Univ. of Mississippi + *IJES* Conf. Proc.) | **NCAA D1 SEC varsity**, n = 56 | ⚠️ **CORRECTED 2026-08-13: 87.25 ± 2.32 (WI) / 86.80 ± 1.32 (control) mph** — *not* "not reported." **The highest-velocity controlled sample in the literature** | Yes (non-randomized) | **No significant gain**, p = .071; **no group difference**, p = .271 |
| **Lee, Choi & Jeon 2026** (*Asian J Kinesiology* 28(1):19) | **Korean collegiate**, n = 29 | **MAX 86.2 / 85.9 mph** ✅ · average 84.0 / 83.5 ⚑ | Yes (active comparator, **not randomized**) | **POSITIVE.** Core-coiling: max **p = .003**, average **p = .011**; comparator unchanged; ANCOVA favored CCT (p = .014 / .042) |

Every large positive effect in the weighted-implement literature comes from samples averaging **67–75 mph**. The effect-size gradient runs in exactly one direction:

**~67 mph HS (+2.2 mph, randomized control) → 10–17 yr underload (+4.8 mph, uncontrolled) → NCAA D1 (null) → collegiate/pro (null).**

**[ESTABLISHED]** The published velocity-training effect shrinks toward zero as baseline velocity rises. This is confirmed independently outside baseball: Fredriksen & van den Tillaar's 2024 systematic review of overarm throwing found underweighted-implement gains of **11–12% in novices versus 2–3% in experienced athletes** ([*Sports Med Open* 10:122](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11550301/)).

**What this means for the coach, stated plainly:**

> Every mph figure you have ever been quoted for a velocity program was measured on someone slower than your athlete. When a facility tells an 88 mph arm to expect "3–5 mph," that number is an extrapolation from 15-year-olds throwing 67, not a measurement of anyone like him. **The honest prior for a mature 85+ arm is 2–3% — roughly 1.5 to 2.5 mph per well-executed year — and a meaningful probability of zero.**

Two caveats that cut the other way, and I want them on the record:
1. Both null studies are underpowered (n = 17 uncontrolled; n = 56 retrospective thesis). Neither can detect a true 1–2 mph effect.
2. The Driveline null used marker-encumbered mocap with subjects told to throw "as hard as comfortable." **A submaximal post-test is a poor instrument for detecting a 1–2 mph change.**

So the honest statement is not "nothing works at 85+." It is: **nobody has run the study, and the studies adjacent to it are null.**

## 2. Physical Qualities That Produce Velocity

### 2.1 The headline: absolute output beats relative output, and jump height is close to worthless

This is the most useful and most counterintuitive finding in this section, and it replicates across two independent samples at two levels.

**King BW, Snow TK, Millard-Stafford M (2025), *J Strength Cond Res* — n = 33 NCAA Division I pitchers** ([PMID 39446825](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39446825/)):

| Variable | r with fastball velocity | p |
|---|---|---|
| **Body mass** | **0.58** | .0004 |
| **Lean mass** | **0.52** | .002 |
| Wingate peak power (absolute) | 0.44 | .011 |
| CMJ peak power (absolute) | 0.43 | .014 |
| CMJ power **relative to body mass** | 0.19 | **NS** |
| **Jump height** | **0.07** | **NS** |
| Sparta Score | −0.06 | NS |

Authors' conclusion: *"absolute power output is a more relevant predictor of baseball pitching velocity than lower-body power variables influenced by body mass."*

**Wong R, Laudner K, Amonette W, Vazquez J, Evans D, Meister K (2023), *J Strength Cond Res* — n = 53 asymptomatic PROFESSIONAL pitchers, age 24.5 ± 3.6** ([PMID 36026465](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36026465/)):

| Jump test (force plate, variable set) | R² with ball velocity | p |
|---|---|---|
| Countermovement jump | 0.10 | **.28 — NS** |
| Squat jump | 0.07 | **.44 — NS** |
| **Drop jump** | **0.30** | **.005** |
| — Reactive strength index specifically | — | **.002** |

*(Spin rate ran the other way: CMJ R² = 0.20, p = .03; SJ R² = 0.19, p = .04; DJ p = .09 NS.)*

A third study appears at first to contradict both, and resolving it is what makes this section useful.

**Sakurai M, Qiao M, Szymanski DJ, Crotin RL (2024), *J Strength Cond Res* 38(7):1288–1294 — n = 19 NCAA D1 pitchers**, in-lab pitching + bilateral CMJ with GRF ([PMID 38900174](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38900174/)):

| Variable | r with fastball velocity |
|---|---|
| **CMJ concentric impulse** | **0.71** |
| **CMJ peak power** | **0.68** |
| CMJ concentric impulse vs A-P linear momentum during pitching | 0.68 |
| Lean/body mass vs pitching linear momenta | **0.71–0.83** |
| Pelvis/trunk pitching mechanics vs CMJ | **No meaningful relationship** |

These are the **highest CMJ–velocity correlations in the literature**, in a paper that sits alongside King's null for relative power and jump height.

**The resolution — and this is the single most useful methodological point in this document:**

> **Impulse and absolute power predict velocity. Height and mass-normalized power do not.** Momentum is mass × velocity. When you divide power by body mass, you delete the term that carries most of the signal. When you measure jump *height*, you are measuring a mass-normalized quantity in disguise — height is determined by takeoff velocity, not by momentum.

Every apparent conflict in the jump-testing literature dissolves once you sort the metrics this way:

| Metric family | Predicts pitch velocity? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute impulse / absolute peak power** | **Yes** | Sakurai 2024 r = 0.68–0.71 (D1); King 2025 r = 0.43–0.44 (D1); Donahue 2018 r = 0.43–0.44 (MiLB) |
| **Body mass / lean mass** | **Yes, strongest** | King r = 0.58 / 0.52; Sakurai r = 0.71–0.83 vs momentum |
| **Reactive strength (drop jump RSI)** | **Yes, in pros** | Wong 2023 R² = 0.30, p = .005 |
| **Stride-leg unilateral / frontal-plane jump** | **Yes** | Lis 2023 r ≥ 0.65; Lehman 2013 |
| **Jump height (bilateral)** | **No** | King r = 0.07 NS |
| **Power ÷ body mass** | **No** | King r = 0.19 NS |
| **Bilateral CMJ set, in professionals** | **No** | Wong R² = 0.10, p = .28 |

**Read together, four conclusions:**

1. **[ESTABLISHED] Body mass and lean mass are the strongest single physical correlates of velocity in college pitchers.** r = 0.58 and 0.52 — roughly 34% and 27% of variance. Nothing in the physical-testing battery beats them. Sakurai's momentum data explains the mechanism: mass is what gets moved down the mound.
2. **[ESTABLISHED] Jump height does not predict velocity** (r = 0.07 in D1). Neither does power expressed *per kilogram* (r = 0.19 NS). The pitcher does not have to move his body over a bar. **The mass he carries is part of the machine, not a penalty.**
3. **[ESTABLISHED] Absolute impulse and absolute power do predict velocity**, replicated across three independent samples at two levels (D1 ×2, MiLB ×1), with r ranging 0.43–0.71.
4. **[EMERGING] Reactive strength (drop jump / RSI) is the one jump quality that survives into a professional sample.** R² = 0.30 in 53 pros, where CMJ and squat jump both failed. This is a stiffness/short-contact quality, not a peak-force quality — mechanically sensible, because the lead-leg block is a ~50–80 ms braking event, not a countermovement jump.

**Two further findings on jump testing worth carrying:**

- **Unilateral asymmetry in pitchers is normal and directional, and should not be coached toward zero.** Lis R, Szymanski DJ, Qiao M, Crotin RL (2023), *JSCR* 37(9):1852–1859, n = 19 D1 ([PMID 37389938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37389938/)): **stride-leg unilateral CMJ height vs fastball velocity r ≥ 0.65, p < .01** — the strongest single association in the paper — and pitchers jumped **significantly higher on the stride leg than the drive leg** (p < .01, η² = 0.34). Bilateral CMJ exceeded summed single-leg by 27%.
- **Jump testing may predict velocity *consistency* rather than velocity magnitude.** Burke AA et al. (2026), *JSCR* ahead of print, n = 8 collegiate pitchers monitored weekly across a 15-week/53-game season ([PMID 42423608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42423608/)): **no CMJ metric predicted mean velocity**, but jump height predicted *lower* velocity variance (γ = −0.060, p = .001) and RSI-modified predicted *higher* variance (γ = 3.800, p < .001). ⚑ n = 8 — hypothesis-generating only. But it reframes the nulls above: jump tests may be answering a stability question, not a ceiling question.

**Frontal-plane unilateral power — the most consistently overlooked quality.** Lehman G, Drinkwater EJ, Behm DG (2013), *JSCR* 27(4):902–908, n = 42 college players ([PMID 22706576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22706576/)): across a battery of med-ball throws, vertical jumps, and broad jumps, the **lateral-to-medial jump was the only test that correlated with throwing velocity across all four throwing conditions and in both RH and LH throwers.** ⚑ Outcome was shuffle/stretch throw velocity, not mound pitch velocity, and the reported multiple-R values for the left-handed subgroup (up to R = 0.982) are certainly overfit — do not quote those. The *lateral bound* finding is corroborated independently in the only intervention sample matching our population (Howell/Cressey 2021, §3.4).

**Corroboration from industry data.** Driveline's force-plate velocity-prediction model weights, in order: squat jump peak power (watts, **absolute**), CMJ **RSI-modified**, 10-to-5 hop **RSI**, and IMTP **net peak force** (absolute). Model **R² = 0.54, mean absolute error ±2.7 mph** ([Driveline, 2021](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2021/05/predicted-pitch-velocity/)). **[EMERGING — not peer-reviewed, sample size and level unstated.]** Note the convergence: absolute power and reactive strength, exactly the two families that survived in the peer-reviewed samples. Driveline explicitly flags that the model has no limb-length and no upper-body input.

**⚑ Practical translation for an 85+ arm.** The training targets that the elite-population data actually supports are: **absolute lower-body power, reactive strength/stiffness, and lean mass.** They do not support chasing vertical jump height or relative power numbers. An athlete who adds 12 lb of lean mass and improves his drop-jump RSI has moved the two qualities with the best evidence; an athlete who added two inches to his vertical has moved a variable with r = 0.07.

### 2.2 Rotational power / medicine ball throws

**Weaker than the field believes, and the most-cited support is the wrong sport.**

- **Taniyama D et al. (2021), *J Strength Cond Res*** ([PMID 34570055](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34570055/)): rotational medicine ball throw velocity vs pitching velocity, **r = 0.62, p = .02, R² = 0.38**. But: **n = 15 pitchers, NCAA Division III.** ⚑ **SAMPLE MISMATCH** — D3 pitchers are typically low-80s; this is below our floor, and n = 15 gives an r = 0.62 a 95% CI of roughly 0.16–0.86. Directional only.
- **The Szymanski medicine-ball literature is not about pitchers.** Szymanski DJ et al. (2007, *JSCR*, [PMID 17685676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17685676/); [PMID 18076221](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18076221/)) and Szymanski 2010 ([PMID 20881505](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20881505/)) studied **high school** players with **bat swing velocity** and **torso rotational strength** as outcomes. **Not pitch velocity.** Anyone citing "Szymanski showed medicine ball training increases velocity" for a pitcher is citing a hitting study on 16-year-olds.
- The one medicine-ball entry in the weighted-implement review — Szymanski et al. 2011, 7 oz medicine ball, 8 weeks, HS, controlled — was **not significant**, and exists only as a *JSCR* supplement conference abstract (25:S64), not a full paper.

**[EMERGING, leaning weak]** Rotational med-ball throw velocity is a reasonable *monitoring* metric — it is cheap, repeatable, and tracks a quality that plausibly matters.

> ### ⚠️ SOFTENED 2026-08-13 — the "no such study exists" verdict no longer holds.
>
> This section previously concluded: **[FOLKLORE]** *that a medicine-ball training block is an evidenced route to pitch velocity in a mature arm. No such study exists in our population.*
>
> **One now does.** **Lee, Choi & Jeon (2026), *Asian J Kinesiology* 28(1):19** — 12 weeks, 4×/week, of what the authors call "core-coiling training" but which is **in substance a rotational medicine-ball and tubing program** (Medicine Ball Side Throw, Medicine Ball Side Catch, Jump and Wall Ball Side Throw, Side Jumping and Wall Ball Throw, Tubing Torso Rotation, three torso-rotation variants, Walking Lunge and Twist). **n = 29 collegiate, baseline max velocity 86.2 / 85.9 mph — clearing this document's floor. Max velocity p = .003, average velocity p = .011, between-group ANCOVA p = .014 / .042.** Full treatment and caveats in §3.4.
>
> **Revised verdict: [EMERGING].** One non-randomized 12-week trial with an active comparator that trained neither rotational power nor explosive lower body. **That is not "medicine ball training raises velocity." It is "one controlled trial at 84–86 mph found rotational power work beat machine-based general strength."** Weak, real, and the first of its kind. The Szymanski critique above stands unchanged — that literature is still HS hitters with bat-speed outcomes.

### 2.3 Anthropometrics — the largest single effect in the entire literature

**Fernandez G, Bullock G, Zappa R, Oliver G, Nicholson K (2026), *Sports Biomechanics* — n = 322 NCAA D1 pitchers (122 ACC, 200 SEC), in-game markerless motion capture, 2022–2024** ([PMID 42253247](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42253247/)).

This is the largest sample in the velocity literature, captured **in-game** with full biomechanics, analyzed with a Super Learner ensemble. The result should reorganize how a coach thinks about ceilings:

| | |
|---|---|
| Model performance | **R² = 0.29, RMSE = 2.70 mph** |
| **Height — share of variable importance** | **81.2%** |
| Body mass — share of variable importance | 2.47% |
| Cross-conference generalization | SEC→ACC RMSE 3.04 mph; ACC→SEC 2.55 mph |

**Two findings, both uncomfortable:**

1. **With 322 D1 pitchers and complete in-game biomechanics available, a single non-modifiable anthropometric variable — height — carried 81% of the model's variable importance.** Everything mechanical, everything trainable, split the remainder.
2. **Total explained variance was still only 29%,** with a 2.70 mph error bar. In an already-selected population, most of what separates an 88 from a 93 is not in the measurement.

The authors conclude that velocity-generation strategies differ between cohorts and argue **against universal mechanics models.** That is a significant statement coming from a 322-pitcher in-game dataset.

**[ESTABLISHED] In a selected elite population, the biggest correlates of velocity are the least trainable ones.** Height dominates; body mass and lean mass follow (King r = 0.58 / 0.52; Sakurai r = 0.71–0.83 against pitching momentum). Every trainable strength and power test comes in behind them.

**Corroborating anthropometric data:** leg length carried **β = 0.292 (p < .001)** in the professional kinematic model, with **+12.7 cm ≈ +3.9 mph** ([PMC11322935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11322935/), n = 337 pro).

**Sprint/acceleration qualities.** Huang JH, Chen SH, Chiu CH (2022), *PLoS One* 17(3):e0265525 — **n = 64** adult male pitchers at a professional tryout (Taiwan, 2019; age 23.9 ± 2.8, 180.3 ± 5.9 cm, 81.4 ± 10.9 kg); pitchers below 130 km/h excluded ([PMC8929570](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8929570/)):

Stepwise regression retained three predictors:

| Predictor | t | p |
|---|---|---|
| **Height** | 2.79 | .007 |
| CMJ ratio (loaded / unloaded) | 2.35 | .022 |
| **Sprint ratio (10 m / 30 m time)** | 2.98 | .004 |

**Adjusted R² = 0.230**, model p = .0003.

Regression: `Velocity = 53.22 + 0.21(height) + 14.3(CMJ ratio) + 75.03(sprint ratio)`. ⚑ **Velocity units are km/h; back-solving at sample-mean inputs gives ~128 km/h ≈ 79.5 mph. SAMPLE MISMATCH — directionally suggestive only.**

Two things worth extracting. First, **height survives as an independent predictor even inside a selected tryout population** — the third independent confirmation, alongside Fernandez's 81.2% variable importance and the professional model's leg-length β = 0.292. Second, the **sprint ratio** — an acceleration-quality index — was the strongest term in the model. **[EMERGING]** Sprint/acceleration ability is an under-examined correlate. **There is no intervention study showing sprint training raises pitch velocity in pitchers.**

**One more replicated correlate: absolute cycle-ergometer power.** Donahue PT, Beiser E, Wilson SJ, Hill CM, Garner JC (2018), *J Trainology* 7(2):24–27 — **n = 27 minor-league pitchers**, velocity from spring-training games ([J-Stage](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/trainology/7/2/7_24/_article)): **30-s cycle mean power vs peak throwing velocity r = 0.441; vs mean velocity r = 0.428.** CMJ measures were *not* the significant predictors. Set beside King's Wingate absolute peak power r = 0.44 in D1, that is **two independent pro/college samples converging on r ≈ 0.44 for absolute cycle-ergometer power** — one of the better-replicated findings in this space, and a reminder that the quality is *absolute power output*, not any particular jump.

**The honest framing of the whole physical-testing battery:** field tests explained **23%** of velocity variance (Huang); force-plate metrics **54%** with ±2.7 mph error (Driveline, undisclosed sample); full in-game biomechanics plus anthropometry **29%** with ±2.70 mph error in 322 D1 pitchers (Fernandez). **Physical qualities are necessary and clearly not sufficient — and in the largest and best-sampled model, most of the explanatory power belonged to height.**

**A scoping review agrees, and the list is short.** Mercier MA, Tremblay M, Daneau C, Descarreaux M (2020), *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med* 6(1):e000704 ([PMID 32153984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32153984/)) reviewed 34 cross-sectional studies. The complete list of physical qualities it identified as associated with pitching performance: **body weight, age, and jumping-test performance.** That is the whole list.

### 2.4 Grip strength — the best population match in the literature, and it is a null

**Barrack AJ et al. (2024), *OJSM* 12(11) — n = 87 NCAA D1 pitchers, mean age 19.6, mean ball velocity 37.87 m/s = 84.83 mph** ([PMC11590131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11590131/)).

**This is the closest sample-to-target match of any cross-sectional study reviewed here** — 87 D1 arms averaging essentially exactly our 85 mph floor. Finding:

> **No significant univariate association between any grip strength variable and ball velocity.**

**[ESTABLISHED, for our population]** Grip strength is not a velocity lever. It matters mechanistically for medial-elbow stress shielding (`anatomy-physiology.md` §2.10) — a load-sharing argument, not a velocity argument. **Stop selling grip work as velocity work.**

**⚠ A widely circulated grip-strength correlation appears to be fabricated.** Figures of **total grip r = 0.705 / dominant r = 0.667 / non-dominant r = 0.655** against fastball velocity, in a sample with a conveniently on-target mean FB of 38.8 m/s (86.8 mph), circulate freely. They trace to an Academia.edu PDF with **no journal record, no DOI, and no PubMed entry.** **[UNVERIFIED — do not cite.]** They are directly contradicted by Barrack's n = 87. The excellent population match is exactly why the numbers spread; it is not evidence they exist. (A separate real study — Tremblay M et al. 2022, *Front Sports Act Living* 4:822454, [PMID 35425896](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35425896/) — reports grip τ = 0.653, but the sample spans **ages 10–22**, so the correlation is substantially age-driven. ⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH.)

### 2.5 Shoulder strength, trunk mobility, and rate of force development — three published nulls

Worth stating explicitly, because all three are heavily marketed.

- **Shoulder rotational strength explains ~12% of throwing velocity, and RFD explains nothing.** Job TDW, Cross MR, Cronin JB (2024), *J Biomech* 176:112339, n = 26 HS + collegiate ([PMID 39326246](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39326246/)): shoulder IR and ER **maximum force R² = 0.12–0.13**; **rate of force development contributed nothing meaningful.** Note the direction — max force beat RFD, the opposite of the usual assumption.
- **Trunk rotation mobility and upper-quarter dynamic stability do not predict velocity.** Bullock GS et al. (2018), *JSCR* 32(1):261–266, **n = 30 NCAA D1 pitchers** ([PMID 28225709](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28225709/)): throwing-side trunk rotation vs fastball velocity **r = 0.131, p = .491**; Upper Quarter Y-Balance, no correlation. Authors' conclusion: *"increased upper extremity stability and trunk mobility are not directly related to fastball velocity."* **[ESTABLISHED]** A clean published null against the movement-screen-drives-velocity claim.
- **Driveline's model reaches R² = 0.54 with explicitly "no information on an athlete's upper body strength or power."** Either upper-body strength adds little, or this is a genuine measurement gap. Both readings remain live — but the two peer-reviewed nulls above tilt toward the first.

**A real gap worth naming: there is no verified IMTP-versus-pitch-velocity correlation study in college or professional pitchers.** IMTP appears in this literature only inside Driveline's undisclosed model. Anyone quoting an IMTP-to-velocity r is quoting something unpublished.

### 3.2 The Reinold–Driveline disagreement, honestly

Both camps are quoted constantly and usually inaccurately. Here is what each actually found and why they diverge.

**Reinold's position** (Reinold MM, Macrina LC, *IJSPT* 2021;16(2):594–596, [article](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/21321-the-safety-and-efficacy-of-weighted-baseballs)) — verbatim:

> *"Weighted balls aren't evil and aren't the cause of injuries. They just change the stress. They also aren't magical and don't work to enhance velocity with everyone."*

His responder distribution is the most useful number he publishes: **80% of the weighted-ball group gained velocity, 8% unchanged, 12% decreased — and 67% of the CONTROL group also gained.** His proposed mechanism is dose-dependent external-rotation PROM gain (he reports ~8° at 16–32 oz), i.e. acquired layback → velocity → stress.

**Driveline's position** (Marsh et al. 2018): in an older, higher-level sample they found **no velocity change, no ER ROM change (122 ± 21° → 123 ± 10°, p = .637), and no elbow valgus torque change (p = .942)** — i.e., neither the benefit nor Reinold's proposed mechanism.

**Why they disagree — the real list, not the tribal one:**

1. **Population and ceiling.** 13–18 year olds at ~67 mph versus 18–23 year olds at ~78.5 mph. **This is the most parsimonious explanation, and it cuts directly against our population** — the higher-level sample is the one that failed.
2. **Control-group arithmetic.** Reinold's control *also trained* and gained +0.67 mph. His headline +2.24 mph is really **+1.6 mph net**. Driveline had no control at all, so their null is a null against nothing.
3. **They are not testing the same intervention.** Reinold used **2–32 oz**, including implements 6.4× regulation. Driveline's published acute work spans **3–7 oz**. The ER/GTO mechanism and the injuries plausibly belong to the 16–32 oz drills specifically.
4. **Confounding.** Driveline's 6 weeks bundled weighted implements + manual therapy + weightlifting + other modalities. Attribution is impossible in either direction. Reinold's design was cleaner but lacked the strength/intent stack Driveline argues is the actual driver.
5. **Measurement.** Reinold: Stalker radar, mound, full intent. Driveline: marker-based mocap, "throw as hard as comfortable." **A submaximal encumbered post-test cannot resolve a 1–2 mph change.**
6. **Power.** n = 17 uncontrolled, 9 gained / 8 lost — consistent with a null *and* with a small true effect.
7. **Conflict of interest, both directions.** Driveline authors are employees or part-owners of the company selling the implements (disclosed in-paper). Reinold's public brand is substantially built on weighted-ball caution, with ASMI/Andrews co-authorship carrying an institutional prior.
8. **Outcome framing.** Reinold reports a between-group mean; Driveline reports a within-group mean plus a responder split. Both conceal the same fact: **the response is wildly heterogeneous and neither study is powered to characterize responders.**

**Where I land.** The disagreement is smaller than it looks. Reinold found ~1.6 mph net in 15-year-olds. Driveline found nothing in collegians and pros. **Both are consistent with "the effect is real and small in developing arms, and unmeasured-to-absent in mature ones."** Neither supports a confident mph promise to an 88 mph athlete.

**Stress cost (one line, per mission scope):** the only RCT reported a **24% arm-injury rate vs 0% control** — 2 olecranon stress fractures, 1 partial UCL, 1 UCL reconstruction — concentrated in the *following* season and associated with the 16–32 oz work; separately, acute work in 26 collegiate/pro pitchers found **no arm-kinetic penalty across 3–7 oz** (O'Connell et al. 2022, *J Appl Biomech*, [PMID 35981710](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35981710/)). See `open-disputes.md` §8; not re-litigated here.

### 3.3 The underload case — the most defensible implement claim

Four independent lines converge on **underload (3–4.4 oz), not overload**, as the better-evidenced lever:

1. Yang 2013: underload-only, controlled, **+2.1 mph significant**; control not significant.
2. DeRenne 1990: **underload +4.72 mph > overload +3.75 mph**.
3. Erickson 2020: underload-only, **+4.8 mph**, zero injuries (uncontrolled, ⚑ ages 10–17).
4. Fredriksen & van den Tillaar 2024 across overarm sports: *"The use of lighter balls always enhanced throwing performance with the normal balls, while the use of overweight balls gave conflicting results."*
5. Mechanistically: **Fleisig et al. 2017** (*Sports Health*, n = 25 HS/collegiate, [PMID 27872403](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27872403/)) — with a 4 oz ball, **arm and trunk velocities match standard pitching**, while *"arm and trunk velocities steadily decreased as ball weight increased from 5 to 32 oz."* And **O'Connell 2022** — in 26 collegiate/professional pitchers, ball weight across 3–7 oz significantly affected pitch velocity and arm speed while **none of the measured arm joint kinetics changed.**

**[EMERGING, best-supported implement claim]** If the goal is arm speed in a mature arm, the 3–7 oz window is where the mechanism (arm speed manipulation without kinetic penalty) and the outcome data both point. **Heavy implements decelerate the arm** — that is a measured fact, not an opinion — so any velocity mechanism they carry must be indirect and delayed.

**Why it matters:**
1. **It clears the floor on max velocity** (86.2 / 85.9 mph) — the only positive controlled result that does.
2. **It converges with the mechanism.** Trunk power alone predicts ball velocity at R² = 0.731 (Aguinaldo & Escamilla 2019); hip rotators do ~90% of the work of rotating the torso and supply ~30% of the power that ends up rotating the shoulder (Roach & Lieberman 2014); trunk muscular torques are the primary energy *sources* (Naito 2021).
3. **It converges with the only other positive controlled non-implement study** — Oyama 2026's 8-week lumbopelvic-hip block (⚑ HS). **Two independent controlled trials, two countries, two populations, same target: the trunk/hip.** That is the strongest convergence in this entire section.
4. **It partially rehabilitates rotational medicine-ball work**, which §2.2 correctly labels FOLKLORE for pitch velocity on the grounds that the Szymanski literature is HS hitters with bat-speed outcomes. **Core-coiling is, in substance, a rotational medicine-ball program — and here it has a controlled positive velocity outcome in a collegiate sample.** §2.2 should be softened accordingly: *the med-ball training literature was previously null-or-off-population; it now has one positive, non-randomized, active-comparator trial at 84–86 mph.*

**Why to hold it loosely — and these are not small:**
1. **Not randomized.** No allocation concealment, no blinding, none described.
2. **⚠️ The comparator is close to a straw man, and this is the biggest problem.** The RWT group did a **machine-based general-strength circuit with no rotational work, no plyometric work, and no explosive lower-body work** — leg press and leg extension, not jumps or bounds. So the finding is **not** "rotational training beats strength training." It is **"12 weeks of explosive rotational and med-ball work beats 12 weeks of bodybuilding machines."** That is a much narrower claim, and given that this document's own §2.1 establishes that *absolute power and reactive strength* are the qualities that predict velocity, the comparator was training neither.
3. **It clears the floor only on peak velocity.** Average velocity, 84.0 / 83.5 mph, sits below it.
4. n = 29, one Korean university, one squad. No kinematics, no kinetics, no stress outcome.
5. **Command did not improve in either group** — worth saying, since rotational-power marketing frequently implies it will.
6. Published in a low-profile regional journal (KCI-indexed; not PubMed-indexed at time of review). The protocol description is unusually good; the design reporting is not.

**Verdict: [EMERGING — the most interesting positive result in this literature, and the one most likely to be over-quoted within a year.]** It should be cited as *"one non-randomized 12-week trial, active comparator, collegiate, positive on peak velocity"* — never as *"core training adds velocity."* See `idea-scouting.md` Sweep 2 #4 for the scouting verdict.

**Three things jump out.**

**First — Oyama 2026 is the single most interesting intervention result in this literature for our purposes**, and almost nobody is citing it. An 8-week *lower-body/trunk strength* block, with a control group, raised ball speed. It is the only controlled non-implement study with a positive velocity outcome. ⚑ It is a high-school sample, so directional only. **And it raised elbow varus moment** — the authors explicitly recommend reducing pitch counts as ball speed rises. Velocity is not free, exactly as the within-athlete torque data predicts (Slowik 2019, R² = 0.957 within-subject; Manzi et al. 2021, *JSES*, n = 91 pro analyzed: between-pitcher R² = 0.228 for shoulder distraction vs **within-pitcher R² > 0.85** across 10 kinetic measures, [PMID 33930560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33930560/)).

**Second — Escamilla 2012 shows that in a controlled 4-arm design, three completely different 6-week programs all worked and none beat the others.** Throwers Ten (band/dumbbell arm care), Keiser pneumatic resistance, and plyometrics each produced 1.2–2.0% gains; the control did not move. ⚑ High school. **The read: at a developing level, the specific modality appears to matter less than doing something structured. Whether that generalizes to a mature arm is exactly what is untested.**

**Third — Lambert 2023 documents that a D1 control group LOST rotator-cuff strength across an 8-week offseason.** Maintenance is not the default state. That is a program-design fact, not an injury fact — and it sets up §5.5. ⚠️ **Note (2026-08-13): §5.5's Gdovin study is NOT a removal experiment — it is an uncontrolled pre-post with no control group. Lambert 2023, with its actual control arm, is now the better-designed of the two, though its outcome is cuff strength rather than velocity.**

**[FOLKLORE]** That there is a peer-reviewed base for plyo-ball-only protocols, constraint drills (pivot pickoff, roll-in, rocker, walking windup), long-toss programs, sprint training, olympic lifts, trap bar work, or velocity-based training producing measured mph in pitchers. **None of these has a published intervention study with a pitch-velocity outcome in any baseball population, let alone ours.** They may work. There is no evidence either way. Say so out loud.

**⚠ And one meta-analytic number that will be quoted at you.** Myers NL, Sciascia AD, Westgate PM, Kibler WB, Uhl TL (2015), *JSCR* 29(10):2964–2979 ([PMID 25763521](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25763521/)) pooled 13 RCTs across baseball, tennis, and softball and reported **periodized training g = 3.445 (95% CI 1.976–4.914)**. A Hedges' g of 3.4 is not a plausible training effect — it is a small-study-bias artifact, and the authors themselves flag that **6 of the 13 studies had confidence intervals crossing zero.** Do not carry that number forward.

A more temperate review: Zhang H, Jiang Q, Li A (2023), *Heliyon* 9(12):e22797 ([PMID 38125451](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38125451/)) — 16 studies, all ages; of the 12 with comparative statistics, **8 favored the experimental group on throwing velocity.** No pooled estimate, mixed ages, minimal injury data.

**On long toss specifically:** Fleisig GS et al. (2011), *JOSPT* 41(5):296–303 ([PMID 21212502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21212502/)), n = 17 college pitchers, is a **single-session biomechanics comparison, not a training study**. It found max-distance throws produce the greatest shoulder ER (180 ± 11°), shoulder IR torque (101 ± 17 N·m), elbow varus torque (100 ± 18 N·m), and elbow extension velocity (2,573 ± 203 °/s). It measured **no velocity outcome whatsoever.** The widely circulated claim that "a study proved max-distance long toss decreases pitching velocity" is a blog misreading of this paper. **[UNVERIFIED → false as stated.]**

## 4. Where Velocity Comes From Mechanically

### 4.1 The power accounting

The cleanest mechanistic decomposition available is **Roach NT, Lieberman DE (2014), *J Exp Biol* 217(12):2139–2149** ([article](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/12/2139/12091/Upper-body-contributions-to-power-generation)) — 20 baseball players, 3D mocap at 1000 Hz, with therapeutic braces used to selectively restrict segments.

⚑ **SAMPLE MISMATCH: mean ball speed 27.7 ± 3.8 m/s (≈62 mph), throwing at a 1 m target from 10 m, not off a mound.** The magnitudes do not transfer to our band. The *architecture* does.

| Finding | Value |
|---|---|
| Peak power, shoulder internal rotation, acceleration phase | **11,838 ± 4,170 W** |
| Negative work at the shoulder during cocking (elastic storage) | **−201 ± 70 J** |
| Hip rotators' share of the power for rapid shoulder IR | **~30%** |
| Hip rotators' share of torso rotation work | **~90%** |
| Elbow and wrist direct power generation | **Minimal — passively driven** |

**Selective restriction, effect on ball speed:**

| Restricted | Δ ball speed | p |
|---|---|---|
| **Shoulder (external rotation limited)** | **−8 ± 6%** | <.001 |
| **Torso rotation** | **−5 ± 6%** | <.001 |
| Clavicle | −3 ± 6% | .031 |
| Wrist | Inconclusive (sham effects) | — |

**Three conclusions I would defend:**

1. **[ESTABLISHED] The shoulder is a spring, not a motor, and the muscles cannot produce that power directly.** ~11.8 kW of peak internal-rotation power in a ~30–50 ms window is far beyond the contractile capacity of the internal rotators. It comes from **elastic recoil of energy stored during cocking (−201 J)** — the anterior capsule, the ligaments, and the tendons of the internal rotators loaded in the layback position. This is why "arm strength" training has never produced velocity in a controlled trial: **you are not trying to build a bigger motor, you are trying to build a better spring and a better loader of that spring.**
2. **[ESTABLISHED] The hips do ~90% of the work of rotating the torso and supply ~30% of the power that ends up rotating the shoulder.** This is the mechanistic justification for lower-body training as the primary velocity lever — and it is consistent with Oyama 2026 being the only positive non-implement intervention.
3. **[ESTABLISHED] The elbow and wrist are passengers.** They generate almost no power directly. Distal training targets are misallocated effort for velocity.

### 4.2 Energy accounting: the trunk is the engine, and ~27% of the work never reaches the ball

Three studies quantify the chain in ways a coach can act on.

**Naito K (2021), *J Exp Biol*, n = 16 male collegiate pitchers** ([PMID 34495332](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34495332/)):
- **Trunk muscular torques are the primary energy SOURCES.** The shoulder and elbow function **predominantly as TRANSFER mechanisms, not generators.**
- Of 329.2 J of total muscular work from trunk + arm joints, **72.7% was directed to moving the throwing hand and ball.**
- **→ ~27% of the work generated does not reach the ball.** That is the cleanest quantified loss term in the literature.

**Aguinaldo A, Escamilla R (2019), *OJSM*, n = 31 (16 professional at 81.2 mph, 15 HS at 68.0 mph)** ([PMID 30828584](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30828584/)):
- **Trunk power ALONE predicted ball velocity, R² = 0.731.**
- Professionals generated significantly greater trunk power than high schoolers.
- **Absolute elbow valgus torque was higher in pros (71.3 vs 50.7 N·m), but NORMALIZED torque did not differ** — the extra load is body-size scaling, not riskier mechanics.

**de Swart AFMJ et al. (2022/2025), *Sports Biomech*, n = 22** ([PMID 36226680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36226680/)) ⚑ HS sample: **the two legs do different jobs.** Energy **generation** was higher in the **trailing leg**, arising primarily from the trailing hip. The **lead leg mainly TRANSFERS** energy upward in a distal-to-proximal order. **Rear leg = generator; front leg = conduit and brake.**

**Synthesis of the power accounting [ESTABLISHED for the architecture, EMERGING for the magnitudes]:**

> **Trail hip and trunk generate. Lead leg transmits and brakes. Shoulder and elbow transfer and recoil.** Roughly 73% of generated work reaches the ball. The arm is the last and least powerful link, and every intervention aimed at it is aimed at the smallest term in the equation.

### 4.3 Where velocity is lost inside the elite band

**First, the finding that frames everything else: kinematic explanatory power collapses as you move up.**

**Manzi JE, Dowling B, Wang Z, et al. (2024), *OJSM* 12(8)** ([PMID 39143985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39143985/) | [PMC11322935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11322935/)) modeled both levels in one paper:

| | High school | **Professional** |
|---|---|---|
| n | 59 (538 pitches) | **337 (3,627 pitches)** |
| Mean velocity | 31.3 ± 2.9 m/s = **70.0 mph** ⚑ | 38.3 ± 2.1 m/s = **85.7 mph** ✅ |
| **Model R²** | **0.925** | **0.536** |
| Leg length coefficient (B) | **26.406** | **13.706** |

**Two conclusions, both important:**

1. **In high schoolers, standard 3D kinematics explains 93% of velocity variance. In professionals at 85.7 mph, it explains 54%.** Nearly half of what separates an 87 from a 93 is **not visible in the kinematics at all.**
2. **Leg length is the only anthropometric retained at both levels, and its coefficient is roughly HALF as large in professionals.** Anthropometric leverage compresses through the selection funnel — but it does not disappear.

Professional retained predictors (B, p): **leg length 13.706 (<.001)**, stride length 0.060 (<.001), trunk flexion at BR 0.371 (<.001), max shoulder ER 0.688 (<.001), elbow extension velocity 0.001 (.006).

**Note also what drops out at the professional level: timing of peak trunk rotational velocity is retained in the HS model (β = 0.133) and NOT in the professional model.** **[EMERGING] Sequencing timing is a discriminator at lower levels that saturates in the elite band.** By 85+, essentially everyone sequences well enough; it stops separating people. This is consistent with the `biomechanics.md` §5.2 finding that no pitcher uses a single stable sequence.

**The older professional-model coefficient table, retained for reference:**

| Predictor | β | Trainable? |
|---|---|---|
| Trunk flexion at ball release | 1.829 | **Downstream marker, not a lever** (see caution below) |
| Peak shoulder IR velocity | 0.381 | Output, not input |
| **Stride length** | **0.334** | **Yes — and it is not a torque contributor** |
| Max shoulder ER | 0.333 | Partly structural (retrotorsion), partly acquired laxity |
| **Leg length** | **0.292** | **No — anthropometric.** +12.7 cm ≈ **+3.9 mph** |
| Trunk rotation at foot contact | 0.289 | Yes |
| Elbow extension velocity | 0.176 | Output |

**Caution, repeated because it is abused:** the implied **+8.3 mph per 10° of trunk flexion at release** is not causal at that magnitude. Forward tilt at release is largely a *consequence* of a lead-leg block that already worked. Telling an 87 mph arm to "get out over the front side" will not buy 8 mph.

**Arm path — a rare lever with a real mph number.** **Dowling B et al. (2023), *OJSM*, n = 182 professional pitchers** ([PMID 38045766](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38045766/)): for each **30 cm increase in early arm path**, ball velocity rose **0.354 m/s (+0.79 mph)** and elbow varus torque rose. Within-pitcher R² for arm path vs velocity ≈ 0.79; vs elbow varus torque ≈ 0.96.

**[EMERGING]** This is one of the few mechanical variables with (a) a professional sample, (b) a per-unit mph coefficient, and (c) a stated stress cost. It is also cross-sectional. **Nobody has lengthened a pitcher's arm path and measured what happened.**

**Hip–shoulder separation.** Kew ME et al. (2023), *OJSM* systematic review, 12 studies, 930 pitchers (**46.1% youth**, 17.6% HS, 16.5% collegiate, 19.8% professional) ([PMID 38035212](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38035212/)) associated separation with **+2.6 ± 0.5 mph**. ⚑ The pooled sample is nearly half youth. Read against `biomechanics.md` §3.3, where separation at foot contact explained only **17% of peak trunk rotation velocity** — two steps removed from ball speed. **[EMERGING, over-weighted in coaching.]**

A better-framed adult finding: **Wada et al. (2025), *Heliyon*, n = 18 collegiate at 134.2 ± 5.7 km/h = 83.4 mph** ([PMID 39975838](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39975838/)) — trunk separation angle at stride foot contact was the **only** kinematic variable correlated with ball speed (ρ = 0.50), and **pivot-leg rotational displacement correlated positively with pelvis rotation (ρ = 0.70) but NEGATIVELY with trunk separation (ρ = −0.49).** **[EMERGING] A quantified mechanical trade-off: more pivot-leg/pelvis rotation can eat your separation.** Small n; borderline population.

### 4.4 The two "free velocity" levers — mph with no elbow-torque cost

This is the most actionable material in the document, and both findings come from large professional samples.

**1. Stride length toward ≥80% of body height.** ⚠️ **DEMOTED 2026-08-13 — see correction box.**
**Manzi JE, Dowling B, et al. (2021), *J Sports Sci* 39(23):2658–2664 — n = 315 professional pitchers** ([PMID 34240663](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34240663/)) ⚠️ *(authorship corrected 2026-08-13 — first author is **Manzi**, not Dowling; the library previously cited this as "Dowling 2021" and "Dowling/Manzi group")*:
- Mean stride length **78.3 ± 5.3% of body height**
- **Per 10% BH increase: ball velocity +0.9 m/s = +2.0 mph** (B = 0.089, **β = 0.25**, p < .001)
- **NO significant difference in elbow varus torque across stride-length quartiles (p = 0.072)**
- ⚠️ **Cross-sectional / observational — which is now the entire point of `open-disputes.md` #13**
- Pitchers reaching **≥80% BH threw harder without added elbow torque**

> ### ⚠️ CORRECTED 2026-08-13 — STRIDE LENGTH HAS BEEN EXPERIMENTALLY MANIPULATED, TWICE, BY INDEPENDENT GROUPS. NEITHER FOUND A GAIN. DO NOT RE-IMPORT "NEVER TESTED."
>
> **This document previously ranked stride length as the #2 recommendation — "the best mechanical lever" — and stated in Open Question #8 that "nobody has lengthened a professional's stride and measured what happened." The second half is false.**
>
> **Experiment 1 — the Buffalo dataset.**
> **Ramsey DK, Crotin RL, White S (2014).** *Effect of stride length on overarm throwing delivery: A linear momentum response.* ***Hum Mov Sci* 38:185–196.** [PMID 25457417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25457417/)

> **Matsuda T, Hirano A, Umakoshi Y, Kimura A (2025).** ***Front Sports Act Living*.** [PMID 40264933](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40264933/)

> **The pitcher's own self-selected stride was significantly FASTER than both altered conditions. Lengthening the stride reduced velocity acutely.**
>
> **And the normalization the recommendation rests on is itself disputed twice.**
> - **Solomito MJ, Cohen AD, Garibay EJ (2023), *Sports Biomech* 22(11):1460–1469** ([PMID 32912079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32912079/)) — n = 99 collegiate: **no significant association between normalized stride length and ball velocity.**
> - **Yanagisawa O, Taniguchi H (2020), *J Phys Ther Sci* 32(9):578–583** ([PMID 32982054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32982054/)) — n = 18 collegiate: **absolute** stride length r = 0.55, p = .02, but **stride length as % body height r = 0.36, p = .15 — NOT significant.** Also null normalized to lower-extremity length and to hip-abduction width.
>
> ### The honest reading
>
> > **A long stride looks like a MARKER of a pitcher who throws hard, not a LEVER that makes one.**
>
> Pitchers who generate more momentum stride further *as a consequence*. When you take a pitcher and move his stride, nothing good happens — and when you move it 20% longer, something bad happens.
>
> **What survives, in fairness to the original claim:**
> - Both manipulations are **acute, instructed changes to a habituated stride** at ±20–25%, in samples averaging **~73–81 mph — below this document's floor**, at n = 19–20. Forcing an over-stride tests whether a man can absorb an insult, not whether an owned, integrated stride is worth anything.
> - **Manzi 2024 retained stride length in both the HS and professional models.** The cross-sectional association is real.
> - **No LONGITUDINAL stride-lengthening intervention exists.** The only near-miss: an unpublished 2026 University of Nevada, Reno master's thesis (Zack Buck, advisor Phil Pavilionis) — D1 pitchers, 4-week resistance-band ankle-mobility + balance block, pre/post stride length — **with no velocity outcome and no control group.**
>
> **Ranking action: stride length is demoted from "the best mechanical lever" to "the best cross-sectional correlate, with two independent acute manipulations against it and an unresolved normalization dispute." It should not be sold to an athlete as +2 mph.** See `open-disputes.md` #13.
>
> **One finding worth keeping from the Buffalo data, because it is genuinely useful and counterintuitive:** the **shorter** stride cut mean heart rate by **11.1 bpm** across an 80-pitch simulated game at no velocity cost (Crotin et al., *MSSE* 46(3):565–71, PMID 23917472). **For a starter, stride length may be a stamina lever rather than a velocity lever.**

**2. An open pelvis at foot contact.**
**Dowling B, Knapik DM, Luera MJ, et al. (2022), *OJSM* — n = 157 professional pitchers** ([PMID 36479467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36479467/)):
- Open-pelvis vs closed-pelvis: **39.1 ± 1.7 vs 38.4 ± 2.1 m/s = 87.5 vs 85.9 mph, p = .029 — a ~1.6 mph advantage**
- Plus longer stride, greater lead-knee flexion, faster peak knee-extension velocity
- **NO significant difference in elbow varus torque**

**[EMERGING, strongest available] These are the only two mechanical variables with (a) a professional sample, (b) a per-unit mph coefficient, and (c) a documented absence of elbow-torque cost.** Everything else with a clean effect size is paid for.

**⚠ One honest contradiction on stride length.** Solomito MJ, Cohen AD, Garibay EJ (2023), *Sports Biomech*, n = 99 collegiate ([PMID 32912079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32912079/)) found **no significant association between normalized stride length and ball velocity**, suggesting instead a beneficial band of 131–137% of *leg length*, with stride length acting **indirectly** through trunk and pelvic rotation at foot contact (p < .001). The studies used different normalizations (% body height vs % leg length) and differ 3× in sample size; Manzi 2024 retained stride length in both HS and professional models, which favors the Dowling result. **Do not treat "80% of height" as a hard threshold** — see §4.8 on why absolute thresholds do not transfer across capture systems.

### 4.6 Ground reaction force — the peak is the wrong measurement

Two studies together resolve a long-standing confusion.

- **Nicholson KF, Hulburt TC, Kimura BM, Aguinaldo AL (2022), *J Sports Med Phys Fitness*, n = 50 HS + 26 collegiate** ([PMID 34821495](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34821495/)): **ball speed showed only a weak association with stride-leg medial force**; max elbow valgus torque showed **no** correlation with GRF at all. Authors: *"other factors such as muscle strength, coordination, kinematics, or stride technique may overshadow the effects of GRF in highly skilled pitchers."*
- **Wasserberger KW, Giordano KA (2025), *Sports Biomech* 24(5), n = 105 high-velocity pitchers** (Driveline database, rear + lead force plates) ([PMID 37991012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37991012/)): using **statistical parametric mapping across the full GRF time series** rather than discrete peaks, pitch velocity significantly predicted **lead-leg braking GRF from ~27% to ~35% of the interval between front-foot contact and ball release.** Peak GRF magnitudes exceeded prior published values, consistent with a higher-velocity sample.

**[EMERGING, and a genuine methodological insight] Discrete GRF peaks are weak velocity predictors in skilled throwers. The signal lives in the *timing* of braking, not its magnitude.** The peak-based studies (including the bodyweight-controlled r = 0.23 in `biomechanics.md` §3.5) may have been measuring the wrong quantity. **When the braking force arrives appears to matter more than how big it is.**

**Center-of-mass velocity — "get down the mound faster" is mostly wrong.** Zappa RM et al. (2026), *J Biomech* 205:113411 — **n = 332 NCAA D1 pitchers, 8-camera markerless at 300 Hz, IN-GAME** (the largest in-band mechanics sample anywhere): only a **weak** positive trend between max forward COM velocity and pitch velocity, with high between-pitcher variability. Time-series analysis found significant associations **only at 0–1% and 4–7% of the motion** — i.e., **COM velocity generated BEFORE peak knee height ("body drift") is beneficial, while max COM velocity per se is not a robust predictor.** ⚠️ Verified via the LSUHSC institutional repository; no PubMed record confirmed at time of writing.

Lead knee extension · trunk transverse rotation at BR · sagittal trunk tilt at BR · early arm path length · trunk contralateral tilt at BR · max knee extension velocity. See §4.5 for exchange rates.

Max elbow extension velocity · max humeral IR velocity · max shoulder ER · trunk flexion at release. Naito shows the shoulder and elbow are transfer elements; Wasserberger shows transfer rather than generation drives speed. **Their high predictive weight in regression models is the single most common misreading in the applied literature.** They are readouts of upstream delivery, not training targets.

### 4.8 What the measurement error does to all of the above

Every number in §4 is bounded by the instrument that produced it, and the bounds are not small.

- **Markerless per-joint position error: Hawk-Eye 56.6 ± 9.4 mm; Theia3D 52.0 ± 12.3 mm vs marker-based** (Aguinaldo AL et al., 2026, *J Sports Sci*, n = 18 collegiate, [PMID 41294254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41294254/)). **Stride length showed the strongest cross-system agreement (CCC > 0.85); shoulder rotational variables showed the greatest variability.**
  **→ The variable with the cleanest in-band effect size (stride length) is also the one that measures most consistently. The variables with the most suspicious pooled effect sizes (MER, shoulder rotation) are the ones that measure worst. That is not a coincidence.**
- **Variability transfers across capture methods; absolute means do NOT** (Lerch BG et al., 2025, *J Biomech*, [PMID 40418881](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40418881/)). Within-system regression slopes are probably comparable across the literature. **Absolute thresholds — "80% of body height," "28° of trunk tilt" — are system-specific and must not be transported between studies.** This alone may explain the lead-knee and stride-length magnitude contradictions in §4.4–4.5.
- **Single-camera markerless is not adequate for these effect sizes.** pitchAI validation (Dobos TJ et al., 2025, *Sports Biomech*, [PMID 36409062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36409062/)): time-series joint-angle R² = 0.69–0.98, but **arm speed error 3.62 m/s and stride length error 5.75% of body height.** Recall that a 10% BH stride change is worth ~0.9 m/s of ball velocity. **The measurement error is larger than the effect being measured.**

## 5. Program Design

**Read this section knowing that essentially none of it rests on a periodization trial in pitchers.** There is no published comparison of block versus undulating versus concurrent programming with a pitch-velocity outcome in any baseball population. What follows is inference from the intervention data above plus general training science, and it is labeled as such.

### 5.1 What the real-world aggregate data says a block is worth

The only large pre/post datasets on our population are industry, uncontrolled, and self-selected. Used carefully they are still the best estimate of realistic magnitude available.

**Driveline, 2018 summer** ([article](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2018/11/2018-summer-pitching-review/)): 265 pitchers assessed, **120 with entry/exit data.**
- Mean gain, all athletes: **+0.74 mph**
- Mean gain, ≥3 weeks: **+0.95 mph**
- Distribution (≥3 wk): **60.6% gained ≥0.5 mph · 19.2% maintained · 20.2% LOST velocity**

**Driveline, 2019–2020** ([article](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2021/06/pitching-training-velocity-results-2019-2020/)):
- Single training block: **52% gained >1 mph · 27% within ±1 mph · 21% lost**
- First-to-latest mocap: **mean +1.8 mph**
- Athletes with **200+ days** between assessments (n = 35): **+3.29 mph**
- Their own caveat, verbatim in substance: *"It's generally tougher to gain velocity for hard throwers"* — a relative lack of low-hanging fruit.

**[EMERGING — no control group, self-selected, motivated population, heterogeneous goals.]**

**How to read these honestly:** a single training block at the best-resourced facility in the sport, in a mixed HS-to-pro population, produces **roughly +1 mph on average with a 20% chance of losing velocity.** Sustained engagement over 200+ days produces **~+3.3 mph**. Neither figure is isolated to 85+ arms, and both are inflated by the developing athletes in the sample.

**And there is one Driveline dataset restricted to our population — it is the single most relevant number in this document.**

**"The Value in Developing Velocity in the Minors," Driveline, 4 April 2019** ([article](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2019/04/value-developing-velocity-minors/)) — **n = 58 "high-level athletes," entry fastball ≥88 mph, age ≥19, ≥3 weeks of training. Average age 23, average 67 days between measurements.**

### 5.2 Time course — how long adaptations take

| Adaptation | Time course | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum throwing-intervention length to expect anything | **≥6 weeks** | Fredriksen & van den Tillaar 2024, explicit recommendation |
| Implement/throwing block velocity expression | 6–10 weeks in positive studies | Reinold (6), Yang (10), DeRenne (10) |
| Trunk/lower-body strength → ball speed | **8 weeks** | Oyama 2026 (⚑ HS) |
| Shoulder lean mass change | 8 weeks, +227 g with BFR | Lambert 2023 |
| Meaningful aggregate velocity change | **200+ days → +3.29 mph** | Driveline, n = 35 |

**[EMERGING]** The 6–10 week window is where throwing-specific interventions express. The 200-day figure suggests the *strength and mass* adaptations — the ones with the best correlational support — express on a much slower clock. **These are different time constants and should be sequenced accordingly, not run simultaneously at full volume.**

**⚠ Transfer lag is a genuine, unmeasured gap.** **No study anywhere measures the latency between a strength or power adaptation and its expression in throwing velocity** — not in baseball, not in handball. The concept ("delayed transformation," "phase potentiation") exists only in Verkhoshansky/Issurin theory and is applied to baseball by analogy. The nearest empirical proxies are indirect: Driveline retests on a 6-week cycle, implying that is where they expect detectable change; and their own offseason scheduling logic (below) implies ~3 months of lead time is required for a velocity block to fit at all.

**Driveline's published offseason block structure** — the clearest public statement of the lead-time constraint ([Off-Season Baseball Throwing Program](https://drivelinebaseball.com/blogs/blog/off-season-throwing-program)). Four phases inside a ~20-week professional offseason (Oct–Feb), built **backward** from spring training:

1. **On-ramping** — 3–4 weeks, low volume → high intent
2. **Velocity** — 1–4 weeks, scheduled throwing at 100% effort
3. **Mound blending** — ~1 week
4. **Mound development** — 1–4 weeks, pitch design, command, bullpens

**And the scheduling consequence they state explicitly: an October start gets the full block; a November start still includes velocity work; a December start gets on-ramping and mound development with NO velocity phase at all; a January start is preparation only.**

**[EMERGING] The practical rule that falls out of this: if the athlete is not on the mound by early November, the offseason does not contain a velocity block.** That is an industry scheduling heuristic, not a trial result — but it is the most concrete statement of transfer lag available.

**Autoregulation thresholds from the same source** ([Driveline, 2025](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2025/02/understanding-and-managing-training-fatigue-from-theory-to-practice/)): within-week undulation targeting a ~6% weekly velocity decline distributed across sessions; **1–9% performance decline = productive stress; ≥10% = exhaustion requiring 2–3 weeks of recovery**; session termination at 4–6% drop (frequency work) or 6–10% (capacity work). **[EMERGING — industry practice, no validation study.]**

### 5.3 A defensible velocity block for an 85+ arm

**[EMERGING — constructed from the evidence above, not from a periodization trial. Label it as such to the athlete.]**

**Order of operations follows the evidence hierarchy, not tradition:**

1. **Mass and absolute strength first (12+ weeks, deep offseason).** This is the quality with the strongest elite-population correlations (lean mass r = 0.52, body mass r = 0.58) and the slowest time constant. It is also the only one where the athlete is not simultaneously accumulating throwing stress. Target **absolute** force and power output — not per-kilogram numbers, and not vertical jump height.
2. **Reactive strength / stiffness second (concurrent, then emphasized).** Drop jumps, low-amplitude plyometrics, short ground-contact work. RSI is the single jump quality that survived into a professional sample (R² = 0.30). The lead-leg block is a braking event; train braking.
3. **Throwing-specific implement work third (6–10 weeks), in the 3–7 oz window.** This is where the mechanism (arm speed without kinetic penalty, O'Connell 2022) and the outcome data (underload > overload) both point. Heavy implements measurably *slow* the arm (Fleisig 2017) and carry the injury signal from the only RCT.
4. **Peak/expression phase.** High-intent mound work with a single, unchanged radar system.

**Concurrent-training note — there is exactly one baseball study, and it is thin but on-point.**

**Rhea MR, Oliverson JR, Marshall G, Peterson MD, Kenn JG, Naclerio Ayllón F (2008), *JSCR* 22(1):230–4 — n = 16 NCAA D1 baseball players** ([PMID 18296980](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296980/)): across a season, the moderate-to-high-intensity **endurance group LOST 39.50 ± 128.03 W** of lower-body power while the **speed/speed-endurance group GAINED 210.63 ± 168.96 W** (between-group p < .05). Authors: endurance and neuromuscular power training *"appear incompatible when performed simultaneously."*

⚠️ **State the caveats: n = 16, position players not pitchers, no velocity outcome, and the endurance group's SD is 3× its own mean change.** This is the most-cited baseball concurrent-training result and it is fragile. **No study exists on aerobic training interference with throwing velocity specifically.**

**[EMERGING]** High-volume steady-state aerobic work competes with power adaptation, and the aerobic *demand* of pitching is low (see `anatomy-physiology.md` §3.1). The aerobic system's role here is PCr resynthesis between pitches, better served by cardiac-output and repeat-sprint methods than by distance running. **[FOLKLORE]** that running poles builds anything relevant to velocity.

### 5.4 In-season — the finding that contradicts the coaching consensus

**MLB fastball velocity RISES across a season. It does not decay.**

**Podhorzer M (2012), "Historical Monthly Velocity Trends," RotoGraphs/FanGraphs** ([article](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/historical-monthly-velocity-trends/)) — starting pitchers, 2007–2011:

| Year | Mar/Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep/Oct | Season |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | 90.6 | 90.9 | 91.0 | 91.1 | 91.2 | 91.0 | 91.0 |
| 2010 | 90.5 | 90.7 | 90.7 | 90.8 | 90.9 | 90.7 | 90.7 |
| 2009 | 90.4 | 90.5 | 90.7 | 90.9 | 91.2 | 91.0 | 90.8 |
| 2008 | 89.8 | 90.2 | 90.4 | 90.6 | 90.4 | 90.6 | 90.3 |
| 2007 | 89.4 | 89.7 | 89.7 | 89.7 | 89.9 | 90.3 | 89.8 |
| **5-yr avg** | **90.1** | **90.4** | **90.5** | **90.6** | **90.7** | **90.7** | **90.5** |

**Velocity climbs ~0.6 mph across the season, with the largest jump April→May (+0.3) and a plateau from June onward.** Podhorzer's operational warning is the important line: *"pitchers do not suddenly regain two miles per hour on their fastball in the middle of the season."*

**Corroboration at the minor-league level:** Crotin RL, Bhan S, Karakolis T, Ramsey DK (2013), *JSCR* 27(8):2206–2212, **n = 12 MiLB pitchers over 8 games** ([PMID 23222081](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222081/)): **fastball velocity increased linearly across the first 8 games (R = 0.91, p < .001), ~2 m/s total.** Rest days and work:rest ratios had no significant effect.

**[ESTABLISHED at the league level] The widespread belief in monotonic in-season velocity decay is wrong.** Whatever in-season capacity loss occurs does not express as velocity loss. **This reframes "in-season maintenance" as protecting a rising curve, not arresting a falling one** — and it means a mid-season velocity *drop* in an individual is a signal worth investigating, not the expected pattern.

**What about in-season strength loss? The data are much weaker than commonly claimed.**

- **Wilkin LD, Haddock BL (2006), *JSCR* 20(4):829–32, n = 9 D-II pitchers** ([PMID 17194237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17194237/)): isokinetic shoulder IR/ER at preseason, midseason, postseason — **"no differences … at any speed tested or time point examined."** n = 9, so read as "no detectable change," not "no change."
- **⚠ MISINFORMATION CORRECTION.** A claim circulating widely — that collegiate pitchers lose "3–14%" of strength across a season, **attributed to Wilkin & Haddock** — is a misattribution; that paper reports no significant change. *(A separate real finding of 3–14% periscapular decline in a collegiate cohort is carried in `anatomy-physiology.md` §2.7 from a different 2024 IJSPT source; that one stands, but do not attach Wilkin's name to it.)*
- **Merfeld B et al. (2024), *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 9(2):98, n = 12 NCAA D-II/III across a 12-week summer league** ([PMID 38921634](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38921634/)): **throwing-arm shoulder strength composite −9.03% (ES 0.72, p = .08); non-throwing arm −2.03%.** The asymmetry — throwing arm only — is the credible finding. ⚠️ The same paper reports **CMJ peak power +44.79% in 12 weeks**, which is implausible and almost certainly familiarization drift on n = 12.
- **Lambert 2023: an NCAA D-IA control group lost shoulder flexion and IR strength across just 8 offseason weeks** doing their normal program.
- **Hornsby WG et al. (2021), n = 4 D1 players across 3 seasons** ([PMID 33462166](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33462166/)): IMTP peak force **increased** significantly year over year under a maintained program. n = 4 — a case series, but it shows the opposite of decay.

**Honest summary: there is no good in-season strength-loss dataset in collegiate or professional baseball.** The largest samples are n = 9 and n = 12; the D1 longitudinal study is n = 4 and shows gains. **In-season strength maintenance is a defensible practice recommendation with a thin evidence base — and the velocity data suggest whatever is happening to strength is not costing mph.**

## 6. The Ceiling Question

### 6.1 What actually caps an individual

**The ceiling is visible as a gradient: every model's explanatory power collapses as the population gets more selected.** That collapse *is* the ceiling.

| Model | Population | Mean mph | Variance explained |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sgroi 2015, n = 420 | ⚑ Youth/adolescent | — | R² = 0.78 — **but age alone carries 0.658** |
| Manzi 2024, n = 59 | ⚑ High school | 70.0 | **R² = 0.925** |
| Werner 2008, n = 54 | ⚑ Collegiate | 78.3 | ~0.68 (10 predictors on n = 54 — overfit) |
| Huang 2022, n = 64 | Adult amateur tryout | ~79.5 | **adj. R² = 0.230** |
| **Manzi 2024, n = 337** | **Professional** | **85.7** | **adj. R² = 0.536** |
| **Fernandez 2026, n = 322** | **NCAA D1, in-game** | — | **R² = 0.29, RMSE 2.70 mph** |
| Driveline force plate | Undisclosed | — | R² = 0.54, MAE ±2.7 mph |

**Read the gradient, not any single row.** In high schoolers, kinematics explains 93% of velocity. In professionals it explains 54%. In the largest in-game D1 sample with full biomechanics plus anthropometry, 29% — and **81% of that model's variable importance was height.**

**[ESTABLISHED] Half or more of what separates an 87 mph professional from a 93 mph professional is not captured by any measurement we currently take.** And the practical precision limit on any capacity-based velocity prediction is **±2.7 mph** — which is, not coincidentally, larger than a realistic annual gain at this level.

**⚑ A warning about the youth models everyone quotes.** Sgroi 2015's famous "78% of variance" collapses to roughly 12% once age is removed (age alone R² = 0.658). It is a maturation model wearing a mechanics model's clothes. Its per-unit figures — +1.5 mph/year of age, +1.2 mph/inch of height, +2.6 mph for hip-shoulder separation, +1.9 mph per 10% stride-length increase — **should not anchor priors for an adult.**

The ceiling is built from, in rough order of contribution:

1. **Anthropometry — fixed.** Leg length β = 0.292 (+12.7 cm ≈ +3.9 mph); height an independent predictor even in a selected tryout sample. **A 6'6" pitcher and a 5'11" pitcher do not have the same ceiling, and no program closes that gap.**
2. **Passive/elastic properties of the shoulder — largely fixed by early adulthood.** The −201 J of elastic storage and the ~11.8 kW of recoil power (§4.1) depend on capsular and tendinous properties plus the layback arc, which is substantially a function of **humeral retrotorsion laid down before skeletal maturity and locked thereafter** (`anatomy-physiology.md` §4.1). **This is, in my view, the most under-appreciated ceiling determinant in the sport.** It is also why velocity development has an age window.
3. **Lean mass and absolute power — trainable, and the biggest genuine lever.** r = 0.58 / 0.52 / 0.43.
4. **Reactive strength — trainable.** R² = 0.30 in pros.
5. **Mechanical efficiency — trainable, and where the within-elite separation lives.** Not more velocity per se, but velocity per unit of load (Crotin et al. 2022; `biomechanics.md` §4.5).

### 6.2 The ceiling, measured directly: year-over-year change by baseline velocity

**This is the single best quantitative expression of a velocity ceiling I could verify, and every coach working with an 85+ arm should know it.**

**"Investigating Fastball Velo Changes," MLB Data Warehouse Substack, author "Jon A", 13 February 2024** — Statcast 2021–2023, four-seam + sinker, minimum 50 pitches, **n = 1,163** ([article](https://www.mlbdatawarehouse.com/p/investigating-fastball-velo-changes)):

> ### ⚠️ CORRECTED 2026-08-13 — TWO CORRECTIONS. DO NOT QUOTE THE MEAN WITHOUT THE CAVEAT.
>
> **1. Every prior statement in this library describing "1,163 pitcher-seasons" is wrong. **Do not re-import.**
>
> **2. >
> **The 2.5% (≥+2 mph) and 12.1% (≥+1 mph) figures verified verbatim against the article and stand.** So does the by-bin pattern below, which is the part that actually carries the ceiling argument.
>
> **Provenance:** single-author grey literature on Substack, **no code, no data file, no peer review.** The method is transparently described, which is why it's usable at all — but it is not a citation, it is a blog post with arithmetic in it.

### 6.3 The aging curve — the default trajectory is DOWN

**Petti B & Zimmerman J, "Pitcher Aging Curves," FanGraphs (2012)** — the canonical work, winner of the 2013 SABR Analytics Research Award, and **blog analysis, not peer-reviewed.** Delta/paired-season method, year-over-year change weighted by harmonic mean of IP, Baseball Info Solutions data 2002–2011.

| Finding | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Within ~0.5 mph of peak | **ages 23–28** | [Introduction](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/pitcher-aging-curves-introduction/) |
| Cumulative loss accelerates | **~1 mph/yr from age 29** | Introduction |
| Total career cumulative loss | **~3.75 mph** | Introduction |
| **Starters** | peak early 20s (~24); down a full 1 mph by **age 26**; ~2 mph below peak by 30 | [Starters and Relievers](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/pitcher-aging-curves-starters-and-relievers/) |
| **Relievers** | do not lose a full mph off peak until **age 32**, then decline sharply | Starters and Relievers |
| Age-24 mean FB 91.1 → age-34 89.4 | survivorship-attenuated | [Attrition](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/pitcher-attrition-and-velocity-decline-by-age/) |
| **~40% of pitchers hold velocity**, losing only **0.3 mph total from 21→38**, plateauing 25–30 | ~60% decline | [Maintaining Velocity](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/pitcher-aging-curves-maintaining-velocity/) |

⚠️ **An older, smaller analysis disagrees on peak age.** Kalk J (2008), *The Hardball Times*, n = 143, PITCHf/x, single year-pair, reported **+1.5 mph from age 24 to a peak at 28–29.** The author titled it "Preliminary" and expressed surprise at the steepness. **Treat as historical, superseded by the larger delta-method work.**

**[ESTABLISHED, with survivorship caveat] At the major-league level, velocity does not improve with age, and starters begin losing it in their mid-20s.** The window for adding velocity is roughly **17–24**. After that, a program's realistic goal shifts from "add mph" to "delay the loss."

**But note the 40%.** Roughly two in five professional pitchers hold their velocity essentially flat from 21 to 38. **That is the achievable target for a mature arm, and it is a real training outcome, not a consolation prize.** A 26-year-old MiLB arm holding 94 for three more years has beaten the curve.

### 6.4 Context: the whole sport is getting faster, which is not the same as individuals getting faster

League-average four-seam velocity, per Associated Press reporting (July 2026) and FanGraphs:

| Season | League-avg 4-seam FB |
|---|---|
| 2008 (first tracked) | **91.9** |
| 2013 | 92.7 |
| 2021 | 93.7 |
| 2025 (full) | **94.5** |
| **2026 (through mid-July)** | **94.7 — sixth consecutive rising season** |

RHP average 95.2; RH relievers 95.6. Triple-A rose from 92.7 (2022, first tracked) to 93.6 (2026). **Six pitchers averaged ≥100 mph on the four-seamer in 2026.**

**[ESTABLISHED] The population mean is rising ~0.2 mph/year.** But this is **selection, not development** — it reflects who gets rostered, who gets drafted, and reliever usage patterns, layered on top of whatever individual development is occurring. **A rising league average does not mean individual pitchers are gaining; the year-over-year data in §6.2 says most are not.** Do not let one be used as evidence for the other. One directly relevant cohort datapoint: **starters aged ≤25 averaged 94.7 mph in early 2022, an all-time high for that cohort.**

### 6.5 The approach to the ceiling in adolescence — where the mph actually get added

**Eisenmann J (2026), "Chasing Velo," *Archives of IronMan Performance* Vol. 1** ([Substack](https://joeeisenmann.substack.com/p/chasing-velo-a-preliminary-analysis)) — **self-published, NOT peer-reviewed**, though the author is a legitimate pediatric exercise scientist. n = 25 MLB pitchers, 154 retrospective observations reconstructed from Perfect Game showcase records, Baseball-Reference, and Statcast; maturity indexed by percent of adult height.

**Reconstructed trajectory:**

| Age | Velocity | Annual gain |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | 72 mph | — |
| 14 | 78 | **+6** |
| 15 | 84 | **+6** |
| 16 | 89 | **+5** |
| 17 | 92 | **+3** |
| 18 | 94 | **+2** |

80% of peak velocity at ~13.5 yr; 90% at ~15.4 yr; several at 100% of peak by 17–18. A **plateau across 85–91% of adult height (around peak height velocity)**, then resumption of gains at ≥95% of adult height.

⚠️ **Severe limitation: retrospective selection on the outcome.** All 25 became MLB pitchers. This is the survivors' trajectory, not a prognostic curve. The sample is explicitly tall, large, and early-maturing (~1 year ahead of norms).

**But the deceleration structure — +6, +6, +5, +3, +2 — is exactly the shape a ceiling hypothesis predicts, and it is the only source that charts the approach *through* the 85+ transition.** By the time an athlete reaches our floor, he is on the flat part of his own curve. **[EMERGING]**

### 6.6 Two things that do not exist, and you should know their names

**No published velocity-ceiling model exists.** Not peer-reviewed, not industry. The concept appears only as: (i) Driveline's informal exclusion of 45+ FV prospects as "closer to their velocity ceiling"; (ii) the bin-wise pattern in §6.2; (iii) the "40% who hold velocity" split in the FanGraphs work; (iv) capacity-based prediction with ±2.7 mph error. **Nobody has built the model. That is why this question is worth asking.**

**No heritability estimate for throwing or pitching velocity exists.** No twin study, no h². The nearest verified adjacent work (Maes HH et al., 1996, *MSSE*, [PMID 8970142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8970142/), 105 twin pairs, nine motor tests) **contains no throwing test at all.** Adolescent twin estimates for grip strength (~59%), vertical jump (~49%), and standing long jump (~52%) exist but are proxies, and the specific figures could not be verified at primary source.

> ⚠️ **This is exactly where AI content farms fabricate. If you encounter a "twin study on throwing velocity heritability" with a specific h², assume it is invented until you open the PubMed record yourself.**

### 6.7 How I would estimate where an 88 mph arm can get to

**[EMERGING — this is a defensible procedure, not a validated model. No published model predicts an individual ceiling.]**

**Step 1 — establish the physical-quality prediction.** Force-plate test (squat jump peak power absolute, CMJ RSI-mod, 10-to-5 RSI, IMTP net peak force) and get a predicted velocity. Error bar is **±2.7 mph** and honesty requires stating it.

**Step 2 — compute the gap.** Predicted minus actual.
- **Actual > predicted:** he is already converting his physical qualities efficiently. **His velocity is limited by his engine.** Gains must come from mass, absolute power, and reactive strength, on a 6–12 month clock.
- **Actual < predicted:** he has physical capacity he is not expressing. **His velocity is limited by his delivery.** This is the higher-yield, faster-timeline case.

**Step 3 — apply the anthropometric discount.** An 88 mph arm at 6'5" with long levers has a different ceiling than an 88 mph arm at 5'11". Leg length is worth ~3.9 mph per 12.7 cm in professionals; that is not recoverable.

**Step 4 — apply the age discount.** 18 → wide open. 22 → narrowing. 26+ → the aging curve is working against you.

**Step 5 — check the two mechanical variables with a professional-sample mph coefficient and no documented torque cost.** Is his stride ≥80% of body height? Is his pelvis open at foot contact? (§4.4)

⚠️ **REVISED 2026-08-13.** The previous version said *"if he is short-striding at 74% BH, there is ~1–1.5 mph sitting there."* **Delete that sentence and do not re-import it.** Two independent acute manipulations of stride length found no gain, and one found the pitcher's own stride was significantly *faster* than a lengthened one. **A short stride is a flag to investigate — is something constraining it? — not mph sitting on the table.** Treat both variables as *descriptors of the delivery you are looking at*, not as inventory.

**Step 6 — state a range with a probability of zero attached.** For a healthy 21-year-old at 88 with a clear physical-quality deficit and a short stride: **90–92 within 12–18 months is a defensible target — and roughly a 1-in-5 chance of no gain at all.** For a 25-year-old at 88 who already tests well and already strides long: **the honest answer may be that 88–89 is close to his ceiling, and the leverage is in efficiency, command, and pitch mix rather than mph.**

**Say the 1-in-5 out loud.** In the best population-matched dataset available — 58 high-level athletes entering at ≥88 mph — **18.4% LOST more than 1 mph**, only 42% gained a full mph, and **the group mean was +0.65 mph** (⚠️ corrected 2026-08-13 from +1.35). That is the base rate at the best-resourced facility in the sport, and a coach who never mentions it is selling.

## 7. Synthesis: Ranked by Evidence Quality

### Highest confidence

1. ⚠️ **SOFTENED 2026-08-13 — was "the removal experiment is the best evidence in the document."** **Keep lifting — but on general grounds and on the correlational case, NOT on Gdovin.** The single study behind the "floor protection" framing is an **uncontrolled n = 12 pre-post with no control group** and an unobtained effect magnitude (§5.5), running against a season phase in which velocity normally *rises*. **It is not a removal experiment and it is not the strongest design in the document.**
   **What actually justifies lifting at this level:** body mass r = 0.58 and lean mass r = 0.52 with velocity (n = 33 D1); CMJ concentric impulse r = 0.71; absolute cycle-ergometer power r ≈ 0.44 replicated in D1 and MiLB; drop-jump RSI R² = 0.30 in 53 professionals; and Lambert 2023's documentation that a D1 control group **lost** rotator-cuff strength across 8 normal off-season weeks. **All correlational or off-outcome. Zero intervention trials with a velocity outcome.** Say so.
   **The recommendation survives. Its evidentiary rank does not — it drops below #2b.**
2. ⚠️ **DEMOTED 2026-08-13 — was "lengthen the stride toward ≥80% of body height."** **Stride length is the best cross-sectional CORRELATE, and it is not a demonstrated lever.** +2.0 mph per 10% BH in 315 professionals is real and cross-sectional. But **two independent groups have experimentally manipulated stride length and neither found a gain** — the Buffalo cohort (Ramsey/Crotin/White 2014, *Hum Mov Sci* 38:185–196, n = 19, ±25%: velocity equivalent) and Matsuda et al. 2025 (*Front Sports Act Living*, n = 20, ±20%: **the pitcher's own normal stride was significantly FASTER than both altered conditions**). And the %-body-height normalization is contradicted by Solomito 2023 (n = 99) and Yanagisawa 2020 (n = 18, %BH p = .15). **The honest reading: a long stride is a marker of a pitcher who throws hard, not a lever that makes one.** Do not sell it as +2 mph. See §4.4 and `open-disputes.md` #13.

2b. **Train the trunk and hips as the power source — now with two controlled trials behind it.** [EMERGING, and this is what stride length was demoted *below*] Lee/Choi/Jeon 2026 (n = 29 collegiate, baseline max 86.2 mph, 12 wk rotational/med-ball "core-coiling" vs machine strength: **max p = .003, average p = .011, ANCOVA p = .014/.042**) and Oyama 2026 (n = 54 ⚑ HS, 8 wk lumbopelvic-hip, **p = .014**). **Two countries, two populations, two controlled trials, same target.** Both non-ideal designs; together they are the best positive intervention evidence in this document. §3.4.
3. **Add lean mass and absolute power.** [ESTABLISHED as correlate] Body mass r = 0.58, lean mass r = 0.52 (D1); CMJ concentric impulse r = 0.71 and CMJ peak power r = 0.68 (D1); absolute cycle-ergometer power r ≈ 0.44 replicated in D1 and MiLB. **Measure and train absolute output — never per-kilogram, never jump height.** No intervention trial exists, which is the honest weakness.
4. **Open the pelvis at foot contact.** [EMERGING] **~+1.6 mph with no elbow-torque cost**, n = 157 professionals. The second and last "free" mechanical lever.
5. **Train reactive strength / stiffness, not jump height.** [EMERGING, strong] Drop jump R² = 0.30 in **53 professionals**; CMJ and squat jump both non-significant for velocity in that same sample. Jump height r = 0.07 in D1.
6. **Train the trail hip and trunk as the power source.** [ESTABLISHED mechanistically + one controlled intervention] Trunk power alone R² = 0.731 with ball velocity; trunk torques are the primary energy source with the shoulder and elbow acting as transfer elements; energy *generation* is higher in the trailing leg. Oyama 2026 is the only controlled non-implement study to raise ball speed — an 8-week lumbopelvic-hip block (⚑ HS sample), which also raised elbow varus moment.
7. **If using implements, use underload (3–5 oz), not overload.** [EMERGING] Underload beat overload head-to-head; the controlled positives are underload-only; 3–7 oz manipulates arm speed with no measured kinetic penalty in collegiate/pro pitchers; ≥5 oz progressively *slows* the arm.

### Overrated

- **Weighted balls as a velocity program for a mature arm.** The only two studies in collegiate/professional pitchers are both **null**. The famous positive result is +1.6 mph net in 15-year-olds throwing 67. Driveline's own +7.1 mph "MaxVelo" figure came from a 68–72 mph junior-high cohort.
- **Medicine ball training.** The literature everyone cites (Szymanski) is **high school hitters with bat-speed outcomes.** The one med-ball entry in the pitching review was non-significant.
- **Jump height and relative power.** r = 0.07 and 0.19, both non-significant. Dividing power by body mass deletes the signal.
- **Grip strength.** In the best population match available — **87 D1 pitchers averaging 84.83 mph** — there was **no significant association with ball velocity at all.** The widely circulated r = 0.705 figure appears to be fabricated.
- **Trunk mobility and movement screens.** Trunk rotation vs velocity **r = 0.131, p = .491** in 30 D1 pitchers; Y-Balance, nothing.
- **Rate of force development.** Contributed nothing to throwing velocity where max force explained 12–13%.
- **Peak GRF numbers.** Discrete peaks are weak predictors in skilled throwers; the signal is in braking *timing* (27–35% of foot-contact-to-release), not magnitude.
- **"Get down the mound faster."** Max forward COM velocity showed only a weak trend in 332 in-game D1 pitchers; only early "body drift" mattered.
- **"Fix the sequence."** Timing of peak trunk rotation is retained in high-school models and **drops out of the professional model entirely.** It saturates by our band.
- **Long toss as an evidenced velocity tool.** No training study exists. The paper everyone cites measured no velocity outcome.
- **In-season velocity "decay."** League velocity **rises ~0.6 mph across a season.** A mid-season drop in an individual is a signal, not the norm.
- **Case-study marketing.** The denominator is the 18–21% who lose velocity.

### Genuinely unknown

Velocity-based training, olympic lifts vs. squats vs. trap bar, sprint/sled training, plyo-ball-only protocols, constraint drills, block vs. undulating vs. concurrent periodization, detraining/retraining timelines, transfer lag. **Zero intervention studies with a pitch-velocity outcome in any baseball population.** They are not disproven — they are untested. Coaches should stop citing evidence that does not exist for them.

## 9. Open Questions

1. **Does anything add velocity to an arm already at 85+?** The two studies nearest our population are both null and both underpowered. **This is the central unanswered question of the entire field**, and it is answerable — a randomized, 12-week, 85+-only trial with radar (not encumbered mocap) post-testing at full intent.
2. **Who responds?** Response is wildly heterogeneous (80/8/12 in Reinold; 52/27/21 at Driveline) and **no study has characterized responders versus non-responders.** The predicted-vs-actual gap (§6.7) is a plausible moderator and has never been tested as one.
3. **Does the mass/lean-mass correlation reflect a causal within-athlete lever?** r = 0.58 between subjects. **Nobody has run a mass-gain intervention in pitchers and measured velocity.** This is the single highest-value untested study given that mass is the strongest correlate in the literature.
4. **Do reactive-strength gains transfer?** RSI is the one jump quality that predicts velocity in professionals. There is no RSI-training intervention with a velocity outcome.
5. **What is the actual transfer lag?** Positive throwing studies run 6–10 weeks; the aggregate strength/mass signal appears at 200+ days. **No study anywhere — baseball, handball, any throwing sport — measures the latency between a strength adaptation and its expression in ball speed.** The concept exists only in periodization theory.
6. **Is the elastic/passive shoulder contribution trainable at all in a mature arm**, or is the −201 J of storage capacity fixed by retrotorsion and capsular adaptation laid down before skeletal maturity? If fixed, it is the hard ceiling, and we should be able to measure it.
7. ⚠️ **REWRITTEN 2026-08-13 — the premise was wrong. Do not re-import "removing strength training costs velocity."**
   The previous version asked why removal costs velocity when addition doesn't produce it, and called the asymmetry *"the most interesting unexplained result in the document."* **There may be no asymmetry to explain.** Gdovin is an **uncontrolled n = 12 pre-post with no control group**, not a removal experiment — so one arm of the supposed asymmetry is a single observational study against no counterfactual, in a season phase where velocity normally rises.
   **The live question is narrower: does resistance training have ANY demonstrable velocity effect at 85+, in either direction?** Nothing in the literature answers it. **What would settle it: a dose-response trial** — maintenance / +30% / +60% volume, 16 weeks, 85+, radar at full intent, ≥8 dated post-test sessions. **And for the price of an afternoon: obtain the Gdovin full text**, recover the actual mph values, and check the cohort's prior-year fall trajectory. See `open-disputes.md` #14.
8. ⚠️ **REWRITTEN 2026-08-13 — the previous version of this question was factually wrong and said "nobody has lengthened a professional's stride." Do not re-import.**
   **Does the stride-length finding survive a LONGITUDINAL intervention, given that it has already failed two ACUTE ones?** Two independent groups instructed pitchers to alter stride ±20–25% and measured velocity: the Buffalo cohort found it **equivalent** across conditions; Matsuda 2025 found the pitcher's **own normal stride significantly faster than both** the shortened and lengthened conditions. Neither is a training study — both are acute, instructed, sub-elite (~73–81 mph), n = 19–20. **So the open question is now narrower and more interesting: does an 8–12 week block that removes a CONSTRAINT on stride (drive-leg ankle dorsiflexion, incoming momentum, lead-hip capacity) produce a longer stride the athlete owns, and does THAT carry velocity?** The nearest existing work is an unpublished 2026 Univ. of Nevada, Reno master's thesis (Buck; advisor Pavilionis) — D1 pitchers, 4-week resistance-band ankle-mobility + balance block, pre/post stride length, **no velocity outcome, no control group.** **Someone should request that thesis.** Design note: use **release** velocity, never perceived/effective velocity, since a longer stride raises extension and would manufacture a false gain. And note single-camera markerless stride error is **5.75% of body height** — more than half the size of the 10% change at issue.

**Six things that simply do not exist, worth stating as a set:** no periodization comparison in pitchers with velocity outcomes; no transfer-lag measurement; no detraining/retraining velocity data in pitchers; no velocity-ceiling model; no throwing-velocity heritability estimate; and no controlled mechanics-coaching intervention with a velocity outcome in an 85+ population.
