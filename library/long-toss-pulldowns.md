## 1. The finding that organises everything else: the exposure is undefined

**"Long toss" is not one intervention.** Professional baseball's own survey (Stone et al. 2017, *Orthop J Sports Med*, PMC5315237 — **321 respondents across five MLB organisations: 271 pitchers, 19 pitching coaches, 31 athletic trainers**) found:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| At what distance does a throw become "long toss"? | **≈175 ft overall.** Pitchers ≈177 ft, pitching coaches ≈177 ft, **athletic trainers ≈157 ft** |
| On a line, or not? | **≈1/3 "on a line"; a clearly larger share "NOT on a line"** ⚠️ exact splits UNVERIFIED |
| Crow hop? | **≈60% in the "not on a line" group vs ≈28% in the "on a line" group** |

Two consequences, and they run through the whole file:

1. **A 20-ft disagreement between the pitcher and the person managing his arm** about where the category even begins.
2. **No study of "long toss" is a study of what another study, or your pitcher, is doing.** The only dedicated review of the topic says so explicitly — Calcei & Freehill (2021), *Curr Rev Musculoskelet Med*: the variation is "determined by the type of long-toss: shorter distance and on a line versus maximum distance and not on a line," and "the exact benefits of long-toss are difficult to quantify due to the numerous definitions in use."

**Before any sentence about long toss has content, four parameters must be stated: distance, on a line or on an arc, crow hop or not, and intent.** (F-735, F-736)

## 2. What the training literature contains: one randomised abstract, and a gap

**F-038's gap survives seven years intact, and now extends to pull-downs.** No trial anywhere (a) assigned a long-toss or pull-down dose, (b) measured MOUND fastball velocity pre and post, and (c) reported the sample's mean velocity. Two independent search passes, both empty. (F-738)

**The closest object — and read the design before the result.** Peters, Maliska, DeLeon & Coste (Linfield College, 2018, **student symposium abstract**): n = 20 collegiate throwers, 18–22, randomised to **6 weeks of long toss or a weighted-ball programme, ≈10 per arm.**

- **Throwing velocity: did not change. In either arm.**
- **Throwing distance: long toss 93.2 → 97.3 yd; weighted ball 93.6 → 94.29 yd.**
- Shoulder ROM improved in both; abduction most in the long-toss arm.

🚨 **This is NOT a demonstrated null.** No non-throwing control (both arms threw), no reported sample velocity, n ≈ 10 per arm — far below the detection floor (a 10-vs-10 between-athlete velocity split detects ≈2.4 mph at best). A conference abstract with no retrievable sample is not evidence for a magnitude.

⭐ **What it IS worth is the dissociation: the trained variable moved +4.1 yards; ball speed did not move at all.** That is the shape of a specificity result. Distance is largely a launch-angle problem — F-736 records that forward trunk flexion at front foot contact *decreases* as distance grows, i.e. the thrower stands up and arcs it. **Long toss makes you better at long toss.** (F-737)

**The best-designed, best-matched study in the entire topic measured no performance at all.** Luo et al. (2021), *Sports Health* 13(3):237–244, PMC8083146: **n = 24 NCAA Division I pitchers, age 20.0 ± 1.1, properly randomised** to ultra-long-toss (13) vs straight-line long toss (11), five timepoints over three days — **outcomes: shoulder range of motion only.** Somebody had the right athletes and the right allocation and spent it on passive ROM. (F-738)

## 3. Why a pull-down reads faster than a mound fastball

**The ground is not the variable. The run-up is.** Two datasets that look contradictory reconcile completely once footwork is separated from surface:

| Condition | Ball velocity vs mound | Load vs mound | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Static flat ground** | **≈82% of maximum** | **No significant difference** in humeral internal rotation torque or elbow valgus load at ANY distance | Slenker et al. 2014, *AJSM*, n = 29 college (⚠️ one summary says 27 — n UNVERIFIED) |
| **Crow-hop flat ground** | **HIGHER** — pelvis, shoulder AND ball velocity all increased | not isolated | Fleisig et al. 2016, *Sports Health* 9(3):210–215, n = 25 HS + college |
| **Mound vs static flat, randomised within athlete** | **mound +≈2.0 mph** | — | F-464 (Dowling 2020, read in full, ⚠️ 76.5 mph sample) |

Fleisig's own attribution: the gain comes from **"running forward while throwing."**

**So the pull-down number is bought with forward momentum the pitching rules forbid.** A pitcher must stay in contact with the rubber until the stride foot lands. He cannot run. **The pull-down is therefore a different task, not a mound number minus coaching.** (F-739)

⚠️ **And note the exchange rate on static flat ground: full mound-level elbow and shoulder load at 82% of the output. Whatever flat-ground work is for, it is not a cheap day.**

### 3a. The quantity the run-up adds explains ~3% of pitch velocity among D1 pitchers

**Zappa RM, Lerch BG, et al. (2026), *J Biomech* 205:113411, PMID 42308802** — **n = 332 pitchers measured DURING NCAA Division I games**, 8-camera markerless capture at 300 Hz:

- Maximum forward centre-of-mass velocity vs pitch velocity: **a weak positive trend, large between-pitcher differences.**
- Statistical parametric mapping: significant positive association at 0–1% and 4–7% of the motion, **maximum r = 0.164 → R² ≈ 2.7%.**
- Authors recommend judging the relationship pitcher by pitcher; separately report that generating COM velocity **before peak knee height** ("body drift") associated with higher pitch velocity.

⚠️ **Two-pass citation discrepancy (F-727):** pass 1 attached this to *J Biomech* 188:112775 (2025), pass 2 to 205:113411 (2026) — probably two papers from one group. **r = 0.164 and n = 332 replicated; the citation did not. Cite the PMID.**

🚨 **THE SYMMETRIC READING, AND IT IS THE POINT OF THIS PROGRAM.** The useful half: a pull-down number tells you little about the rubber, which is the same conclusion §3 reaches from mechanics. **The half that must be said out loud: this does NOT establish that increasing a given pitcher's forward momentum would not help him.** It is a between-subject correlation in a selected, range-restricted sample — the exact condition under which correlations collapse whether or not the lever is real (Luera 2020, r = .17–.29, n = 149 pros). **Stride length and extension both died of this inference run in the other direction. Running it this direction is the same error with the sign flipped.** The within-athlete slope has never been measured. **Verdict: not a marker to chase, not a lever to dismiss.** (F-740)

## 4. The gap, and the number nobody has: its reliability

**Vendor data only. No peer review, no control, no published method.**

| Claim | Figure | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| VeloU, ≈23 athletes, after a deload + "blending" phase | **5.7 mph** mean pull-down-to-mound gap | replicated on two passes |
| PRP Baseball, 57 pitchers | "6.1 mph mean, range 1–10 mph" | 🚨 **UNVERIFIED — failed the second pass. Do not quote.** |
| "Shuffle" pull-downs (lateral step, not a run) | **1–3 mph** above mound | unreplicated; direction matches §3's mechanism |
| Coaching blogs, full run-and-gun | 8–10 mph | unreplicated |

**Carry the order of magnitude and nothing else: a run-up is worth roughly five to six miles an hour of radar; a shuffle maybe one to three.**

⭐ **The real finding is the absence.** The industry interprets the *size* of an individual's gap — "a big gap means he is losing connection and energy transfer" — and **no reliability estimate, within-athlete SD, or ICC for the gap exists from any source.** If a pitcher's own gap wanders ±2 mph session to session, the diagnosis is noise. That is free to settle: §7. (F-741)

## 5. Arm load: three datasets, three answers, one convergence

| Study | Population | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Fleisig et al. 2011**, *JOSPT* 41(5):296–303, PMID 21212502 | n = 17 college | Mound + flat ground at 37 m, 55 m, max (~80 m). 37/55 m "hard, on a horizontal line"; **max distance trajectory UNCONSTRAINED.** Max-distance throws: **greatest elbow varus torque, 100 ± 18 N·m**, greatest shoulder IR torque; **forward trunk tilt at release DECREASED with distance.** "Caution is advised." |
| **Cross, Dziuk & Raasch 2019**, ISBS proceedings | **n = 19 PROFESSIONAL** | 18–91 m, **approach and arc NOT restricted** — the only study that let pitchers throw their own way. Kinetics rose linearly with distance but were **LOWER than pitching at shorter distances and did not significantly exceed average pitching kinetics even at max distance.** ⚠️ conference proceedings |
| **Leafblad et al. 2019**, *Sports Health*, PMID 31478791 | n = 60 HS + college | IMU + radar, structured long toss. **Ball velocity changed at each distance; elbow torque did NOT. Mound pitching produced no more elbow torque than long toss from 120 ft onward.** ⭐ **Reliability: ≈91% of throwers acceptable for BALL VELOCITY vs ≈79% for ELBOW TORQUE.** |
| motusBASEBALL sleeve study (IJPEFS) | college | No significant velocity differences across 27–55 m and the mound |
| **F-137** (Reinold et al. 2026, *IJSPT* 21(4):428–439) | NCAA D1 | **Mound-equivalent elbow varus torque past ≈120 ft** |

**FIVE datasets. ZERO reported sample mean velocities.** Ninth topic in the registry where that is true.

**The one convergence: ≈120 ft**, reached independently by Leafblad and Reinold with different instruments. **What does NOT survive: any claim that max-distance arc throws are dangerous, or that they are safe.** The best-population study says the load never exceeds pitching; the most-cited says max distance is the worst. §1 dissolves the paradox — "max distance, unconstrained" is not one task either. (F-742)

### 5a. F-137's source is under published attack

**Wong, Evans & Meister (2024), *IJSPT* 19(5), PMC11065779** — letter to the editor concerning the workload-based interval throwing program. Objections: **(1)** unvalidated device for elbow torque; **(2)** throwing technique not controlled while daily workload was summed across every throw — unsound when it is unknown whether each throw was an arc or on a line; **(3)** torque values inconsistent with marker-based motion capture; **(4)** **IMU accuracy decreases as torque increases** — least trustworthy exactly where the decision sits. **Authors' rebuttal: PMC11065784** — the letter contains statements "not supported by science and may be misleading"; the program "is based upon reliable biomechanical data."

⚠️ **F-137 is ANNOTATED, not withdrawn.** Its instruction holds, because Leafblad independently reaches 120 ft with a different instrument. But **objection (2) is not a matter of opinion: §1 proves technique varies, and crow-hop use differs 60% vs 28% by style.** And Leafblad's 91%/79% reliability split is a quantitative version of the letter's point, published by the same senior-author group that wrote the rebuttal.

**OPERATING RULE EARNED: when a registered finding rests on a wearable-derived kinetic number, search that journal for a letter to the editor before trusting it. Cost: one search.** (F-743)

### 5b. Same number, opposite programmes

The shared fact — mound-level torque at ≈120 ft — supports two contradictory instructions:

- **Leafblad:** therefore **introduce the mound EARLIER**, once he is comfortable at 120 ft; the mound adds no load he is not already taking.
- **Reinold/F-137:** therefore **reclassify long toss past 120 ft from arm care to mound-equivalent load** and count it in the ledger.

**Both are correct. Which you act on depends on whether you are trying to get somewhere or to avoid something.** For a healthy 85+ arm in a fall development block the objective is performance, so **Leafblad's reading is live: if he is comfortable at 120 ft, the mound is not an escalation — and the mound is the only surface ever shown to be worth 2 mph (F-464). Get him on the mound sooner and stop treating flat-ground distance as a prerequisite ladder.**

⭐ **Second time in two days: a fact cannot choose between two programmes with different objectives. The decision rule is upstream of the data.** (F-744)

## 7. The applied output — audit the gap before you diagnose with it

**Free. Uses the radar log the program already owns. One afternoon.**

**PROTOCOL.** Per arm, in the SAME session: **10 max-intent pull-downs and 10 max-intent mound fastballs, order counterbalanced** (trial order is unreported in most of this corpus's literature and is free to control — F-413, F-553). Repeat across **3 sessions**, 6 arms. Compute each athlete's gap per session, then the **within-athlete between-session SD of the gap.**

**ARITHMETIC** at the corpus working assumption σ = 1.0 mph per throw (F-289 — an ASSUMPTION, never published for an 85+ arm):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| SE of one session's gap (10 + 10) | **0.45 mph** |
| SE of the change in gap between two sessions | **0.63 mph** |
| **Minimum detectable change in an individual's gap (95%)** | **≈1.24 mph** |
| Throws of each type needed to POWER a 1.0 mph change at 80% | **≈31 of each** — a pull-down day nobody should run |
| MDC at 20 of each | ≈0.88 mph |

**DECISION RULE: if the within-athlete between-session SD of the gap approaches the between-athlete spread the industry reports (1–10 mph — itself UNVERIFIED), then "his gap is big, he has a transfer problem" is noise and the practice stops.**

**What you tell the pitcher:** *"Your pull-down number and your mound number are two different throws — one of them you get a running start on, and the rules don't let you run. We're going to find out how steady the difference is for you before we read anything into it."*

**What the failure looks like on video:** on the long or max-effort flat-ground throw his **trunk stands up at front foot contact** — less forward flexion than on the mound — and the ball leaves on an arc. **That is a launch-angle throw and it is not a rehearsal of a pitch** (the mechanism is F-736).

**How you know it is working:** you do not, in velocity terms. **This cycle produces a measurement, not a prescription**, because §2 establishes there has never been a trial.

🚨 **STANDING PREREQUISITE, FIVE CONSECUTIVE DAYS AT THE TOP OF THIS VAULT: the 1.0 mph within-outing fastball SD is a corpus assumption, never published for an 85+ arm, and it is load-bearing in F-289, F-659, F-684, F-689, F-706, F-718, F-731 and in every figure above. It is in the program's own TrackMan log. One afternoon re-prices eight findings and this protocol with them.** (F-746)

## 8. Dispute #27's tenth venue

**Zero accuracy, command or location outcomes in this entire literature.** Ten objects, ten for ten: Stone 2017 (survey) · Calcei & Freehill 2021 (kinematics/kinetics) · Linfield 2018 (velocity, distance, ROM) · Luo 2021 (ROM) · Slenker 2014 (loads, velocity) · Fleisig 2016 (segment velocities) · Zappa 2026 (velocity) · Fleisig 2011 (torques) · Cross 2019 (kinetics) · Leafblad 2019 (velocity, torque, reliability).

⭐ **It bites unusually hard here, because the stated purpose of the pull-down phase, in its own coaches' words, is to carry explosive long-toss arm speed back into a 60-ft delivery — a claim about transfer INTO a precision task. The literature contains no measurement of the precision half of its own rationale.**

🚨 **And the question a coach would actually ask — does a week of max-intent pull-downs leave a pitcher unable to find the glove-side corner on Friday — has never been looked at, anywhere, by anyone.** (F-745)

## 9. What to say, in one paragraph

Long toss is not one thing, so ask which one. It trains distance, and distance is partly an arc problem — the one randomised trial that measured both saw distance move four yards and ball speed not move at all. The extra miles per hour on a pull-down are bought with a running start the rules forbid, and the quantity that running start adds separates 332 Division I pitchers from each other by about three percent of variance — which is a reason to distrust the radar number, not a reason to conclude the run-up is worthless, because that inference in reverse is what killed stride length. Past about 120 feet, long toss loads the arm like a mound pitch; two groups agree on the distance and draw opposite instructions from it, and for a healthy 85+ arm chasing performance the live instruction is get him on the mound sooner, because the mound is the only surface ever shown to be worth two miles an hour. Nobody has ever run a long-toss or pull-down training trial with a mound-velocity outcome, nobody has measured anyone's command after any of it, and nobody has checked whether the pull-down-to-mound gap everyone diagnoses with is even reliable. **That last one is free.**
