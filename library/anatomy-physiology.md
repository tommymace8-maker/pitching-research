# Anatomy and Physiology of the Throwing Body
### Foundational reference — Day 1 of the daily research program
### Population: elite throwers, 85 MPH floor

**Compiled:** 2026-08-12
**Audience:** pitching coach working with high-output arms (non-clinician)
**Scope:** structural anatomy of the throwing kinetic chain, the physiology of a high-intent pitch and a high-intent outing, the adapted tissue state of the mature elite thrower, injury mechanism at tissue tolerance, and between-outing recovery physiology.

---

## POPULATION SCOPE (read this first; it governs everything below)

**This document is written for one population and one only: the elite thrower at or above 85 MPH.**

Concretely, that means:
- **Top high-school prospect** — Perfect Game / showcase-circuit caliber, D1-committed or draft-followed.
- **College pitcher** — D1 and equivalent.
- **Professional** — MiLB and MLB.
- **Hard floor: 85 MPH.** The athlete this model serves already throws 85+ and is trying to reach 90–95+.

**What this changes about the anatomy and physiology:**

1. **This is not a developing arm. It is an adapted, high-output arm operating near tissue tolerance.** Humeral retrotorsion is already laid down and locked. Capsular and rotator-cuff remodeling has already occurred. The UCL already carries a load history measured in tens of thousands of high-torque repetitions. Growth plates are closed or closing. Nothing in this document treats the arm as a blank structure.
2. **Baseline "abnormal" is the norm here.** Asymptomatic elite pitchers carry imaging findings that would be called pathology in anyone else (Section 4.6). Interpreting an elite arm against general-population norms produces false alarms in both directions.
3. **Velocity is both the goal and the primary load driver.** At 85+, the athlete is already generating elbow varus torque in the range where the isolated UCL cannot survive unassisted. Every additional MPH raises that load. This is the central tension of the entire program.
4. **Growth-plate pathology has been removed.** Little League shoulder (proximal humeral epiphysiolysis), medial epicondyle apophysitis, and youth apophyseal injury are **out of scope** — they are conditions of an open physis and do not describe this population. (Capitellar OCD is retained only as *history* to ask about, since a healed adolescent lesion can still constrain the mature elbow.)
5. **Sample-matching is enforced in the citations.** Studies on youth or unselected high-school pitchers are either dropped or explicitly flagged **⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH — directionally suggestive only.** Priority goes to MLB, MiLB, collegiate, and elite/showcase HS cohorts.

**The most useful thing in this document is the explicit contrast between the elite thrower and the general population.** Wherever that contrast exists and is documented, it is called out with **▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC**.

---

> **EVIDENCE LABELS USED THROUGHOUT**
>
> - **[ESTABLISHED]** — Replicated across multiple independent studies, or basic anatomy/physiology not seriously disputed. Coach off this.
> - **[EMERGING]** — Real data exists but is limited, conflicting, single-lab, or the effect size is unsettled. Hold loosely.
> - **[FOLKLORE]** — Widely repeated in baseball, not supported (or actively contradicted). Stop saying it.
> - **⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH** — The underlying study population is not our population. Directional only.
> - **▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC** — This is where the elite thrower differs meaningfully from the general athlete.

> **SCOPE-OF-PRACTICE NOTICE.** Educational only — not diagnosis, treatment, or medical advice. Anything marked **⚠ REFER** requires a physician, physical therapist, or athletic trainer. At the point of a red flag, the coach's job is to stop the throwing and route the athlete.

## 1. Organizing Principles for the High-Output Arm

**1.1 The delivery is a proximal-to-distal sequence, not a set of positions. [ESTABLISHED]**
Pelvis accelerates and decelerates, then thorax, then upper arm, then forearm, then hand. Each proximal segment's *deceleration* transfers angular momentum distally. Shoulder internal rotation angular velocity at release is on the order of 7,000–7,500 °/s in adult pitchers — the fastest measured human joint motion (Fleisig et al., 1995/1999).

**1.2 The arm is the last, smallest, and most fragile link — and at 85+ it is already the binding constraint. [ESTABLISHED]**
Ligaments do not hypertrophy the way muscle does. Everything upstream exists to reduce demand on tissue whose strength ceiling is essentially fixed. You cannot strengthen your way past a bad sequence, and at this velocity band you cannot outlast a bad one either.

**1.3 ▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: At 85+, the load already exceeds what the passive structure can carry alone. [ESTABLISHED]**
Peak elbow varus torque in adult pitchers is approximately **100 N·m** (Fleisig et al., 1995). Modeling attributes roughly one third each to the UCL, the surrounding musculature, and the bony articulation. The isolated UCL's ultimate tensile strength in cadaveric testing is roughly an order of magnitude below the whole-joint load. **The ligament survives only because the flexor-pronator mass and the radiocapitellar articulation shield it, every pitch.** That is the single most important sentence in this document for an 85+ arm.

**1.4 Velocity is the most consistent kinematic correlate of elbow varus torque. [ESTABLISHED]**
Whether mechanics can meaningfully decouple the two *within an individual* is **[EMERGING]** — recent ASMI kinematic work identifies contributing parameters (arm path, shoulder abduction, trunk position) with modest effect sizes relative to the velocity effect. Do not sell an athlete "free velocity."

**1.5 ▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: Adaptation and pathology are radiographically indistinguishable in this population. [ESTABLISHED]**
Asymptomatic professional pitchers show labral abnormality in ~79% of throwing shoulders (Miniaci et al./Connor et al. lines of work), and asymptomatic pro draft picks show frequent cuff, capsuloligamentous, labral, and osseous findings on 3-T MRI. Elbow MRI findings in asymptomatic professional pitchers **do not correlate with future injury-list placement** (Wilcox et al., 2017). **Imaging findings without symptoms are not injuries.** This cuts both ways: don't panic at a scan, and don't use a clean scan to override a symptomatic athlete.

**1.6 Total high-intent throwing volume is the exposure variable, not game pitch count. [ESTABLISHED]**
Bullpens, flat-ground, long toss, showcase outings, pre-draft workouts, second-team appearances, and velocity-testing days all load the same tissue. Game-day pitch counts systematically undercount the true workload of pitchers (Zaremski et al.).

## 2. The Kinetic Chain, Ground Up

Phase vocabulary (ASMI convention): **wind-up → stride (early cocking) → arm cocking (late cocking, to maximal shoulder external rotation, "MER") → arm acceleration (MER to ball release) → arm deceleration → follow-through.**

---

### 2.1 Foot and Ankle

| Structure | Role in the delivery |
|---|---|
| Gastrocnemius (medial/lateral heads) + soleus, via the Achilles | Drive-leg plantarflexion; terminal push off the rubber. |
| Tibialis posterior | Eccentric control of pronation on both drive and landing foot; supports the medial longitudinal arch. |
| Peroneus longus / brevis | Eversion control; lateral ankle stability at a hard front-foot strike. |
| Talocrural joint | Dorsiflexion range at landing. Restriction forces compensation upstream. |
| Subtalar joint | Pronation/supination — shock absorption then re-stiffening. |
| First metatarsophalangeal joint | Terminal drive-leg extension; last point of ground contact. |

**In throwing.** Drive-leg plantarflexors contribute to the propulsive horizontal ground reaction force (GRF). At front-foot strike the lead ankle must accept and immediately stiffen against a large braking GRF. Peak drive-leg propulsive GRF correlates with energy flow into proximal segments; peak lead-leg braking GRF correlates with energy flow into distal segments — i.e., into the arm (Howenstein et al., 2020, *J Biomech*). **[ESTABLISHED]** ⚑ *youth sample in that specific paper; the energy-flow principle is replicated in collegiate/professional GRF work.*

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Limited lead-ankle dorsiflexion* → lead tibia cannot progress over the foot; athlete lands short, spins open early, or the knee collapses into valgus. **[EMERGING]** as a pitching-specific risk factor.
- *Weak tibialis posterior / arch collapse on the drive leg* → the drive leg "melts" instead of pushing; measurable propulsive GRF loss.
- *Chronic lateral ankle instability (attenuated ATFL from old sprains)* → hesitant landing, early rotation.
- *Achilles or plantar fascia pain* → changes stride length before the athlete reports it.

**Coach can see.** Stride-length inconsistency across an outing; lead foot heel-striking then slapping down; drive-leg heel spinning off the rubber before the hip fires.

### 2.2 Knee and the Lead-Leg Block

**Structures.** Tibiofemoral joint; quadriceps (rectus femoris + three vasti) via the patellar tendon; hamstrings (biceps femoris long/short head, semitendinosus, semimembranosus); collateral and cruciate ligaments as passive limits.

**In throwing — the highest-leverage lower-body event.** After front-foot strike the lead knee is rapidly eccentrically loaded and then, in higher-velocity pitchers, **extends**, converting linear momentum into rotation of the pelvis over a stiffening front leg. Higher-velocity pitchers show more lead-knee extension after foot plant (Driveline R&D, 2022 lead-leg block analysis; concordant with published GRF/energy-flow work). **[ESTABLISHED]** that lead-leg braking force associates with velocity; **[EMERGING]** on the optimal individual knee-extension pattern.

Mechanism: a stiff lead leg creates a rotational impulse about the lead hip. A continuously flexing lead knee absorbs that energy into the quadriceps instead of passing it up. Stride-leg GRF during arm cocking and acceleration explains a large share of the variance in distal-segment (wrist) velocity.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC.** At 85+, the block is usually already present in some form. The question stops being "does he block?" and becomes "does the block *hold* at pitch 75, and does it hold on his highest-intent pitch?" Block quality is one of the first things to degrade under fatigue, and when it degrades, the arm makes up the difference.

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Insufficient eccentric quad strength / rate of force development* → knee keeps flexing through release; velocity lost, arm compensates.
- *Hamstring strain history* → guarded hip extension, shortened stride, altered sequence. Hamstring injuries in this population are common and recurrent.
- *Patellar tendinopathy on the lead leg* → high-volume hard-blocking pitchers. **⚠ REFER** for morning stiffness plus loading pain that doesn't warm up.

**Coach can see.** From the side: does the lead knee angle *increase* (extend) between foot strike and release, or keep closing? Does the head travel over the front leg and stop, or drop into it?

### 2.3 Hip Complex

| Muscle / structure | Action | Role in throwing |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluteus maximus** | Hip extension, external rotation; upper fibers abduct | Drive-leg propulsion; lead-leg hip extension and deceleration at the block. |
| **Gluteus medius** (anterior/middle/posterior fibers) | Abduction; anterior fibers IR, posterior fibers ER | Frontal-plane pelvic control in single-leg stance — level pelvis at leg lift and at landing. |
| **Gluteus minimus** | Abduction, IR | Femoral head centering in the acetabulum. |
| **Deep external rotators**: piriformis, obturator internus/externus, gemellus superior/inferior, quadratus femoris | ER, femoral head stabilization | The hip's "rotator cuff." Control drive-leg ER; eccentrically brake lead-hip internal rotation as the pelvis rotates over a planted femur. |
| **Adductor group**: adductor magnus (adductor + hamstring portions), longus, brevis, gracilis, pectineus | Adduction; magnus is a powerful hip extensor | Drive-leg adduction into the stride; lead adductors pull the body over the front leg. Adductor magnus is the most under-trained hip extensor in most pitchers. |
| **Iliopsoas** (psoas major + iliacus) | Hip flexion; psoas attaches to T12–L5 vertebral bodies and discs | Leg lift; direct lumbar coupling. High psoas tone alters lumbar lordosis and pelvic tilt. |
| **Tensor fasciae latae → iliotibial band** | Flexion/abduction/IR | Compensatory driver when gluteus medius is insufficient. |

**Hip rotation in the delivery.** The drive hip produces and then permits rotation; the **lead hip must accept a rapid internal-rotation moment** as the pelvis rotates over a planted femur. Lead-hip IR range is the most-discussed limiter of pelvic rotation. Associations between hip ROM deficits and arm injury in pitchers are **[EMERGING]** — plausible, several supporting cohorts, no validated screening threshold.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) morphology.** Cam and pincer morphology is over-represented in baseball players relative to the general population, plausibly from adolescent loading during physeal development. **[ESTABLISHED]** that the morphology is more prevalent; **[EMERGING]** on its relationship to throwing injury. Practically: an elite pitcher with hard-end-feel hip IR restriction may have a bony block, not a soft-tissue one — the same interpretive problem as osseous GIRD at the shoulder (Section 4.2). Stretching a bony block accomplishes nothing.

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Weak gluteus medius* → contralateral pelvic drop at leg lift and again at landing; the athlete falls toward the arm side; trunk laterally flexes to compensate, raising effective shoulder abduction and shoulder distraction demand. **[EMERGING]** link to arm load.
- *Restricted lead-hip IR* → the pelvis cannot rotate over the front leg, so rotation is stolen from the lumbar spine (poorly built for it — 2.5) or the thorax opens early.
- *Iliopsoas over-activity* → anterior pelvic tilt → altered rib-cage position → altered scapular resting position.
- *Deep groin/anterior hip pain with rotation, or adductor/pubic symphysis region pain (athletic pubalgia)* → **⚠ REFER**.

**Coach can see.** Pelvis level at peak leg lift; whether the belt buckle rotates before or after the chest; whether the drive hip extends or the pitcher simply falls forward; a lead foot landing closed (blocking hip IR) or open (leaking rotation early).

### 2.4 Pelvis

**Structures.** Ilium, ischium, pubis; sacroiliac joints (stabilized by the posterior sacroiliac, sacrotuberous, and sacrospinous ligaments); pubic symphysis.

**In throwing.** The base segment of the axial sequence; its rotational velocity peaks first. It transmits force through the SI joints, which are built for **force closure** — stability generated by muscular compression across the joint (gluteus maximus, contralateral latissimus dorsi via the thoracolumbar fascia, deep abdominal wall) rather than by motion. **[ESTABLISHED]**

**Dysfunction.** SI joint pain, pubic symphysis stress, and lower abdominal/adductor pain in a high-volume thrower are **⚠ REFER**. Pelvic control failures rarely present as pelvic pain — they present as timing failures above and below.

### 2.5 Lumbopelvic Core

| Structure | Orientation | Function in throwing |
|---|---|---|
| **External oblique** | Fibers run inferomedially | Contralateral trunk rotation + ipsilateral side bend. The *lead-side* EO is a primary trunk rotator into the throw. |
| **Internal oblique** | Superomedially, perpendicular to EO | Ipsilateral rotation. EO of one side + IO of the other = the rotational force couple. |
| **Transversus abdominis** | Horizontal fibers | Circumferential compression; raises intra-abdominal pressure and tensions the thoracolumbar fascia. |
| **Rectus abdominis** | Vertical | Trunk flexion; anterior chain during trunk closing in acceleration. |
| **Quadratus lumborum** | Iliac crest → 12th rib and L1–L4 transverse processes | Lateral flexion; 12th-rib stabilization for the diaphragm; frontal-plane lumbar control. Heavily involved in the contralateral trunk tilt of the delivery. |
| **Multifidus** | Deep, segmental | Segmental lumbar control. |
| **Thoracolumbar fascia (TLF)** | Multi-layered aponeurosis | Mechanical junction of latissimus dorsi, contralateral gluteus maximus, TVA, internal oblique — the posterior oblique sling. |
| **Diaphragm** | Domed, central tendon | Postural as well as respiratory; co-contracts with TVA and pelvic floor for intra-abdominal pressure. |

**In throwing.** Trunk rotation contributes substantially to ball velocity, and trunk *position* at release — forward flexion and contralateral lateral tilt — strongly influences shoulder and elbow kinetics. Two jobs: **produce** rotation and **transmit** without leaking.

**Lumbar rotation is anatomically limited. [ESTABLISHED]** Lumbar facets are sagittally oriented, permitting roughly 2° of axial rotation per segment (~10–13° total). Rotation is supposed to come from the hips and thoracic spine. When either is restricted, the lumbar spine makes up the difference — a mechanism for facet irritation, pars stress, and disc loading.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: the oblique strain.** This is a signature injury of professional and collegiate baseball, disproportionately affecting pitchers and disproportionately the **lead/non-throwing side** — the side that lengthens eccentrically at MER and then contracts violently. Time loss is measured in weeks and re-injury rates are meaningful. **⚠ REFER** for any sudden sharp lateral trunk pain, especially with a reported "pop." **[ESTABLISHED]**

**Other failures.**
- *Poor lumbopelvic control* is associated with altered upper-extremity joint moments and drive-leg GRF (IJSPT, 2025) ⚑ *adolescent sample — directionally suggestive*. **[EMERGING]**
- *QL over-reliance* → excessive lateral trunk tilt → higher effective shoulder abduction → more shoulder load.
- *Pars interarticularis stress (spondylolysis)* → extension-and-rotation low back pain. **⚠ REFER**.

**Coach can see.** Excessive contralateral trunk tilt at release; chest rotating open at or before front-foot strike; hip-shoulder separation present at pitch 10 and gone at pitch 70; ribs flared and lumbar spine arched at MER.

### 2.6 Thoracic Spine and Rib Cage

**Structures.** T1–T12 with frontal/oblique-plane facets (rotation-permissive); costovertebral and costotransverse joints; ribs 1–12; sternum; intercostals; erector spinae (iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis); **latissimus dorsi** (T7–T12 spinous processes, TLF, iliac crest, lower ribs, inferior angle of scapula → intertubercular groove of the humerus); **teres major**.

**In throwing.** The thoracic spine supplies most of the delivery's axial rotation and the extension needed to get the arm up. Rib-cage position dictates scapular resting position: a depressed, flexed thorax puts the scapula in anterior tilt and protraction, reducing available glenohumeral elevation and narrowing clearance. **[ESTABLISHED]**

**Latissimus dorsi deserves its own note.** It is a powerful **shoulder internal rotator, extensor, and adductor** and a major contributor to arm acceleration — and it is a direct mechanical link from the humerus to the contralateral gluteal region through the TLF. A tight lat restricts overhead flexion *and* external rotation and drags the thorax into extension/side-bend to let the arm up.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: latissimus dorsi / teres major strain** is a recognized, high-time-loss professional pitcher injury with a meaningful re-injury rate, essentially absent from general-population sports medicine. It presents as posterior axillary or lateral chest-wall pain, often with abrupt velocity loss. **⚠ REFER.** **[ESTABLISHED]**

**Other failures.**
- *Thoracic extension/rotation restriction* → compensatory lumbar rotation, early trunk opening, or excessive glenohumeral ER demand. **[EMERGING]** as a measured risk factor; **[ESTABLISHED]** as a mechanical constraint.
- *First rib position* — see thoracic outlet, Section 5.5.

**Coach can see.** Poor overhead reach in warm-up; needing lumbar extension to raise the arms; asymmetric seated rotation.

### 2.7 Scapulothoracic Complex

The scapula floats on the thorax, bonily anchored only through the clavicle via the acromioclavicular and sternoclavicular joints.

| Muscle | Attachment / line of pull | Role in throwing |
|---|---|---|
| **Serratus anterior** | Ribs 1–9 → entire medial border of the scapula, anterior surface | The single most important scapular muscle for throwing. Produces **upward rotation, posterior tilt, protraction**; holds the medial border flat. Failure = medial border/inferior angle winging. |
| **Lower trapezius** | T6–T12 spinous processes → medial acromion/scapular spine | Upward rotation, posterior tilt, depression. Chronically inhibited in throwers. |
| **Middle trapezius** | T1–T5 → acromion and scapular spine | Retraction; resists protraction forces. |
| **Upper trapezius** | Occiput/nuchal ligament/C7 → lateral clavicle | Clavicular elevation; part of the upward-rotation couple. Often *over*-dominant. |
| **Rhomboid major/minor** | C7–T5 → medial border | Retraction and **downward** rotation. Overuse pulls the scapula into downward rotation. |
| **Levator scapulae** | C1–C4 transverse processes → superior medial angle | Elevation + downward rotation. Frequent neck/scapular pain source. |
| **Pectoralis minor** | Ribs 3–5 → coracoid process | **Anterior tilt, protraction, downward rotation.** The classic short structure; also a wall of the retropectoralis minor space (thoracic outlet). |
| **Pectoralis major** (clavicular/sternal heads) | Clavicle, sternum, ribs → lateral lip of intertubercular groove | Powerful internal rotator/adductor in acceleration. |

**The upward-rotation force couple. [ESTABLISHED]** Upper trapezius + lower trapezius + serratus anterior rotate the glenoid upward as the arm elevates. Roughly one-third of total arm elevation is scapulothoracic upward rotation (scapulohumeral rhythm ~2:1 GH:ST beyond the setting phase). Without upward rotation and posterior tilt, the humerus runs out of room and the cuff and labrum absorb the difference.

**In the delivery.** During arm cocking the scapula must retract, upwardly rotate, and posteriorly tilt to present the glenoid to the abducted, maximally externally rotated humerus. During deceleration the retractors and serratus resist an enormous distraction force.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: periscapular strength declines measurably across a competitive season.** In collegiate pitchers tracked pre-, mid-, and post-season, all measured strength values declined (range 3–14%), with scaption strength down a statistically significant ~8% over the season, including a 5% drop from mid- to post-season (IJSPT, 2024, collegiate cohort). **[ESTABLISHED for collegiate pitchers]** The practical read: **scapular capacity is a depleting resource across a season, not a fixed trait.** In-season maintenance work is not optional for an 85+ arm.

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Serratus anterior weakness or long thoracic nerve involvement* → medial border winging, lost protraction power, deceleration overload. **⚠ REFER** for true winging.
- *Lower trapezius inhibition + short pectoralis minor* → the anteriorly tilted, protracted scapula; reduced clearance; also narrows the retropectoralis minor space.
- *Scapular dyskinesis* — see 4.4 for the crucial nuance about what it does and does not predict.

**Coach can see.** From behind: does the medial border stay flat during a slow arm raise, and more diagnostically during the *lowering* phase? **Note: the dominant scapula in throwers normally sits slightly lower and more protracted — asymmetry alone is not pathology. [ESTABLISHED]**

### 2.8 Glenohumeral Joint

Least bony constraint, greatest mobility in the body; roughly one-third of the humeral head contacts the glenoid at any instant.

#### Static (passive) stabilizers

| Structure | Anatomy | Function in throwing |
|---|---|---|
| **Glenoid labrum** | Fibrocartilaginous rim; deepens the socket ~50%; the long head of biceps blends into the superior labrum | Concavity-compression; anchor for the glenohumeral ligaments and the biceps root. |
| **Superior glenohumeral ligament (SGHL)** | With the coracohumeral ligament, forms the biceps pulley | Restrains inferior translation in adduction. |
| **Middle glenohumeral ligament (MGHL)** | Anterior | Restrains anterior translation at mid-range abduction (~45°). |
| **Inferior glenohumeral ligament complex (IGHL)** — **anterior band, axillary pouch, posterior band** | The critical structure for throwers | At 90° abduction + maximal ER (the MER position) the **anterior band** is the primary restraint to anterior translation; the **posterior band** tightens in abduction + IR. Functions as a hammock that shifts reciprocally with rotation. |
| **Posteroinferior capsule** | Posterior/inferior capsular tissue | Thickens with repetitive throwing; a soft-tissue contributor to IR loss. |
| **Long head of biceps brachii (LHB)** | Supraglenoid tubercle + superior labrum → intertubercular groove under the transverse humeral ligament and biceps pulley | Depresses/centers the humeral head; heavily loaded in deceleration; torsional load on its anchor drives the SLAP peel-back mechanism. |

#### Dynamic stabilizers — the rotator cuff

| Muscle | Origin → Insertion | Innervation | Action | Throwing role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supraspinatus** | Supraspinous fossa → superior facet, greater tubercle | Suprascapular n. (C5–6) | Abduction initiation; head compression | Head centering throughout; loaded in deceleration. |
| **Infraspinatus** | Infraspinous fossa → middle facet, greater tubercle | Suprascapular n. (C5–6) | External rotation (~60% of ER strength); posterior head stability | Decelerates internal rotation after release — an enormous eccentric job. **Most fatigue- and inhibition-susceptible cuff muscle (3.2).** |
| **Teres minor** | Lateral border of scapula → inferior facet, greater tubercle | Axillary n. (C5–6) | ER; inferior head stability | Deceleration partner to infraspinatus; resists superior migration. |
| **Subscapularis** | Subscapular fossa → lesser tubercle | Upper/lower subscapular nn. (C5–6) | Internal rotation; **anterior head restraint** | The anterior dynamic checkrein at MER, protecting the anterior band of the IGHL. Also contributes to acceleration. |

**Concavity-compression [ESTABLISHED].** The cuff's primary stabilizing mechanism is compressing the convex head into the labrum-deepened concave glenoid — not pulling in a direction. **Cuff balance** (anterior vs. posterior; cuff vs. deltoid) matters more than cuff maximum strength.

**Loading. [ESTABLISHED]** Glenohumeral distraction force during deceleration approaches or exceeds body weight; peak internal rotation torque and horizontal adduction torque occur in arm cocking (Fleisig et al., 1995).

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Infraspinatus/teres minor weakness or endurance failure* → lost deceleration capacity, superior head migration, internal impingement exposure, "arm drag."
- *Subscapularis insufficiency* → anterior translation at MER → strain on the anterior band of the IGHL → the anterior microinstability / internal impingement / SLAP cascade.
- *Posterior capsule stiffness* → obligate anterosuperior head translation in flexion/adduction/IR (cadaveric mechanism **[ESTABLISHED]**; the in-vivo causal chain in living elite throwers **[EMERGING]**).
- *Biceps pulley / LHB pathology* → anterior shoulder pain, catching, pain on resisted elevation. **⚠ REFER**.

**Coach can see.** Loss of arm slot late in outings; the arm "dropping"; discomfort during follow-through (deceleration problem) versus at MER (cocking problem). **Where in the delivery it hurts is diagnostic information — always ask.**

### 2.9 Elbow

#### Ulnar collateral ligament (medial collateral ligament) complex

| Bundle | Attachments | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior bundle** | Anteroinferior medial epicondyle → sublime tubercle of the coronoid. Functionally split into **anterior and posterior bands** that tighten reciprocally through the flexion arc. | **The primary valgus restraint from ~20° to ~120° flexion — the entire throwing range.** This is what fails in a "UCL tear." |
| **Posterior bundle** | Medial epicondyle → olecranon, fan-shaped | Secondary restraint, tightens beyond ~90–120°. Its contracture participates in valgus extension overload. |
| **Transverse (oblique/Cooper's) ligament** | Olecranon → coronoid, both on the ulna | Crosses no joint; contributes little or nothing to valgus stability. |

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC — the core mechanical fact. [ESTABLISHED]** Peak valgus load during adult pitching (~100 N·m typical) far exceeds the ultimate tensile strength of the isolated UCL measured in cadaveric testing. The ligament survives only through load-sharing with the flexor-pronator mass and the radiocapitellar/osseous articulation — roughly a third each by modeling. **At 85+ this is not a theoretical margin; it is the operating condition of every pitch.** This is the entire physiological justification for arm care in this population.

#### Flexor-pronator mass

Origin: the **common flexor tendon on the medial epicondyle**, lying directly superficial to the anterior bundle of the UCL.

| Muscle | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **Flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU)** | Sits directly over the anterior UCL at peak valgus load; the **primary dynamic valgus stabilizer**. |
| **Flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS)** | Secondary dynamic stabilizer. The index and middle finger slips specifically reduce medial joint gapping under valgus stress — **grip pressure literally narrows the medial elbow under load** (ultrasound and shear-wave elastography evidence). |
| **Pronator teres** | Contributes to valgus resistance and to forearm pronation in acceleration/release. |
| **Flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus** | Contributory; palmaris longus is the classic graft donor (absent in ~10–15% of people). |

**[ESTABLISHED]:** FCU and FDS are dynamic valgus stabilizers with EMG activity rising from cocking through acceleration; medial elbow musculature provides measurable **stress shielding** of the UCL in competitive pitchers (*MSSE* varus-strength work).
**[EMERGING]:** whether targeted flexor-pronator training prospectively reduces UCL injury *incidence*. Mechanism solid; outcome trial absent.

#### Ulnar nerve

Course: medial cord of the brachial plexus → medial arm → **posterior to the medial epicondyle through the cubital tunnel** (roofed by Osborne's ligament / the arcuate ligament between the FCU heads) → between the FCU heads into the forearm.

Elbow flexion elongates the nerve and raises cubital tunnel pressure; shoulder abduction adds traction; **medial elbow laxity increases traction further** — which is precisely why ulnar neuritis and UCL insufficiency co-travel in elite throwers. Ulnar nerve instability (subluxation over the epicondyle) has been linked to the degree of UCL laxity in high-level pitchers. **[ESTABLISHED]**

#### Posterior and lateral elbow

- **Valgus extension overload:** in terminal extension under valgus load, the **posteromedial olecranon tip** is levered against the medial wall of the olecranon fossa → chondral wear and posteromedial osteophytes. **▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC:** subclinical posteromedial impingement changes are demonstrable in *asymptomatic* professional players (Kooima et al., 2004).
- **Olecranon stress reaction/fracture:** repetitive traction and torsion; a real and under-recognized entity in the high-velocity arm.
- **Radiocapitellar joint:** absorbs compression under valgus. Capitellar osteochondritis dissecans is an adolescent lesion — in this population it matters as *history*, since a healed lesion can leave a mechanically compromised lateral column.

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Loss of elbow extension* of a few degrees is common and often adaptive in this population; a **new, progressive** loss with posterior pain is a flag.
- *Medial elbow pain at MER/acceleration* → UCL region until proven otherwise. **⚠ REFER**.
- *Posterior elbow pain at release/extension* → valgus extension overload or olecranon stress. **⚠ REFER**.
- *Ring/small finger numbness or tingling* → ulnar nerve. **⚠ REFER**.

### 2.10 Forearm, Wrist, Hand

**Structures.** Pronator teres and pronator quadratus (pronation); supinator and biceps brachii (supination); wrist flexor and extensor groups; distal radioulnar joint and the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC); extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) and its subsheath; intrinsic hand muscles; digital flexors.

**In throwing.** Rapid forearm pronation through and after release; wrist flexion contributes the last increment of ball speed; finger pressure determines spin axis and spin efficiency. Grip force is not incidental — FDS contraction from gripping measurably stiffens the medial elbow (above).

**Weak / tight / injured.**
- *Grip and forearm endurance loss late in outings* plausibly reduces FDS/FCU stress-shielding of the UCL exactly when it is most needed. **[EMERGING]** — mechanistically strong, not demonstrated prospectively. This is one of the better untested hypotheses in the field.
- *ECU subsheath injury / ulnar-sided wrist pain* → **⚠ REFER**.
- *Digital artery thrombosis / vascular finger* in pitchers — uncommon but real; blanching or cold finger, loss of feel. **⚠ REFER**.
- *Blisters and nail issues* → change grip pressure and therefore mechanics. Not trivial at this level.

## 3. The Physiology of a High-Intent Pitch and Outing

### 3.1 Energy system profile

A pitch takes ~1.5–2 s from first movement to follow-through; the high-velocity portion (front-foot strike to release) lasts on the order of **0.05–0.15 s**. Between pitches: ~15–25 s. Between innings: several minutes.

| System | Substrate | Dominance window | Role in pitching |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phosphagen (ATP-PCr, "alactic")** | Stored ATP + phosphocreatine | ~0–10 s | **Dominant for the pitch itself.** **[ESTABLISHED]** |
| **Fast glycolysis** | Muscle glycogen → pyruvate/lactate | ~10 s–2 min | Minor. Pitching does not produce high blood lactate. **[ESTABLISHED]** |
| **Oxidative (aerobic)** | Fat, carbohydrate, O₂ | Continuous; dominant at rest and in recovery | **Governs PCr resynthesis between pitches, innings, and outings.** **[ESTABLISHED]** |

**The key insight. [ESTABLISHED]** PCr resynthesis is an oxygen-dependent, mitochondrial process: fast phase (~half of PCr) in ~20–30 s, near-complete in ~3–5 min. The aerobic system is therefore not "conditioning for a sport that doesn't need it" — **it is the recovery engine that refills the tank between high-intent efforts.** A pitcher with better oxidative capacity in the working musculature restores PCr faster between pitches and repeats high-output efforts with less decay.

**[FOLKLORE]** "Distance running builds endurance for starts." The aerobic *demand* of a start is low; the aerobic *recovery* function matters, but there is no evidence that high-volume slow running develops it best in pitchers, and it competes with power adaptations (concurrent-training interference). **[EMERGING]** that cardiac-output or repeat-sprint methods serve this population better. Practice has outrun published evidence here.

**[FOLKLORE]** "Poles the day after flush lactic acid." There is essentially no lactate to flush after pitching, and lactate is a fuel, not a waste product.

### 3.2 Fatigue mechanisms across an outing

Four distinct mechanisms, all operating:

1. **Peripheral metabolic fatigue [ESTABLISHED].** Local PCr depletion with incomplete pitch-to-pitch resynthesis, plus glycogen depletion in the relevant fibers across 90–110 pitches.
2. **▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC — central inhibition of the infraspinatus [ESTABLISHED].** After a simulated game, pitchers show a **voluntary activation deficit of the infraspinatus** measured by interpolated twitch — the nervous system cannot fully recruit the muscle, independent of the muscle's own condition. **The primary deceleration muscle is neurally inhibited exactly when the arm most needs it.** This is one of the single most important findings in the pitching literature and it is specific to pitching-induced fatigue.
3. **Contractile strength loss [ESTABLISHED].** Post-game measurement in asymptomatic pitchers shows drops on the order of ~11% in external rotation, ~15% in flexion, ~18% in internal rotation, ~11% in adduction versus pre-game — after a *single* game.
4. **Kinematic drift [ESTABLISHED].** With fatigue, measured kinematics change (reduced hip-shoulder separation, altered trunk position, lower arm slot) and — critically — **joint kinetics do not fall proportionally with velocity.** Load per unit of output rises.

**Range of motion consequences [ESTABLISHED].** Significant acute decreases in shoulder internal rotation, total rotational motion, and elbow extension occur immediately after pitching and **persist at 24 hours** post-outing. In collegiate starting pitchers, ROM changes are documented both after a single game and across a season (Reuter et al./collegiate ROM work). **The arm is measurably not back to baseline a day later.** That is the physiological basis for a structured between-start map.

### 3.3 What actually happens over ~100 high-intent pitches

- **1–25:** PCr near-full each pitch; kinematics stable; cuff activation intact.
- **25–60:** Incremental strength and activation loss begins; the athlete compensates with intent; velocity often holds. **This is the deceptive window — output is preserved by increased effort, not preserved capacity.**
- **60–90:** Posterior cuff voluntary activation drops; hip-shoulder separation narrows; command degrades before velocity; kinetics per pitch stay high or rise relative to velocity.
- **90+:** The passive restraints — UCL, labrum, capsule — absorb a larger share of load because their dynamic partners are both weakened and inhibited.

**[EMERGING]** individual thresholds. **[ESTABLISHED]** the general pattern and its cumulative seasonal compounding (see the collegiate periscapular strength decline in 2.7).

**Coach-observable fatigue signs, in the order they appear:** command drift (arm-side misses, elevated fastballs) → loss of breaking-ball depth/finish → visible slot drop → longer time between pitches → **velocity decline last.** **Velocity is a late indicator. Do not wait for it.** **[EMERGING]** as a formal hierarchy; **[ESTABLISHED]** that command and secondary-pitch quality degrade before radar readings.

### 3.4 The high-intent workload problem: pens, showcases, and testing days

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC.** This population's exposure is not the game. It is the game *plus* bullpens at intent, flat-ground, long toss, velocity-testing days, pro days, showcase outings in front of scouts, and — in the amateur half of this population — multiple organizations each believing they own one appearance.

**[ESTABLISHED]** Game-day pitch counts systematically undercount true workload in high-school and collegiate pitchers (Zaremski et al., *AJSM* 2024, HS workload risk factors; UF unaccounted-workload work). **[EMERGING]** Acute:chronic workload ratio approaches applied to pitching: elevated risk above roughly ACWR 1.27–1.5, with the additional and under-appreciated finding that **low chronic workload is itself a risk state** — the underprepared arm suddenly exposed to high intent. ACWR methodology is genuinely contested in the sports-science literature (see the soccer critiques in the References), so treat thresholds as heuristics, not laws.

**The showcase-specific hazard.** A maximal-intent outing performed cold, off a schedule, in front of evaluators, with no prior chronic load, is the near-perfect adverse combination: high acute load, low chronic load, maximal intent, minimal warm-up, and strong incentive to hide fatigue. **[EMERGING as measured; ESTABLISHED as mechanism]**

### 3.5 "Dead arm" in the elite thrower

"Dead arm" is a symptom description, not a diagnosis, covering at least three physiologically distinct states. This distinction is high-value.

**(a) Physiologic dead arm / accumulated neuromuscular fatigue [ESTABLISHED as phenomenon; EMERGING as defined entity].**
Voluntary activation deficits (3.2), residual strength loss, unresolved ROM deficits from prior outings, incomplete tissue recovery. The arm feels heavy and velocity is down, but there is **no localized pain, no night pain, no neurological symptom**, and ROM screening is symmetric-to-baseline without a hard or painful end-feel. Resolves with load reduction and sleep over days.

**(b) Dead arm as the presenting complaint of structural pathology [ESTABLISHED].**
Classically the **anterior microinstability → internal impingement → labral/cuff** cascade. The athlete reports not pain but *velocity and command loss and an arm that won't work*, sometimes a clunk at MER, sometimes brief posterior shoulder pain in late cocking. **⚠ REFER.**

**(c) Neurovascular dead arm [ESTABLISHED].**
Thoracic outlet syndrome, quadrilateral space syndrome, suprascapular neuropathy. Hallmarks: heaviness plus **any** paresthesia, coldness, color change, arm swelling, rapid fatigability out of proportion to work, or ER strength loss with visible infraspinatus atrophy. **⚠ REFER — this category is routinely missed for months in professional pitchers.**

**Decision rule for the coach.** Dead arm *without pain or neuro symptoms*, resolving with 2–5 days of reduced load and good sleep, is (a). Dead arm that **persists past a normal recovery window, recurs on the same schedule, or carries any pain, paresthesia, night symptoms, swelling, or visible atrophy** is not a training problem and must be evaluated.

## 4. The Adapted Tissue State of the Elite Thrower

The central skill: distinguishing adaptation from pathology in a body where the adaptation *looks like* pathology.

### 4.1 Humeral retrotorsion — already locked in

**What it is [ESTABLISHED].** The humeral shaft torsionally remodels during skeletal growth under repetitive high-torque throwing, rotating the humeral head backward relative to the distal epicondylar axis. Dominant-arm retrotorsion in throwers commonly exceeds the non-dominant side by roughly 10–15°.

**Why it matters.** Retrotorsion shifts the **entire rotational arc posteriorly**: the arm gains external rotation and loses internal rotation with **no soft-tissue pathology whatsoever.** Professional pitchers with GIRD show greater humeral retrotorsion than those without (Ruotolo/Crockett-line work, PMC3763355). **A large share of measured "GIRD" in a professional pitcher is bone.** **[ESTABLISHED]**

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC timing.** Torsional remodeling occurs while the proximal humeral physis is open — roughly ages 8–16, greatest in early adolescence — and is **essentially locked at skeletal maturity.** For our population this is a completed, non-modifiable fact. You are managing an arc that was set years ago. Attempting to stretch a 23-year-old's internal rotation toward a normative number is attempting to remodel bone with a towel, and the tissue that actually yields is the anterior capsule you did not want to lengthen.

**Handedness note [ESTABLISHED, professional cohort].** Right- and left-handed professional pitchers differ measurably in glenohumeral ROM and humeral torsion (Harris et al., 2022, *AJSM*) — one more reason population norms are a poor individual reference.

**[FOLKLORE]** "GIRD means he needs aggressive posterior capsule stretching." Not if the deficit is osseous — and in this population much of it is.

### 4.2 GIRD vs. total rotational motion — where the field actually sits

**Definitions.** **GIRD** = dominant IR subtracted from non-dominant, at 90° abduction with the scapula stabilized. **ERG** = external rotation gain. **TRM/TROM** = IR + ER on a side; the derived variable is the **side-to-side TRM difference.**

**The evidence, in order:**

1. **Wilk et al. (2011, *AJSM*)** — 122 **professional** pitchers over 5 seasons: **TRM deficit >5° carried ~2.5× shoulder injury risk.** GIRD carried ~1.9× odds but **did not reach significance (P = .17)**. This is the origin of "total motion beats GIRD." **[ESTABLISHED as a finding; the specific 5° threshold is EMERGING.]** *This is a professional cohort — sample matches our population.*
2. **Subsequent meta-analyses conflict.** A 2018 *Sports Health* systematic review/meta-analysis found GIRD associated with elevated upper-extremity injury risk in overhead athletes. A separate systematic review and meta-analysis of preseason ROM screening across 17 studies **failed to reach statistical significance for any shoulder motion measurement** as an in-season predictor of shoulder or elbow injury.
3. Epidemiologically, most injured pitchers do not have GIRD, and many with GIRD never get hurt.

**Where this leaves an elite-focused coach [EMERGING, trending toward "weak predictor"]:**
- **Preseason ROM screening is a weak population-level predictor. It is not a pass/fail gate.**
- **TRM asymmetry is more defensible than raw GIRD**, because it partially controls for the osseous contribution: if IR is lost and ER gained equally, the arc rotated (likely bone); if IR is lost *without* an ER gain, the arc shrank (more likely soft tissue). For our population — where retrotorsion is a given — this distinction is the whole ballgame.
- **▶ The real value of ROM measurement here is longitudinal and individual, not cross-sectional and normative.** IR and total motion drop after pitching and are still down at 24 h **[ESTABLISHED]**. Tracking an athlete against *his own* pre-outing baseline through a start cycle is a genuine readiness signal. Comparing him to a population mean is not.

**[FOLKLORE]** "Every pitcher needs X° of internal rotation." No validated universal threshold exists, and normative targets ignore individual retrotorsion and handedness.

### 4.3 Capsular changes

**[ESTABLISHED]** Posteroinferior capsular thickening is measurable in throwers by ultrasound. Internal rotation loss in professional pitchers has independent contributions from **humeral retroversion, posterior capsule thickness, and posterior rotator cuff pennation angle** — three different tissues producing one clinical number (*JSES Int*, 2022, professional cohort). This is why a single ROM measurement cannot tell you what to do.

**[ESTABLISHED, cadaveric]** A stiff posteroinferior capsule produces obligate anterosuperior humeral head translation in flexion/adduction/IR.

**[EMERGING]** Whether that obligate translation, the peel-back mechanism, or both, is the operative SLAP pathway in living elite throwers. Both models are widely taught; neither is proven dominant.

**Anterior capsule [ESTABLISHED].** Years of repetitive maximal external rotation produce plastic deformation of the anterior band of the IGHL — "acquired anterior laxity." In our population, assume this has occurred to some degree. It is part of how the athlete reaches the external rotation that 90+ requires.

### 4.4 Scapular dyskinesis — what it does and does not predict

- **[ESTABLISHED]** Highly prevalent in shoulder-injured populations, and highly prevalent in overhead athletes generally.
- **[ESTABLISHED] ▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC** It is *also* highly prevalent in **asymptomatic** professional and collegiate baseball players — a majority in some series.
- **[ESTABLISHED — 2013 Scapular Summit consensus, Kibler et al., *BJSM*]** "The exact role of the dyskinesis in creating or exacerbating shoulder dysfunction is not clearly defined." Treat it as an **impairment/modifier**, not a diagnosis and not a reliable predictor.
- **[EMERGING]** Whether correcting it prospectively reduces injury.

**Coaching implication.** A winging scapula in a pain-free, performing 92-MPH arm is not a reason to shut him down or rebuild his delivery. It *is* a reason to program serratus anterior and lower trapezius work — especially given the documented in-season periscapular strength decline (2.7). It becomes clinically meaningful when it co-exists with symptoms, ER strength loss, or performance decline. **⚠ REFER** if dyskinesis is new, unilateral, dramatic, and accompanied by weakness — true winging can be neurologic (long thoracic or spinal accessory nerve).

### 4.5 Laxity vs. instability

| | **Laxity** | **Instability** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A *sign* — quantity of passive translation available | A *symptom + sign* — translation producing pain, apprehension, or dysfunction |
| Who has it | Many elite throwers; often constitutional and bilateral | A subset |
| Meaning | Not pathologic alone; frequently **enabling** in this population | Pathologic |
| Coach's move | Note it; emphasize dynamic stability, do not stretch | **⚠ REFER** |

**[ESTABLISHED]** Laxity without symptoms is not instability. Many high-level throwers are lax and rely on exceptional dynamic control. "He's loose" is not a diagnosis — and aggressively stretching an already-lax elite thrower is a way to convert an asset into a problem.

**Microinstability [EMERGING].** The concept that small increments of anterior translation at MER — not frank dislocation — drive the internal impingement/SLAP cascade. Widely taught, mechanistically plausible, hard to measure in vivo.

### 4.6 ▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC: what "normal" imaging looks like in this population

This section exists because it is the most valuable single contrast between the elite thrower and the general athlete, and because it will come up the first time one of your athletes gets a scan.

**Shoulder.**
- In asymptomatic professional pitchers, the **labrum was abnormal in ~79% of throwing shoulders** (55% signal change, 45% frank tear) with no significant supraspinatus/infraspinatus differences between throwing and non-throwing sides (Miniaci et al., *AJSM* 2002). **[ESTABLISHED]**
- **High-resolution 3-T MRI in non-symptomatic professional draft picks** shows frequent abnormalities of the rotator cuff tendons, coracohumeral and inferior glenohumeral ligaments, labrum, and osseous structures (*AJSM* 2015). **[ESTABLISHED]**
- In one cohort of asymptomatic professional players, shoulder MRI showed **rotator cuff pathology in ~94%, labral tears in ~76%, and Bennett lesions in ~52%.** **[ESTABLISHED]**
- **Glenohumeral MRI findings correlate with innings pitched** in asymptomatic pitchers (2013) — i.e., these findings are dose-dependent consequences of the job. **[ESTABLISHED]**
- MRI findings of the asymptomatic shoulder **may impact performance but did not predict future injured-list placement** in MLB pitchers (*Arthrosc Sports Med Rehabil*, 2022). **[EMERGING]**

**Elbow.**
- **Elbow MRI findings do not correlate with future disabled-list placement in asymptomatic professional baseball pitchers** (Wilcox et al., 2017). **[ESTABLISHED]**
- Ultrasound in **368 asymptomatic professional pitchers**: dominant-arm **hypoechoic foci 28.0% vs 3.5%** and **calcifications 24.9% vs 1.6%** compared with the non-dominant elbow. Of a 26-pitcher MRI subset, **13 showed scar remodeling of the anterior bundle of the UCL** (Atanda et al., 10-year stress sonography study, 2014). **[ESTABLISHED]**
- **Subclinical medial collateral ligament injury and posteromedial impingement are demonstrable in asymptomatic professional players** (Kooima et al., *AJSM* 2004). **[ESTABLISHED]**

**What this means operationally.**
1. **The elite UCL is a partially remodeled, scarred, load-adapted structure, not a pristine ligament.** Assume damage history in any 85+ arm with years of mileage.
2. **A scan cannot be used to clear or condemn an asymptomatic elite arm.** It has essentially no demonstrated predictive value for future injury-list placement at either the elbow or the shoulder.
3. **Symptoms, strength, ROM trajectory, and performance are the operative signals.** The image is context.
4. Do not let an athlete be talked into surgery — or out of caution — by an image alone. **⚠ REFER** to a clinician who works with throwers specifically; the interpretive baselines are different.

## 5. Injury Mechanisms at Tissue Tolerance

> All of this is **⚠ REFER** territory once symptoms exist. The coach's contributions: recognize the pattern early, remove throwing load, route the athlete, and do not let him pitch through it.

### 5.1 Ulnar collateral ligament injury

**Mechanism [ESTABLISHED].** Repetitive tensile valgus load on the anterior bundle during late cocking and early acceleration, elbow flexed ~85–100°, valgus torque peaking near 100 N·m. Because that exceeds the isolated ligament's tensile capacity, survival depends on flexor-pronator and osseous load-sharing. Failure is **cumulative micro-trauma with a terminal acute event** — pitchers often report a discrete pop, but the ligament was being consumed long before.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC context.** Given 4.6, the 85+ arm's UCL likely already shows remodeling, scar, hypoechoic change, or calcification. The question is not "is it damaged" but "is the rate of damage exceeding the rate of repair" — see the tendon collagen-balance physiology in 6.1.

**Risk factors [ESTABLISHED]:** higher pitch velocity; higher workload/volume; **pitching while fatigued**; **pitching with arm pain**; year-round throwing with no true off-season; prior arm injury.

**Risk factors [EMERGING] — elite pitch-tracking data.** Recent MLB studies associate specific pitch characteristics with UCL reconstruction:
- Increased fastball, changeup, and sinker velocity; increased slider spin rate; increased cutter release extension (*AJSM* 2025, 115 UCLR pitchers vs 230 matched controls).
- Four-seam spin rate **100 rpm above league average ≈ +20% odds of UCLR**; **cutter arm-side movement +1 inch above league average ≈ +36% odds** (*Arthroscopy* 2025, MLB 2016–2024).
These are association studies on elite populations with confounding by role and workload. **Interesting, not causal.** But note the direction: the pitch characteristics the modern development industry chases are the ones associated with the injury.

**Risk factors [FOLKLORE] for our population:** "Curveballs cause Tommy John." Even in youth data this failed to hold; in an 85+ population, velocity and cumulative high-intent volume dominate the risk picture and pitch type is a distant secondary consideration.

**Warning signs the coach can observe.** Medial elbow pain at MER or early acceleration; arm-side command loss; a shortened arm action or "pushing" the ball; velocity loss at maintained effort; forearm tightness appearing earlier in each successive outing; new ulnar-sided numbness (5.4 — this often heralds the ligament).

### 5.2 Labral / SLAP lesions

**Mechanisms [ESTABLISHED as mechanisms; EMERGING as to which dominates]:**

1. **Peel-back (Burkhart & Morgan, 1998).** In late cocking, the abducted, hyper-externally-rotated humerus transmits a **posteriorly directed torsional force through the long head of the biceps** to its anchor. The anchor assumes a more vertical, posterior vector; the posterosuperior labrum peels off the glenoid rim — a type II SLAP.
2. **Internal (posterosuperior) impingement.** In the same position, the **articular undersurface of supraspinatus/infraspinatus is compressed between the greater tubercle and the posterosuperior glenoid rim.** ▶ **Some degree of this contact is physiologic in elite throwers** — it becomes pathologic with excessive anterior translation, scapular malposition, or ER beyond what the athlete's arc supports. Produces articular-sided partial cuff tears plus posterosuperior labral fraying.
3. **Deceleration traction** on the biceps anchor in follow-through.

**Warning signs.** Posterosuperior shoulder pain specifically in the late-cocking position; click or catch at MER; a "dead arm" presentation with velocity loss; follow-through pain if deceleration-dominant.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC caution [ESTABLISHED].** Labral abnormality is present in ~79% of *asymptomatic* professional throwing shoulders. Surgical outcomes for SLAP repair in overhead throwers are notably less reliable than for most shoulder procedures — return-to-prior-level rates are the sobering number in this literature. This is a "find a thrower-specific clinician and be conservative" injury.

### 5.3 Rotator cuff

**Mechanism in elite throwers.** Predominantly **articular-sided partial-thickness tears** of supraspinatus/infraspinatus from internal impingement, plus **tensile overload of the posterior cuff in deceleration.** ▶ Bursal-sided/subacromial impingement pathology is a general-population and older-athlete pattern; **throwers fail on the inside.** Coaching or rehab imported from general shoulder-impingement models frequently targets the wrong surface.

**Contributors.** Posterior cuff endurance failure and voluntary activation deficit (3.2); in-season periscapular strength decline (2.7); scapular malposition; anterior microinstability increasing posterosuperior excursion.

**Warning signs.** ER strength loss on manual testing; post-outing posterior shoulder ache lingering longer each time; slot loss; follow-through pain.

### 5.4 Ulnar neuritis / cubital tunnel

**Mechanism [ESTABLISHED].** Traction plus compression. Elbow flexion elongates the nerve and narrows the tunnel; shoulder abduction adds traction; **medial elbow laxity increases traction further**; the medial head of triceps or an anconeus epitrochlearis can compress; the nerve may sublux over the epicondyle. Ulnar nerve instability correlates with degree of UCL laxity in high-level pitchers (PMC11967374).

**Warning signs.** Numbness/tingling in the **ring and small fingers**, sometimes only during or immediately after throwing; medial elbow "electric" pain; grip weakness or loss of feel for the ball; clumsy hand. **⚠ REFER.**

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC read:** in this population, **new ulnar symptoms should raise suspicion about the ligament**, not just the nerve. They co-travel.

### 5.5 Thoracic outlet syndrome

**Anatomy.** Compression of the brachial plexus (neurogenic, the large majority), subclavian vein (venous — effort thrombosis / Paget-Schroetter), or subclavian artery, in one of three corridors: the **interscalene triangle** (anterior and middle scalenes + first rib), the **costoclavicular space** (clavicle + first rib), or the **retropectoralis minor space**.

**Why pitchers.** Repetitive overhead abduction/ER, hypertrophied scalenes, elevated or anomalous first rib (cervical rib), and scapular depression/protraction all narrow these corridors — a combination essentially unique to the overhead-throwing population.

**Warning signs.** Diffuse arm heaviness and fatigability out of proportion to workload; vague aching down the arm; paresthesias often in an ulnar (C8–T1, lower plexus) distribution; coldness or color change. **Venous TOS presents with arm swelling, cyanosis, and prominent superficial veins — urgent.** **⚠ REFER; venous and arterial presentations are emergencies.**

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC prognosis [ESTABLISHED].** In MLB pitchers undergoing first-rib resection for neurogenic or vascular TOS, **~74–81% returned to MLB pitching**, at a mean of **~10 months** post-op, average post-op career length **~3 seasons**, with performance metrics not significantly different from matched controls among those who returned (Arnold et al., 2022, *OJSM*; 2021 companion work). Encouraging but sobering — and this is one of the most frequently missed diagnoses in professional pitching, often carried for months as "dead arm."

### 5.6 Valgus extension overload and olecranon stress

**Mechanism [ESTABLISHED].** In terminal extension under valgus load the **posteromedial olecranon tip** shears against the medial wall of the olecranon fossa → chondral wear and posteromedial osteophytes. Separately, repetitive traction/torsion produces **olecranon stress reactions and fractures** in high-velocity arms.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC.** Subclinical posteromedial impingement is demonstrable in asymptomatic professionals (Kooima et al., 2004) — so its *presence* is not the finding; its *symptomatic progression* is.

**Warning signs.** Posterior elbow pain **at and just after ball release**; loss of terminal extension; pain on forced extension; a sense of the elbow "jamming." **⚠ REFER.**

**Important trade-off coaches should know exists:** osteophyte removal without addressing underlying valgus laxity can **increase UCL strain** — the spurs are partly a consequence of laxity. This is a surgeon's decision, but the athlete should hear the trade-off named.

### 5.7 Weighted-ball and velocity-development programs

**[ESTABLISHED, single well-designed trial]** Reinold et al. (2018, *Sports Health*): a 6-week weighted-ball program produced modest velocity gain and a **3.3° increase in shoulder external rotation ROM**, but **24% of the weighted-ball group sustained an injury** (primarily elbow stress fractures and UCL injuries) versus **0%** in the 5-oz control group. ⚑ *Sample was high-school-aged pitchers, not exclusively 85+ — but this is the population feeding directly into ours.* The ER gain is best read as **acquired laxity, not improved mobility.**

**[EMERGING]** Whether individualized, supervised, properly-dosed weighted-implement work carries acceptable risk in a mature 85+ arm. Recent professional-population survey work (IJSPT, 2025) has not reproduced so stark an injury signal. Field practice varies enormously.

**Honest reading for this population:** the 24% figure indicts *that protocol in that population*, not all weighted implements. But the burden of proof sits with the intervention, and the specific injuries reported — olecranon stress and UCL — are exactly the tissues already operating near tolerance at 85+.

### 5.8 A note on capitellar OCD as history

Not a current-population injury, but **ask about it.** A healed adolescent capitellar osteochondritis dissecans lesion can leave a mechanically compromised lateral column in a now-90-MPH arm, altering how valgus compressive load is shared and effectively raising the share carried medially. Any history of adolescent lateral elbow pain, effusion, catching, or locking is worth flagging to the medical staff. **[EMERGING]** as a stated risk modifier in mature throwers.

## 6. Recovery Physiology for the High-Output Arm

### 6.1 Inflammation, repair, and the collagen balance window

| Phase | Window | What happens | Meaning for an 85+ arm |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hemostasis / acute inflammatory** | 0–~72 h | Vasodilation, neutrophils then macrophages, cytokine signaling | Inflammation is **required** for repair. Blanket suppression is not obviously good. |
| **Proliferative / repair** | ~3 days–6 weeks | Fibroblast proliferation, type III collagen deposition, angiogenesis | New tissue is disorganized and weak. "It feels fine" is most dangerous here. |
| **Remodeling / maturation** | ~6 weeks–12+ months | Type III → type I collagen, alignment along load lines, tensile strength recovery | **Mechanical loading directs alignment.** Rest alone produces weak, disorganized tissue. |

**▶ The single most useful physiological fact for structuring a between-start week. [ESTABLISHED — Magnusson & Kjaer line of work]**
After a bout of loading, tendon **collagen synthesis peaks around ~24 h and stays elevated ~72 h**, but **degradation rises earlier**. Net collagen balance is therefore **negative up to roughly 36 h post-load and turns net positive from ~36–72 h.**

**Direct implication: repeatedly reloading connective tissue inside the negative-balance window produces net matrix degradation.** That is a mechanistic account of tendinopathy from insufficient rest — and, extended to the elite arm, a physiological argument against stacking high-intent throwing days.

*Caveat stated plainly:* this work is from patellar tendon and Achilles in non-throwers. It has **not** been mapped for the UCL under pitching loads. It is the best available model, not a measurement of your athlete's ligament. (This is Open Question #2.)

**Bone [ESTABLISHED].** Remodeling proceeds osteoclastic-resorption-first, creating a transient window of *weakened* bone after a load increase — the basis for olecranon and rib stress reactions when high-intent volume ramps too fast. Bone stress injuries need weeks to months.

### 6.2 Sleep

**[ESTABLISHED]** Sleep is when the majority of growth hormone is secreted (pulsatile, slow-wave-sleep-linked); sleep restriction impairs reaction time, decision-making, and glucose handling.

**[ESTABLISHED, but ⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH for injury odds]** Milewski et al. (2014): adolescent athletes sleeping <8 h/night had ~1.7× the odds of musculoskeletal injury. Sample is adolescent athletes broadly, not elite pitchers — **directionally suggestive only** for our population, though the mechanism is general.

**[EMERGING]** Sleep *extension* improves performance metrics (Mah et al., Stanford extension studies) — small, unblinded studies; direction right, effect size uncertain.

**▶ ELITE-SPECIFIC threats to sleep in this population:** night games with late arousal, cross-country travel and time-zone shifts, showcase travel schedules, and — for the amateur half — school schedules stacked on top of evening outings. **Sleep is the highest-return, lowest-cost recovery intervention available and it is free.** For this population it is also the most frequently compromised.

### 6.3 Protein synthesis and fueling

- **[ESTABLISHED]** Muscle protein synthesis is elevated ~24–48 h after resistance exercise; stimulated by ~0.3 g/kg high-quality protein per feeding (~20–40 g, ~2–3 g leucine); ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day total supports lean mass in trained athletes; distribution across ~3–5 feedings is modestly superior to skewed intake.
- **[ESTABLISHED]** Carbohydrate availability matters for glycogen restoration across a dense schedule. For a starter this is a full-week fueling question, not a post-game-shake question.
- **[EMERGING]** Collagen peptide (~15 g) + vitamin C ~30–60 min before loading may raise collagen synthesis markers and support tendon/ligament adaptation. Mechanistically interesting; unproven for injury prevention in throwers. Given 6.1, it is a plausible target for future study in this population specifically.
- **[FOLKLORE]** The "anabolic window" as a narrow 30-minute requirement. Total daily protein and training stimulus dominate.

### 6.4 Recovery modalities — candid evidence audit

| Modality | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Sleep, 8–10 h, consistent timing** | Strong mechanistic; strong observational (⚑ adolescent sample for the injury odds) | **[ESTABLISHED]** — do this first, above everything else. |
| **Adequate energy and protein intake** | Strong | **[ESTABLISHED]** |
| **High-intent load management — counting pens, showcases, and testing days as load** | Strong mechanistically; workload epidemiology supports; ACWR thresholds contested | **[ESTABLISHED principle / EMERGING thresholds]** — the most effective recovery intervention is not overloading in the first place. |
| **Structured, standardized warm-up before high-intent throwing** | Injury-reduction RCT evidence exists but ⚑ in youth cohorts (Sakata 2019); mechanism general | **[EMERGING for our population]** — low cost, no velocity penalty observed, worth doing. |
| **In-season periscapular and posterior cuff maintenance work** | Documented in-season strength decline in collegiate pitchers | **[ESTABLISHED need; EMERGING outcome]** |
| **Active recovery (low-intensity aerobic)** | Modest evidence for perceived recovery and blood flow | **[EMERGING]** — probably mildly helpful, harmless. |
| **Cold water immersion for perceived soreness / next-day readiness** | Reasonable evidence for reduced DOMS and perceived fatigue | **[ESTABLISHED for the perceptual effect]** |
| **Routine CWI after resistance training** | Piñero et al. (2024, *Eur J Sport Sci*) meta-analysis: **CWI attenuates resistance-training hypertrophy**; small, consistent; strength/power gains blunted in some analyses | **[ESTABLISHED]** — **do not routinely ice after lifting.** Use CWI tactically (dense in-season stretches where next-day readiness beats adaptation), never habitually in an off-season building block. |
| **Icing the arm after an outing** | Widely practiced; no good evidence it improves healing or prevents injury; inflammation is part of repair | **[FOLKLORE as a treatment claim; EMERGING for perceived soreness]** |
| **Compression garments** | Small perceived-soreness effects | **[EMERGING]** — cheap, harmless, low return. |
| **Massage / soft-tissue work** | Modest short-term perceived recovery and ROM; no durable structural change | **[EMERGING]** |
| **Static stretching as injury prevention** | Repeatedly fails to reduce injury incidence; a baseball RCT found shoulder strengthening non-inferior/favorable vs stretching for arm-injury prevention | **[FOLKLORE]** in isolation. Targeted mobility for a *measured* restriction is a different thing. |
| **Whole-body cryotherapy chambers** | Sparse, mixed | **[EMERGING]**, largely unsupported beyond perception. |
| **Dry needling, cupping, percussion, kinesiology tape** | Short-term perceptual/ROM effects; no durable structural effect | **[EMERGING → FOLKLORE]** depending on the claim. Adjuncts, not treatments. |
| **Blood flow restriction training in rehab** | Growing support for building/maintaining strength at low loads | **[EMERGING, promising]** — clinician-supervised only. |
| **Post-outing running to "flush lactic acid"** | No physiological basis for pitching | **[FOLKLORE]** |
| **Routine NSAIDs after outings** | Suppresses the inflammatory signaling that drives repair; evidence of blunted tendon/muscle adaptation | **[EMERGING concern]** — physician's call, not a clubhouse habit. **⚠ REFER** |
| **Sleeper / cross-body stretch to "fix GIRD"** | Improves measured IR short-term; ineffective against osseous retrotorsion; risks driving an already-lax anterior capsule | **[EMERGING with strong caveats]** — appropriate only for a demonstrated soft-tissue posterior restriction, under a PT/ATC's direction. In this population, assume a large osseous component first. |

### 6.5 A defensible between-start framework

Not prescriptive medicine — a physiologically-reasoned template to discuss with the athlete's medical and performance staff.

- **Day 0 (start / high-intent outing):** peak load. Post-outing: fuel, hydrate, sleep. Perceptual modalities only.
- **Day +1:** ROM and strength are measurably still depressed **[ESTABLISHED]**; collagen net balance is still **negative** **[ESTABLISHED, non-throwing tissue]**. Low-intensity movement, blood flow, mobility. **No high-intent throwing.**
- **Day +2:** collagen balance now net positive. Reintroduce lower-body strength and moderate-intent throwing.
- **Day +3:** highest-intent secondary work / bullpen — **gated on the athlete's own ROM and ER-strength markers having returned to his individual baseline**, not on the calendar.
- **Day +4:** taper, mobility, sleep emphasis.
- **Throughout:** track *individual* baselines — ER strength, total rotational motion, elbow extension, perceived arm readiness. **Trend against his own baseline is the signal. Population norms are noise at this level.**

**Showcase and testing-day addendum.** A maximal-intent outing off-schedule with low chronic load is the highest-risk exposure this population faces (3.4). If it cannot be avoided, it should at minimum be preceded by a genuine chronic-load ramp and a full warm-up, and followed by the same recovery map as a start — not treated as a free throw.

## 7. Coach-Facing Summary Tables

### 7.1 Where it hurts → what it suggests → what to do

| Location & timing | Structures implicated | Coach action |
|---|---|---|
| Medial elbow at MER / early acceleration | UCL anterior bundle, flexor-pronator origin, ulnar nerve | **Stop throwing. ⚠ REFER.** |
| Posterior elbow at/after release | Olecranon and olecranon fossa (valgus extension overload), olecranon stress reaction | **Stop throwing. ⚠ REFER.** |
| Posterosuperior shoulder in late cocking | Posterosuperior labrum, articular-side cuff, internal impingement | **⚠ REFER.** |
| Anterior shoulder at MER | Anterior band of IGHL, subscapularis, biceps pulley / LHB | **⚠ REFER.** |
| Diffuse shoulder ache in follow-through | Posterior cuff (deceleration overload), biceps anchor | Reduce load; **⚠ REFER** if persistent. |
| Ring/small finger numbness or tingling | Ulnar nerve (cubital tunnel) or lower brachial plexus (TOS) | **⚠ REFER** — and raise UCL suspicion. |
| Whole-arm heaviness + coldness, swelling, or color change | Vascular thoracic outlet syndrome | **⚠ REFER URGENTLY.** |
| Posterior axillary / lateral chest wall, with abrupt velocity loss | Latissimus dorsi / teres major strain | **⚠ REFER.** |
| Lateral trunk, sudden, often with a pop | Oblique strain (usually lead side) | **⚠ REFER.** |
| Heaviness with no pain, resolving in 2–5 days with load reduction | Physiologic fatigue (3.5a) | Manage load; re-check ROM and ER strength against his baseline. |
| Heaviness that persists, recurs on schedule, or carries any neuro symptom | Structural or neurovascular (3.5b/c) | **⚠ REFER.** |

### 7.2 The five things with the best evidence behind them for an 85+ arm

1. **Count and manage total high-intent throwing volume — pens, flat-ground, long toss, showcases, testing days — not game pitch counts.** Game counts undercount true load. **[ESTABLISHED]**
2. **Never let him throw at intent through arm pain or overt fatigue.** Fatigue produces measurable posterior-cuff neural inhibition while joint kinetics stay high. **[ESTABLISHED]**
3. **Protect sleep, 8–10 h, consistently, through travel and night games.** **[ESTABLISHED mechanism]**
4. **Maintain posterior cuff, periscapular (serratus anterior, lower trap), and flexor-pronator strength and endurance *in season*** — these are documented depleting resources across a season and they are the active load-sharers for the passive structures that actually fail. **[ESTABLISHED need; EMERGING outcome data]**
5. **Track the individual, longitudinally: ER strength, total rotational motion, elbow extension, perceived readiness — against his own baseline.** Population norms and one-off screens are weak predictors; his own trajectory is not. **[ESTABLISHED for post-outing deficits; EMERGING as a formal readiness gate]**

### 7.3 Things to stop saying

- "He needs to stretch out his GIRD." **[FOLKLORE when the deficit is osseous — and in this population much of it is.]**
- "His MRI is ugly, so he's hurt." **[FOLKLORE — ~79% of asymptomatic pro throwing shoulders have labral abnormality; elbow MRI doesn't predict injury-list placement.]**
- "His MRI is clean, so he's fine." **[Equally wrong — imaging has poor predictive value in either direction here.]**
- "Run to flush the lactic acid." **[FOLKLORE]**
- "Ice it and he'll be fine." **[FOLKLORE as a treatment claim.]**
- "He's just got dead arm, he'll throw through it." **[Dangerous — see 3.5.]**
- "His scap wings, so he's going to get hurt." **[FOLKLORE — majority prevalence in asymptomatic pros.]**
- "Velocity's still there, so he's not tired." **[Backwards — velocity is the last thing to go.]**
- "He's loose, that's a red flag." **[Laxity ≠ instability; in this population laxity is frequently an asset.]**

## 8. Open Questions

For the biomechanist or coach to chase next:

1. **Can mechanics decouple velocity from elbow varus torque within an individual 85+ arm, and by how much?** The population-level velocity–torque correlation is strong, but the residual — pitchers who throw 95 at below-expected torque — is where all the coaching leverage lives, and it is poorly characterized in public literature.
2. **What is the real dose–response curve for high-intent throwing and net collagen balance in the UCL specifically?** The 36–72 h window comes from patellar tendon and Achilles work in non-throwers. Nobody has mapped it for a ligament under ~100 N·m of repeated valgus load. This is the single biggest gap between our recovery reasoning and our evidence.
3. **Is there a practical in-game or between-inning marker of posterior-cuff voluntary activation deficit** — something an ATC could measure in the dugout — that would flag the inhibition documented in 3.2 *before* command drifts and long before velocity drops?
4. **Does flexor-pronator strength and endurance training prospectively reduce UCL injury incidence in 85+ arms?** The stress-shielding mechanism is established; the outcome trial does not exist. Related: does grip endurance specifically track with late-outing medial elbow load?
5. **Why does the Reinold 2018 weighted-ball injury signal (24%) diverge so sharply from professional-population survey data?** Dosing, supervision, skeletal maturity of the sample, or study design — and which of those, if any, makes weighted implements defensible in a mature 85+ arm?
