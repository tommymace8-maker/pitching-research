### 1. ⚠️ Stride length has been experimentally manipulated — twice, independently — and neither found a gain. **The most consequential item in either sweep.**

**Sources:**
- **Ramsey DK, Crotin RL, White S (2014).** *Effect of stride length on overarm throwing delivery: A linear momentum response.* ***Hum Mov Sci* 38:185–196.** [PMID 25457417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25457417/)
- **Matsuda T, Hirano A, Umakoshi Y, Kimura A (2025).** ***Front Sports Act Living*.** [PMID 40264933](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40264933/)
- *Smith S, Smith E, Bowman T (2017), SABR* Baseball Research Journal *— grey literature, directionally consistent, do not lean on it*

**Who and standing:** Ramsey and Crotin are a genuine biomechanics group (Buffalo); Crotin also co-authors the Sakurai/Lis jump-testing papers already in `velocity-development.md` §2.1. Matsuda et al. are an independent Japanese group. **Neither has a commercial interest in the answer.**

**What the manipulations found:**

| Study | n / level | Manipulation | Ball velocity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ramsey/Crotin/White 2014 | 19 (15 collegiate, 4 elite HS) ⚑ | **±25%** of self-selected stride, randomized crossover, 8-camera mocap 240 Hz + 2 force plates, two 80-pitch simulated games, 72 h washout. Stride differed p ≤ .001 | **Hand and ball velocity EQUIVALENT across conditions** |
| Matsuda 2025 | 20 college ⚑ | **±20%** instructed | **Under 72.7 · NORMAL 75.8 · Over 72.7 mph. His own stride significantly FASTER than both. Lengthening made them SLOWER** |

**And the normalization is contradicted twice, by critiques that disagree with each other:**
- Solomito, Cohen & Garibay (2023), *Sports Biomech* 22(11):1460–1469 ([PMID 32912079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32912079/)), n = 99 collegiate: **no significant association between normalized stride length and ball velocity**; proposes 131–137% of *leg length* instead.
- **Yanagisawa O, Taniguchi H (2020), *J Phys Ther Sci* 32(9):578–583** ([PMID 32982054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32982054/)), n = 18 collegiate: **absolute** stride length **r = 0.55, p = .02** — but **as % body height, r = 0.36, p = .15, NOT significant.** Also null against lower-extremity length and hip-abduction width.

> ### ⚠️ COUNT THE BUFFALO DATASET ONCE — a hazard in its own right
> The 19-athlete Buffalo cohort has been reported across **at least seven papers**: PMIDs **25457417, 26707678, 23917472, 25804970, 29578381, 37109515**, plus *Life* 15(9):1440 (2025), PMID 41010383. **Anyone citing "multiple Crotin/Ramsey studies" is citing one experiment on 19 pitchers up to seven times.** Canonical velocity citation: **PMID 25457417.**
>
> **I made this error myself on the first pass of this sweep, in the direction of the argument I was already making.** Logged in `daily/2026-08-13-coach.md` §11.

**VERDICT: DEBUNKED for "stride further, throw harder." UNPROVEN for the trained version.**

> **A long stride is a MARKER of a pitcher who throws hard, not a LEVER that makes one.** Pitchers who generate more momentum stride further *as a consequence*. That is why it correlates at n = 315 professionals and does nothing when you move it.

**In fairness:** both manipulations are **acute, instructed changes to a habituated stride** at ±20–25%, in samples averaging **~73–81 mph — below our floor** — at n = 19–20. Forcing an over-stride tests whether a man can absorb an insult, not whether an owned stride is worth anything. **Manzi 2024 retained stride length in both the HS and professional models; the cross-sectional association is real. Its causal reading is what died.**

**Applied:** `velocity-development.md` §4.4, §7 (demoted from #2), §6.7 Step 5 (the "1–1.5 mph sitting there" line deleted), §9 Open Question #8 (rewritten). `coaching-translation.md` §11.7, §12, and the cue is retired in §9. Logged as `open-disputes.md` **#13**.

### 2. Nevada/Reno — the only stride-length TRAINING intervention in existence, and it is unpublished

**Source:** [University of Nevada, Reno — Nevada Today, 2026](https://www.unr.edu/nevada-today/news/2026/pitching-research); corroborated by [ASPPH](https://aspph.org/nevada-reno-researchers-study-how-ankle-motion-shapes-a-baseball-pitchers-stride/)

**Who:** **Zack Buck** (MS Kinesiology, 2026), advisor **Phil Pavilionis, Ph.D.** (associate teaching professor, athletic training education lead).

**What:** D1 Wolf Pack pitchers, fall offseason. Ankle ROM testing + a dynamic balance assessment mirroring the single-leg demands of pitching. **4-week intervention: resistance-band ankle mobility drills + balance exercises integrated into normal training. Stride length measured during pitching trials before and after.** Hypothesis: stride length is gated at the **drive-leg ankle**. Working target stated as **80–87% of pitcher height.**

**Defended spring 2026. No outcome data disclosed. No journal publication. No velocity outcome and no control group** even if the data exists.

**VERDICT: UNPROVEN — but it is the closest thing to a longitudinal stride-lengthening intervention anywhere, and it is the right design philosophy** (remove a constraint rather than cue the outcome, which is precisely the approach the two failed manipulations did *not* take). **ACTION: request the thesis.**

### 3. ⚠️ The 85 mph floor claim was too strong — and one study clears it POSITIVELY

**Sources:**
- **Ake H et al. (2016).** M.S. thesis, Univ. of Mississippi + *IJES* Conference Proceedings. n = 56 NCAA D1 SEC pitchers. **Baseline 87.25 ± 2.32 mph (weighted-implement) / 86.80 ± 1.32 mph (control)** — the **highest-velocity controlled sample in the entire literature**, and `velocity-development.md` recorded its baseline as *"not reported."* Result still **null** (p = .071 within, p = .271 between). **Standing lesson: nobody checks a negative claim as hard as a positive one, and "not one" was doing rhetorical work it had not earned.**

### 4. **The first positive controlled velocity result above the 85 mph floor** — and what "core-coiling" actually is

**Source:** **Lee CM, Choi W, Jeon BH (2026).** *The Effect of Core-Coiling Training for 12 Weeks on Skill-Related Physical Fitness and Performance in Collegiate Baseball Pitchers.* ***Asian Journal of Kinesiology* 28(1):19.** DOI [10.15758/ajk.2026.28.1.19](https://www.jkinesiol.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.15758%2Fajk.2026.28.1.19)

**What "core-coiling training" IS, since the name obscures it:** it is **a rotational medicine-ball and tubing program** — the thing American facilities have called "rotational power work" for fifteen years. Eight exercises: *Tubing Torso Rotation · One-Side Torso Rotation · One-Leg Torso Rotation · **Medicine Ball Side Throw** · **Medicine Ball Side Catch** · **Jump and Wall Ball Side Throw** · **Side Jumping and Wall Ball Throw** · Walking Lunge and Twist.*
**Dose: 70-min sessions (10 warm-up / 50 main / 10 cool-down), 4×/week × 12 weeks. Intensity 60% 1RM wk 1–4 → 65% wk 5–8 → 70% wk 9–12.** **Fully specified and runnable Monday.**

**Sample:** n = 15 CCT vs 14 comparator, age ~20, Korean collegiate. **Baseline MAX velocity 86.2 / 85.9 mph — clears the floor. Baseline AVERAGE 84.0 / 83.5 — does not.**

**Results:** max velocity **p = .003** (comparator NS, p = .547); average velocity **p = .011** (comparator NS, p = .165); between-group ANCOVA **p = .014 / .042**; agility **p = .002**. **Control/accuracy: no improvement in either group.**

**VERDICT: PROMISING — and the item in this library most likely to be over-quoted within a year.**

**Why it deserves real attention:** it is the only positive controlled velocity result clearing the floor; it converges with the mechanism (trunk power alone R² = 0.731 with ball velocity; hips do ~90% of torso-rotation work; trunk torques are the primary energy *sources*); and **it converges with the only other positive controlled non-implement trial, Oyama 2026's lumbopelvic-hip block. Two countries, two populations, two independent controlled trials, same anatomical target.** It also **partially rehabilitates rotational med-ball work**, which this library had labeled FOLKLORE for velocity on the (still correct) grounds that the Szymanski literature is HS *hitters* with *bat-speed* outcomes.

**⚠️ Why to hold it loosely — the second one is disqualifying for the strong claim:**
1. **Not randomized.** No allocation concealment or blinding described.
2. **The comparator is close to a straw man.** It did **chest press, pull-down, shoulder press, arm curl, squat, leg press, leg extension, leg curl** — **no rotational work, no plyometrics, no explosive lower-body work at all.** So this is **not** "rotational training beats strength training." It is **"12 weeks of explosive rotational work beats 12 weeks of bodybuilding machines"** — and this library's own §2.1 says the qualities that predict velocity are **absolute power and reactive strength**, neither of which the comparator trained.
3. Clears the floor on **peak** velocity only.
4. n = 29, one squad, one university. No kinematics, no kinetics, no stress outcome.
5. **Command did not improve** — say that out loud, since rotational-power marketing implies it will.
6. Low-profile regional journal; not PubMed-indexed at review.

**Cite it as:** *"one non-randomized 12-week trial, active comparator, collegiate, positive on peak velocity."* **Never** *"core training adds velocity."*
**What would settle it:** the same trial with a real comparator — rotational med-ball vs a **matched-dose explosive lower-body program**, randomized, at 85+. **Until then we do not know whether the trunk is special or whether anything explosive beats leg extensions.**

### 5. Spin efficiency is ~65% fixed across three years — and the one documented mover bought a delivery change

**Source:** **Michael Rosen, FanGraphs, 13 Apr 2026.** ["Emerson Hancock Became Less Efficient and More Effective"](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/emerson-hancock-became-less-efficient-and-more-effective/)

**Who and standing:** Rosen is the 2026 SABR Analytics Research Award winner and author of *Pitch Plots*. High standing; his June 2025 changeup piece is already load-bearing in this library.

**The number, verified verbatim:** *"I took 185 pitchers who threw at least 25 fastballs in both 2023 and 2026; over that three-year span, the r-squared between their spin efficiency was 0.65."*

**The case:** Hancock dropped his **arm angle 23° → 13°** and moved his landing from left of his back foot to right of it (crossbody). Per Driveline's **Spenser Davis**, the crossbody landing *"cut off his tendency to pronate."* Four-seam spin efficiency fell from 99%; his fastball moved from "dead zone" to "an average pitch"; he added a sweeper (a third of his pitches in one recent start) and posted **2.38 FIP, 30.6% K%.** **Connor White's caution: *"The arm is not one fixed lever."*** And the field's own line: *"once you go supinator, it's hard to go back."*

**VERDICT: PROMISING — and it answers `stuff-and-command.md` Open Question #9 in the uncomfortable direction.**

> **Spin efficiency is roughly two-thirds a fixed trait over three years. The one-third that moves appears to move via ARM SLOT and LANDING POSITION — i.e. via a delivery change, with all of Dispute #10's and #12's costs attached.** The pitch-design industry sells efficiency work as a grip project. **It may be a mechanics project in a grip's costume, and nobody is quoting that price.**

Logged as `open-disputes.md` **#15**.

### 6. Arm angle is platoon-dependent — and the author says he can't explain his own result

**Source:** **Davy Andrews, FanGraphs, 27 Nov 2024.** ["An Arm Angle Update That Ends With a Mystery"](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/an-arm-angle-update-that-ends-with-a-mystery/)

**Findings:** lower arm angles perform better against **same-handed** batters; **higher** arm angles better against **opposite-handed**. Same-sided correlations: **arm angle→wOBA .13, →xwOBA .23**; velocity −.18. **The mystery Andrews flags: every metric correlated better with xwOBA than with actual wOBA**, and he writes *"I don't have a good explanation for this,"* speculates about spray angle, and invites readers to check him.

*(Prior context, unchanged: Alex Eisert, FanGraphs, 7 Feb 2023 — +0.02% called-strike rate per degree toward sidearm across 473 pitchers with 500+ pitches; effect disappears when controlling for pitch selection and count.)*

### 9. Weighted balls reframed: "pitch speed, not ball weight, drives joint stress"

**Source:** [SportRxiv preprint 821](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/821), **Kyle Boddy, Alex Cochrane, Joseph Marsh, Gretchen Pouch, Deven Morgan (Driveline), 16 Apr 2026.**

**Numbers (verified on the primary page):** n = 30 youth pitchers, **age 13.3 ± 0.7 yr, 57.0 ± 12.3 kg.** Mass-corrected elbow varus: **4 oz vs 5 oz +3.0 N·m** (p = .006, g = 0.53); **6 oz vs 5 oz −5.4 N·m** (p < .001, g = 1.06). **With pitch speed as a covariate, 4 oz vs 5 oz drops to 1.2 N·m, ns (p = .07).** SPM: 46% of the elbow cycle, 52% of the shoulder.

**The reframe:** after mass-correction, *lighter* balls produce higher joint moments — but the difference largely disappears once speed is controlled. **So the "heavy balls are safer / light balls are dangerous" debate may be a velocity-management question wearing an implement costume.**

**VERDICT: PROMISING as a reframe. ⚑ SEVERE SAMPLE MISMATCH — thirteen-year-olds, n = 30. And Boddy is the founder of the company selling the implements, which does not make it wrong but does make it the paper most likely to be over-extrapolated in the wild.** Relevant to Dispute #8 and consistent with Sweep 1 #10's objection: this is **another concentric-phase acute study**, and the Reinold injury signal was olecranon stress fractures and UCL tears — cumulative and deceleration-phase events an acute study cannot speak to.

### 13. ⚠️ A NEW HAZARD CLASS — search-summary sample conflation. This one does not require an AI content farm.

**What happened:** search-result summaries auto-attributed the **43,650-pitch / 2,052-pitcher** sample from Bloebaum's UCM preprint (#1010) onto his **separate** energy-flow preprint (DOI 10.51224/SportRxiv.871). Both are real papers by the same author on the same platform. **An ordinary search summary manufactured a phantom sample size, with no fabricated source and no bad actor anywhere in the chain.**

**Why this is worse than Sweep 1 #12.** The content-farm hazard has a tell — unearned precision from a domain nobody has heard of. **This one has no tell.** The paper is real, the author is real, the number is real, and it belongs to a different paper.

**NEW STANDING RULE: sample sizes are verified on the paper's own page. Never from a search summary, never from an abstract aggregator, never from another paper's citation of it.**

**And the broader lesson from today, which is bigger than this item:** five library corrections landed on 2026-08-13 and **not one was a fabrication.** Every one was a real source read slightly wrong — a cross-sectional finding read as a lever, two adjacent quantities collapsed, a summary figure taken over the underlying table, a sampling unit misread, an absolutism unearned. **Verifying that a citation exists is not verifying that the sentence around it is true, and this program has been treating those as the same operation.** Full analysis in `daily/2026-08-13-coach.md` §11.

### 1. Vary-the-effort as an injury-prevention strategy — and the effort/torque math that undercuts it
**Sources:**
- Fleisig GS (2026). *Baseball has an elbow injury problem: biomechanics may have the solution.* *Sports Biomechanics*, Jun 2026. [PubMed 42312376](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42312376/)
- Fiegen et al. (2023), *AJSM*. [PubMed 36625428](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36625428/)
- Wolf JH, Kinney S, Waterman BR, Bullock GS, Nicholson K (2025), *OJSM*. [PubMed 40933947](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40933947/)
- Hyeamang LJ, Dowling B, Hodakowski A, et al. (2026), *OJSM*. [PubMed 41573128](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41573128/)

**Who and standing:** Fleisig is Research Director at ASMI — the single most authoritative voice in this literature. Wolf/Nicholson is Wake Forest (KinaTrax). Hyeamang/Dowling/Verma/Garrigues is Rush + Sports Medicine Analytics — Dowling ran Motus R&D.

**The claim:** Fleisig proposes that varying effort rather than throwing max on every pitch may both reduce UCL damage and improve performance by disrupting hitter timing.

**What the primary data actually shows:**

| Study | n / level | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Fiegen 2023 | 10 HS `[OFF-POPULATION]` | 75% effort → **81% of max elbow stress, 90% of max velocity.** Per 25% perceived-effort drop: **−13% stress, −7.5% velocity** |
| Wolf 2025 | 19 collegiate | Only 100% vs 60% differed: **92.5 → 73.2 N·m** (P = .017). **Kinematics unchanged across all five effort levels** |
| Hyeamang 2026 | 38 HS + 24 **professional** | At 50% RPE, HS threw **86% of velocity, 75% of max varus torque, 80% cumulative torque, 67% torque loading rate.** Pros far more consistent at submaximal effort |

**VERDICT: PROMISING for the strategy, DEBUNKED for the naive version of it.**
Effort reduction does lower torque, but **sublinearly and inefficiently** — the exchange rate is roughly 13–19% stress reduction per 25% perceived-effort reduction, at a 7.5–10% velocity cost. "Throw at 60%" is not a meaningful protective instruction, especially for the amateur half of this population, and Hyeamang shows the perception is badly calibrated in HS arms. Fleisig's *strategic* case (deception, timing) is separate from and probably stronger than his *load* case.

**What I'm doing with it:** submaximal throwing days become **radar-gated, not feel-gated** (see `coaching-translation.md` §4). Genuinely new — neither library file covers effort levels at all.

### 2. Interval throwing programs rebuilt from 111,196 throws
**Source:** Reinold MM, Dowling B, Fleisig GS, Macrina LC, Wilk KE, Alexander FJ, Ahmad CS, Dugas J, Andrews JR (2026). *Interval Throwing Programs of Various Duration for Baseball Players Based on Biomechanical Workload Data.* *IJSPT* 21(4):428–439. [IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/159340-interval-throwing-programs-of-various-duration-for-baseball-players-based-on-biomechanical-workload-data)

**Who and standing:** the entire ASMI/Andrews/Reinold axis. As authoritative as return-to-throw programming gets.

**What's new:** 238,611 flat-ground throws from **34 healthy NCAA D1 pitchers**; second-order polynomial regression of throwing distance → peak elbow varus torque; workload expressed as ACWR with 7-day acute / 28-day chronic windows. Four published programs (6-week, 12-week, 5-month, 7-month) with 91–100% ACWR compliance in the 0.7–1.3 band.

**The number a coach should memorize:** **the elbow varus torque at 120 ft of long toss is similar to pitching off a mound.** That reframes long toss from "arm care" to "mound-equivalent load" past ~120 ft, and it is why these programs cap the long-toss phase there.

**VERDICT: PROMISING.** Sample matches (D1), and it converts a folk practice into a dosed one. Caveats: 34 athletes, all healthy, ACWR methodology is itself contested in sports science, and this is a *modeling* paper — no injury outcomes. Not in either library file. **The most immediately useful item in this sweep.**

### 3. Skill is not less variability — it's better-organized variability
**Source:** Bloebaum A (Driveline Baseball). *Skilled pitching is not less joint variability but better-structured joint variability: a time-resolved uncontrolled-manifold analysis of the baseball pitching delivery.* SportRxiv preprint. [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/1010)

**Sample:** **43,650 game-speed pitches, 4,338 athlete-sessions, 2,052 pitchers**, markerless capture. This is by far the largest kinematic-variability dataset in the public literature.

> ### ⚠️ CORRECTED 2026-08-13 — read this before using this item
>
> **The error:** this entry originally used the word "skilled" and sat in a run of command items, which caused the synergy index to be read as a **command/accuracy** finding. **It is not.** The partial **ρ = 0.22, p < 10⁻⁴⁷ at release, is an association with PITCH VELOCITY.** Bloebaum's outcome variable is velocity throughout; the paper contains no accuracy or location measure at all.
>
> **Why the mistake is easy to make:** the paper decomposes variance *about the release point*, so "release point" appears everywhere in the methods — but as the **performance variable being protected**, not as an outcome being predicted. Higher-velocity pitchers organize their joints to protect the release point better. That is a statement about coordination quality, not about where the ball goes.
>
> **What it still supports:** the *structure* of variability (below). **What it does NOT support:** any claim that a higher synergy index means better command. The command evidence is item #4 and `stuff-and-command.md` §8.3–8.4 — different papers, much smaller samples.
>
> *If a future cycle encounters the old "skilled pitchers / command" framing elsewhere, it is this error propagating. Do not re-import it.*

**Finding (corrected):** **higher-velocity** pitchers show both lower absolute between-trial joint variability *and* a higher uncontrolled-manifold synergy index (partial ρ = 0.22, p < 10⁻⁴⁷ at release) — their variability is preferentially confined to directions that don't move the release point. Convergence is **segment-staggered**, not a monotonic decline: trunk and pelvis reach their variability minimum **before foot plant**, the shoulder only at MER. Proportional funnel depth is **invariant across skill levels** — higher-velocity pitchers have a lower absolute variability *floor*, not a different convergence shape. **Trunk degrees of freedom carry only ≈5% of release-preserving variance**; the synergy is an arm-chain phenomenon over a stable proximal base.

**VERDICT: PROMISING, with the preprint caveat.** Not peer-reviewed; ρ = 0.22 is a modest effect; and this is Driveline analyzing Driveline's own athletes with its own markerless pipeline. But the sample size is an order of magnitude beyond anything else, the method (UCM) is well-established outside baseball (Scholz & Schöner 1999, *Exp Brain Res* 126:289–306), and the conclusion is directly coachable: **stop trying to make him a machine; make the *release* invariant and let the joints negotiate.** Genuinely new to this library.

### 4. Command has a biomechanical signature — and it's repeatability at foot plant, adjustability everywhere else
**Source:** Driveline Baseball (Feb 2026). *The Interaction of Biomechanics and Command.* [Driveline](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2026/02/the-interaction-of-biomechanics-and-command/) — winner of the 2025 SABR Dr. Mike Marshall Pitching Biomechanics Research Award.

**Sample:** 27 pitchers, college → independent → affiliate → MLB. 270 throws. Intended Zone Tracker + Launchpad biomechanics. Joint angles/velocities at PKH, FP, MER, BR.

**Findings:** several features correlated |r| > 0.5 with miss distance — glove-shoulder abduction and torso lateral tilt at FP were called out. 10 PCs explained 80% of variance; **negative correlations dominated, leading the authors to conclude "adjustability was at least as important as repeatability."** Repeatability mattered at FP; adjustability mattered in angular velocities at peak knee height and in shoulder horizontal abduction / pronation rate later.

**VERDICT: PROMISING.** Small n, blog-published (not peer-reviewed), and the PCA interpretation is soft. But it converges with #3 from a completely different method, and it gives me a concrete drill (variable-tempo bullpens converging to one FC position). **Neither library file addresses command at all — this is the biggest coverage gap in the program.**

### 5. Lower arm slot: now quantified on both sides of the ledger
**Sources:**
- *Arm slot angles affect elbow and shoulder joint torque in elite college pitchers.* *Sports Biomechanics* 24(8), n = 66 (OpenBiomechanics). [PubMed 39744973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39744973/)
- Sawchik T / Driveline (Mar 2026). *Pitchers are going low — and for good reason. But it's not for everyone.* [Driveline](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2026/03/pitchers-are-going-low-and-for-good-reason-but-its-not-for-everyone/)
- MLB Statcast arm-angle metric (public since 2024); deGrom at 30° in 2025, his lowest in five tracked years.

**Numbers:** ~**4.23 N·m more elbow varus torque per 10° of more-overhand slot** at consistent velocity (n = 66 elite college); MLB release points ~2 in lower than 2016, arm angle down 1.41° since 2020; pitchers dropping slot 2°+ gained **+2.14 run value** and **+18.3 rpm** on the four-seam at a cost of **~0.15 mph.**

**VERDICT: PROMISING — and the best velocity-cost-to-stress-benefit ratio in the entire library.** Contraindications are real and named: requires wrist radial-deviation mobility to hold spin efficiency; Skenes tried and reverted; and the relationship **inverts in high schoolers** (lower slot → higher elbow flexion torque). This confirms and quantifies the biomechanist's claim that pros throw from a lower slot at lower torque — see cross-examination in `daily/2026-08-12-coach.md`.

### 6. Torque numbers are not comparable across labs — and now we know exactly why
**Source:** Dowling B, Slowik JS, Fleisig GS (2026). *The influence of sample and filter frequencies on baseball pitching data.* *Sports Biomechanics*, Feb 2026. [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14763141.2026.2625797)

Published studies sample at 240–500 Hz with low-pass filter cutoffs varying widely (13.4 and 18.0 Hz most common), and those choices materially change the computed data. Since inverse-dynamics torque depends on the second derivative of position, filter cutoff is not a detail — it is a large fraction of the answer.

**VERDICT: PROMISING (methodological).** This is the mechanism behind the library's unresolved 64 vs 100 vs 120 N·m spread. **New operating rule: a torque value without its sampling rate and filter cutoff is not a number.**

### 7. The kick-change — pitch design's current center of gravity
**Sources:** [Baseball America](https://www.baseballamerica.com/stories/5-mlb-pitchers-who-have-added-a-kick-change-baseballs-trendiest-new-pitch-for-2025/), [Lookout Landing](https://www.lookoutlanding.com/2025/2/28/24367452/what-the-kick-change-is-pitch-of-2025-andres-munoz-brian-bannister-supination-pronation), [ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/mlb/story/_/id/45007922/mlb-2025-kick-change-changeup-new-york-mets-holmes-canning-megill)

Spike the middle finger onto a seam off a two-seam grip; the finger "kicks" the ball at release, tilting the axis forward and producing splitter-like depth at higher velocity than a conventional change. 2024 was the sweeper, 2025 multiple fastballs, 2026 is the kick-change (Birdsong → Holmes, Megill, Canning).

**VERDICT: PROMISING, and — importantly — low stress cost.** This is a *grip and release* change, not a mechanics change. Note the counterweight from the anatomy file: MLB pitch-tracking studies associate increased changeup velocity and increased spin/movement with UCLR odds. **The industry is chasing exactly the pitch characteristics that the injury-association studies flag.** Say that out loud to any athlete adding one.

### 8. Computer-vision seam/spin-axis extraction for pitch design
**Source:** Driveline Baseball (Apr 2026). *Investigating a mystery pitch as a test case for a new (computer) vision for pitch design.* [Driveline](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2026/04/investigating-a-mystery-pitch-computer-vision-pitch-design/)

CNN trained on thousands of labeled pitch images extracts spin axis and seam orientation from ordinary video — used to reverse-engineer Tatsuya Imai's arm-side-moving "backwards slider," which he throws by getting *underneath* the ball. Pitched (correctly) as democratizing seam-orientation work that currently requires Hawk-Eye or Trackman 3D-spin cameras.

**VERDICT: PROMISING but early / partly MARKETING.** No published accuracy validation against Hawk-Eye or Rapsodo seam-orientation output. Single case study. The seam-shifted-wake physics underneath it is solid (A. Smith & B. Smith, Utah State, 2021), but SSW itself is already mainstream and is *not* new. **What's new is cheap seam measurement, and that would be a real unlock if it validates.**

### 9. Constraint-design heuristics as a framework rather than a drill list
**Source:** Crider B / Driveline Baseball (Feb 2025). *Constraint Based Learning Heuristics for Throwers.* [Driveline](https://www.drivelinebaseball.com/2025/02/constraint-based-learning-heuristics-for-throwers/)

Four levers: implement **mass**, **moment of inertia** (size/shape), **center-of-mass kinematics** (mound vs flat, pre-throw velocity), and **distal limb kinematics** (preset arm positions). Organizing principle: shape the force-time curve to maximize impulse — *"manipulating time is the most valuable tool you have."*

**VERDICT: PROMISING as a framework, UNPROVEN as specific dosing.** No kinetics published for most of these constraints at 85+. But it lets me *design* a constraint instead of borrowing one, which is a real upgrade. Adopted into `coaching-translation.md` §7.

### 10. Weighted balls: "no increase in arm stress" is a half-truth
**Sources:** Serio N, *Applied Baseball Science* Substack (Mar 2026). [velou.substack.com](https://velou.substack.com/p/weighted-balls-dont-increase-arm); underlying study O'Connell et al. (2022), 26 collegiate/pro pitchers, five ball weights 85–198 g. [PubMed 35981710](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35981710/)

The claim circulating widely: weighted balls don't raise elbow varus torque or shoulder IR moment. **What the study actually measured: the concentric acceleration phase only.** Deceleration — where the shoulder sees ~114 %BW of distraction — was not measured. The paper also doesn't specify plyoball vs hard leather, tested only experienced throwers, and was a single acute session.

**VERDICT: UNPROVEN, trending toward MARKETING when quoted without the caveat.** Serio states his own caveats honestly; the claim as it propagates on social media does not. This does not overturn the Reinold 2018 24%-injury-rate signal, which involved **olecranon stress fractures and UCL injuries** — deceleration- and cumulative-load injuries that a concentric-phase study cannot speak to.

### 13. The MLB pitcher-injury report — consensus document, not evidence
**Source:** MLB's 62-page report on pitcher injuries (Dec 2024), based on interviews with 200+ former pitchers, surgeons, ATCs, club officials, biomechanists, agents. [MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/news/mlb-releases-report-on-pitcher-injuries-2024) · [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/mlb/story/_/id/43026688/mlb-study-identifies-factors-rise-pitching-injuries)

Blames velocity, "stuff" optimization, and max-effort pitching. Notes average MLB fastball rose from 91.3 (2008) → 94.2 (2024) → **94.6 mph (2026)**.

**VERDICT: UNPROVEN as an intervention document; useful as a description of consensus.** It is an interview-derived report, not a study, and it offers few concrete answers. Its usefulness is that it establishes what the industry's most powerful actor believes — which shapes what gets funded and what rules get considered. Note the tension with the library's strongest prospective evidence: **in 305 professionals followed 4.5 years, fastball velocity did NOT differ between the UCL-surgery and healthy groups (85.0 vs 84.7 mph, p = .604); torque did.** MLB's report blames velocity; the best prospective data blames torque. Those are not the same claim.

### 14. Field-level replication that forces a concession: the kinematic sequence really is that variable
**Sources:** Scarborough DM, et al. (2020). *Kinematic sequence patterns in the overhead baseball pitch.* *Sports Biomechanics* 19(5) — **n = 22 pitchers, 208 pitches, 14 distinct sequence patterns, NOT ONE fully proximal-to-distal**, fewer than 10% of pitchers used a single pattern, and **variability was similar in high-school pitchers and professionals.** [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14763141.2018.1503321) A further sample of 30 pitchers / 249 fastballs produced **17 patterns.**

**VERDICT: PROMISING → effectively ESTABLISHED.** I came into this sweep intending to attack the biomechanist's n=14 sequence finding as too small to act on. It replicates in two larger independent samples. **I concede.** See `open-disputes.md` — this one moves *off* the dispute list.
