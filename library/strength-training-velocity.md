# Strength Training and Fastball Velocity at 85+

**Deep research pass, 2026-08-17.** 108 agents, 5 search angles, 25 falsifiable claims extracted and put through 3-vote adversarial verification. **19 confirmed, 6 refuted.** Every surviving citation resolved to a real journal record with matching DOI/PMID.

Population scope: elite HS showcase / D1 / MiLB / MLB, 85 mph floor. Off-population studies are included but flagged, never used as norms.

---

## The headline

**For a pitcher already throwing 85+, there is essentially no intervention evidence that squats, trap-bar deadlifts, Olympic lifts, single-leg work, medicine-ball rotational work, or plyometrics add fastball velocity.**

Not "weak evidence." Not "mixed evidence." **Nobody has run the study.** Every number routinely cited to justify lifting for velocity at this level is cross-sectional correlation — somebody measured strong pitchers and found they throw hard.

---

## What the evidence actually is

### The correlations (real, but nobody manipulated anything)

| Finding | Sample | Design |
|---|---|---|
| CMJ concentric impulse r=0.71, peak power r=0.68 | n=19 D1 (Sakurai 2024, PMID 38900174) | Cross-sectional, single session |
| Drop jump R²=0.30; **CMJ R²=0.10 p=0.28 and squat jump R²=0.07 p=0.44 both NULL** | n=53 professionals (Wong 2023, JSCR 37(4):823-828) | Cross-sectional |
| Wingate peak power r=0.44; CMJ peak power r=0.43 | n=33 D1 (King 2025, PMID 39446825) | Cross-sectional |
| Whole assessment battery adds **6 percentage points** over height/weight/age's 36% | n=379 elite HS PDP (Feng 2025, PMID 38190251) | Cross-sectional |

**None had an intervention, a control group, or randomization.**

### The size confound — the most important finding here

In the largest D1 dataset with body composition (King 2025, n=33):

- **Body mass r=0.58, p=0.0004** — the strongest single predictor
- Lean mass r=0.52 — second
- Wingate peak power r=0.44, CMJ peak power r=0.43 — behind both
- Adding power to body mass moved total variance only **~32% → ~34%**

And the nulls: **CMJ power per kilogram r=0.19, jump height r=0.07, Sparta Score r=−0.06** — all non-significant.

Sakurai shows the same structure: lean and body mass correlate r=0.71–0.83 with pitching linear momentum and r=0.71/0.81 with CMJ concentric impulse.

**The "power" signal is substantially a size signal.** Absolute impulse carries it; divide by body mass and it disappears. Nobody has randomized pitchers to gain mass and measured mph.

### The intervention studies that exist

**Escamilla 2012 (PMID 22549085)** — the only randomized controlled baseball velocity trial with a true no-training control. n=68 randomized four ways (Throwers Ten / Keiser pneumatic / plyometric / control), 3×/wk × 6 wk. Gains: **TT +1.7%, KP +1.2%, PLY +2.0%**, control not significantly different. Between-group differences **not** significant.
⚑ SAMPLE MISMATCH: 14–17 y/o, all positions not pitchers, crow-hop throws, no absolute mph reported. At a ~65–75 mph baseline that's roughly **1 mph**.

**Lambert 2023 (PMID 36933646)** — the one Level I RCT in NCAA D1A pitchers, with CONSORT diagram and block randomization, n=28, 8 weeks layered on a real squat/RDL/single-leg/bench/row/deadlift S&C program. **It never measured ball velocity.** Full-text search for "mph", "m/s", "ball velocity", "radar", "TrackMan", "Rapsodo" returns zero hits. Strong design, contributes nothing to this question.

**Fredriksen & van den Tillaar 2024 (Sports Med Open 10:122)** — the systematic review of controlled overarm-throwing training. Its 17 studies are all throwing-implement work (weighted balls, forearm loading, pulleys, bands). General strength training was **excluded by design**, verbatim. So there is no review-level answer either.

## What has no answer at all

No verified study reports mph gain from a barbell lift, an Olympic lift, a trap-bar deadlift, single-leg work, or a med-ball program in a cohort **documented to throw 85+**. Specifically unanswered:

- **Which lifts transfer** — untested
- **What loads, volumes, frequencies** — no study prescribed and measured them against mph
- **Adaptation timeline** — untested
- **Off-season vs in-season periodization for velocity** — no study compares them
- **Diminishing returns above 85 mph** — no study stratifies response by baseline velocity. This is an *inference* from restriction-of-range statistics, not a tested moderation.
- **Whether LOSING strength costs velocity** — rests entirely on one uncontrolled n=12 pre-post (Gdovin 2025), which cannot separate strength loss from concurrent throwing-volume loss, seasonal timing, or regression to the mean. No controlled detraining trial in pitchers was found.

## Claims REFUTED on adversarial vote — do not carry forward

1. **"Underload gains are 11–12% in novices vs 2–3% in experienced throwers."** Refuted 0–3. Do not cite as review-level support for diminishing returns at 85+.
2. **Reinold 2018's +3.3% velocity gain attributed cleanly to weighted balls with strength held constant.** Refuted 0–3 — mischaracterizes the study arms.
3. **Reinold 2018's "24% injury rate vs zero in a lifting-only control" as a safety argument for lifting.** Refuted 0–3 — same problem. (For scale: that cohort threw **67.8 mph**, ~17 mph below our population.)
4. **Escamilla 2012's null between-group result as evidence against modality-specific transfer.** Refuted 0–3 — over-reads a null at n≈17/arm.
5. **Feng 2025's 6% as a hard ceiling on strength's explanatory power.** Split 1–2 — present as suggestive, not as a bound.
6. **Luera 2020 as *demonstrating* restriction of range.** A single homogeneous sample cannot demonstrate it. Say "consistent with." Also: those are kinematic variables, so extending the collapse to strength–velocity correlations is argument by statistical analogy, not direct evidence.

## Honest reading for a coach

The defensible position is narrow and worth stating precisely:

**Bigger, more powerful pitchers throw harder.** That is well established and replicated. **Whether making a given pitcher bigger and more powerful makes *him* throw harder is untested at this level.** Those are different claims, and the literature only supports the first.

This does not mean stop lifting. It means the honest justification is general athletic development, durability, and the mass/impulse association — not a demonstrated velocity return. Anyone quoting you an mph figure from a lifting program at 85+ is quoting something that does not exist.

**What would settle it:** a randomized controlled trial in pitchers documented at 85+, with a real comparison arm, a prescribed and reported load/volume/frequency, throwing volume held constant across arms, and mph as the primary outcome. Nobody has run it.

## Fabrication check

**None detected this round.** Every cited paper resolved to a real journal record — verified across PMC, SpringerLink, LWW/Ovid, Human Kinetics, NCBI eutils, Europe PMC, and in one case the first author's MS thesis (matching n and demographics). No retractions or errata.

Note: the previously-flagged fabricated grip-strength correlation (r=0.705) did not reappear — and the real Feng 2025 finding is its **opposite**: grip strength did *not* correlate with pitch metrics.
