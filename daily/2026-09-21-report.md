## 0. THE ONE-PARAGRAPH RESULT

Finger–ball friction is a **genuine, manipulated, within-athlete lever on spin rate** — the only one this corpus has ever registered (F-425). Destroying it costs **−28.8% of ball angular velocity, ≈ −620 rpm** (F-425), the ball slips **21.6 mm instead of 8.9 mm** across the fingertip at release (F-426), and control degrades in a specific, diagnosable direction: the ball leaves **up and to the arm side** (F-430). **But the lever is one-directional, and this is the finding that matters to Tommy.** The manipulation that works is *removing* friction; the manipulation that adds it does almost nothing. Read at source, rosin **does not reliably raise the friction coefficient on a dry hand at all** — it raises it only when the hand is *wet*, and its real and replicated effect is to **cut the variance** of friction within and between people (F-427). So the honest statement is: **rosin is not a spin supplement, it is a variance damper, and the 620 rpm figure is the penalty for a soaked hand, not the bonus for a chalked one.** Both pitching experiments ran at **130 km/h = 80.8 mph with velocity instructed constant** (F-428) — below this program's floor and confounded by the instruction. Everything about finger *strength* remains a marker, not a lever: the one study correlating finger force with spin is a **77.9 mph sample** in which **hand grip strength was null** (F-431). The cycle's sharpest new thread is that pinch strength falls **22–24% by pitch 100** in collegiate arms (F-432) — which predicts a within-outing decay of the friction-dependent part of spin that **nobody in any sport has measured** (F-433).

## 1. THE ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS — what actually makes a ball spin

Spin is not "wrist snap." It is a **tangential impulse applied at a moment arm**, delivered in the last ~8 ms before release, and it is **friction-limited at the fingertip**.

| Requirement | Tissue / physics | Is it trainable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal force** into the ball (fingers pressing) | FDS, FDP, intrinsics; pinch, not crush | Plausibly — but see F-431, the evidence is a 77.9 mph correlation |
| **Coefficient of friction** at the skin–leather interface | Skin moisture, hardness, leather finish, any applied agent | **Directly and instantly manipulable — this is the lever (F-425)** |
| **Tangential force** = μ × normal force | The product is what spins the ball | Only through the two rows above |
| **Seam catch** — the finger hooking a raised seam | Geometric; a mechanical stop that defeats slip | **Unstudied as a skill. The real open question (F-426)** |

**The anatomist's position.** Friction is the *only* term in this table that a pitcher can change in one second, at zero physiological cost, with no adaptation timeline. That is extraordinarily rare in this corpus — eighteen prior cycles have mostly found capacity channels that a 90 mph arm has already saturated. **But the asymmetry is brutal.** The interface in a healthy, dry-handed pitcher is already operating near the friction it needs; that is why removing friction is catastrophic and adding it is nearly inert (F-427). The correct mental model is a **floor, not a ceiling**: friction is a precondition you can fall below, not a resource you can stockpile.

**One line on injury, then the work moves on.** Both source papers note that the alternative to reducing centrifugal force under slip is to *squeeze harder*, loading the forearm flexors — the same FDS that provides dynamic valgus stability (F-432's rationale). Flagged; not this program's topic.

## 2. THE BIOMECHANICS PASS — six papers, read at the source

### 2.1 ⭐ The manipulation — friction changed, spin measured (F-425, F-426)

**Yamaguchi T, et al. (2025).** *Impact of slip distance between fingertips and ball on baseball pitching performance under different friction conditions.* **Sci Rep 15:9514.** PMCID **PMC11950353**, PMID 40148381, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-93632-y. **Read in full 2026-09-21** via the PMC OA bucket.

n = **6** semiprofessional male pitchers, Japanese company league, age 29.8 ± 5.8, 5 RHP / 1 LHP, all overhand. Four conditions in randomised order — **no application, water, rosin powder, pine resin** — five 4-seam fastballs each, 20 pitches per man, new ball and hands washed between conditions. Fingertips filmed at **2000 fps**; TrackMan for velocity, spin and arrival location.

**Ball angular velocity at release (ω_ball_max):**

| Condition | ω_ball_max (rad/s) | **converted (rpm)** | vs rosin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 160.6 ± 27.9 | **1,534 ± 266** | **−28.8%**, p = 0.005 |
| Rosin powder | 225.7 ± 10.2 | **2,155 ± 97** | — |
| Pine resin | 225.7 ± 19.4 | **2,155 ± 185** | −28.9% vs water, p = 0.005 |

Friedman χ²(3) = 16.200, p = 0.001, N = 6, W = 0.900. **The rpm column is this cycle's conversion (× 60/2π), not the authors' — they report rad/s throughout.**

**The mechanism, and it is clean (F-426).** Total slip distance of the ball across the index fingertip: **water 21.6 ± 5.3 mm vs rosin 8.9 ± 3.5 mm (p = 0.022) vs pine resin 8.2 ± 2.2 mm (p = 0.002)**. Decisively: **ω_fc — the motion of the fingertip relative to the ball centre — was NOT affected by friction (χ²(3) = 0.200, p = 0.978).** The finger does the same thing in every condition. What changes is whether the *ball* comes with it. In every condition but water, the finger **catches a seam** and the ball stops slipping mid-release; under water, slip runs continuously to release and **no seam catch occurs at all**.

> **This is the most mechanistically satisfying result the corpus has logged in a month.** It cleanly separates *what the athlete does* from *what the equipment interface permits*, and it says the athlete's contribution is invariant. Whatever friction is doing, it is not doing it by changing the delivery.

### 2.2 ⭐ The counterintuitive one — rosin does not do what everybody says (F-427)

**Yamaguchi T, Yamakura N, Murata S, Fukuda T, Nasu D (2020).** *Effects of rosin powder application on the frictional behavior between a finger pad and baseball.* **Front Sports Act Living 2:30.** PMCID **PMC7739770**, PMID 33345022, DOI 10.3389/fspor.2020.00030. **Read in full 2026-09-21.**

n = **10 young adult males — NOT pitchers.** Index finger slid across baseball leather bonded to a force sensor. The authors state in their own abstract that this **"was not a real baseball pitching situation."** Verbatim finding:

> *"For most participants, the friction coefficient was **not necessarily increased** by the presence of rosin powder at the finger pad–leather sheet interface **under dry conditions**. However, **under wet conditions**, rosin powder application increased the friction coefficient compared with the non-powdered condition in the large normal force condition."*

And the effect that *did* replicate: *"rosin powder application **stabilizes** friction under both dry and wet conditions; that is there was **less dependence of the friction coefficient on the normal force and less variation in the friction coefficient among individuals.**"*

**Read plainly: on a dry hand, rosin buys you roughly nothing in friction magnitude. It buys you consistency.** That is a different product than the one the sport thinks it is buying, and it changes the outcome variable you should measure (§3, Q1).

### 2.3 The compensation — pitchers hide the loss in their arm path (F-429)

**Yamaguchi T, Suzuki S, Suzuki S, Nishi T, Fukuda T, Nasu D (2025).** *Effect of finger–ball friction on upper limb movement during fastball pitching in baseball.* **Sci Rep 15:27759.** PMCID **PMC12310948**, PMID 40739159, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-12298-8. **Read in full 2026-09-21.**

n = **8** semiprofessional pitchers (same lab, same protocol, overlapping cohort), 39-marker OptiTrack at 360 fps, SPM1D paired t-tests across foot contact → release.

- Ball speed **and** spin rate significantly lower under water (both p = 0.012, r = 0.630).
- **Pitching radius significantly GREATER under low friction** — 38–52% (p = 0.006) and 62–90% (p < 0.001) of normalised time.
- Centrifugal-force index (v²/R) significantly **lower** under low friction at 70–84% (p < 0.001).
- Hand velocity trended lower but **was not significant anywhere** (p > 0.05).

Interpretation the authors give, and it is supported by their own exit questionnaire (*"some participants answered that they pitched the ball by pushing their arms forward in the low-friction condition"*): **a pitcher who feels the ball slipping lengthens his radius and slows his hand to reduce the centrifugal force trying to throw the ball out of his fingers.** He protects the grip by de-tuning the delivery.

⚠️ **And the individual differences are the coaching content.** In PMC11950353, participant **f** had small slip in *every* condition including water and **lost no performance at all**; participants **b** and **d** swung wildly with condition. **Friction sensitivity is an individual trait, and it is measurable in twenty pitches.**

### 2.4 The finger-strength literature is a marker, and its sample is 77.9 mph (F-431)

**Yeh M-C, et al. (2024).** *Using a sensor-embedded baseball to identify finger characteristics related to spin rate and pitching velocity in pitchers.* **Sensors 24:3523.** PMCID **PMC11175286**, PMID 38894314. **Read in full 2026-09-21.**

n = 21 pitchers, Taiwan, mean age 20, 9 years training. **Table 1, read at source:**

- **Fastball velocity 125.33 ± 6.49 km/h = 77.9 ± 4.0 mph, range 113.5–138.5 km/h = 70.5–86.1 mph.**
- **Spin rate 1,751 ± 171 rpm** (range 1,422–2,056). League four-seam mean is 2,313 (F-143).

🚨 **SAMPLE MISMATCH — DIRECTIONAL ONLY. This is literally the corpus's standing warning made flesh: "collegiate" here means 77.9 mph, and only the extreme top of the range touches the 85 floor.**

Reported: finger length discrepancy, two- and three-point pinch, index/middle finger RFD, middle-finger impulse and force discrepancy correlate with spin and velocity at **r = 0.49–0.58, p = 0.01–0.05**. **Hand grip strength correlated with nothing.**

**Three kills on this paper, in order of severity:**
1. **Every reported p-value falls in 0.01–0.05, across ~20+ tested pairings.** At n = 21, r = 0.49 is p ≈ 0.024. **Bonferroni for even 10 tests requires r ≈ 0.62. Not one of these survives.** This is a fishing expedition reported as a finding list.
2. **Restriction of range runs BACKWARDS here, and it inflates.** The sample spans 70.5–86.1 mph — an SD of 4.0 mph, far wider than any 85+ staff. Per the standing Luera 2020 constraint (n = 149 pros, r = .17–.29), **these correlations should be expected to collapse toward zero in Tommy's population.**
3. **The abstract states "Finger length and strength were measured as dependent variables. Spin rate and velocity were independent variables."** That is backwards, and it is a tell about the care taken.

**The one genuinely useful thing in it is the null:** gross hand-grip strength — the thing sold by every grip gadget — **was unrelated to spin and velocity even in a sample this permissive.** Registered as F-431.

### 2.5 The within-outing thread — and its confound (F-432)

**Yasui Y, Tsukada K, Yonezawa K, Hagihara T, Nakagawa T, Kawano H, Miyamoto W (2026).** *Paper 58. Pitch Count Alone Is Not Enough: Age-Dependent Pinch Strength Change and Associated Factors in Youth Baseball Pitchers.* **Orthop J Sports Med 14(8 suppl 6)**, AOSSM Annual Meeting 2026. PMCID **PMC13530651**, DOI 10.1177/2325967126S00315. **Read in full 2026-09-21 — and "in full" means the abstract, because that is all that exists.**

15 elementary (age 12), 15 high school (17), **10 collegiate (19)**, Teikyo University, 2021–2024. Elementary threw 70 fastballs, older cohorts 100. Pinch strength measured **every 10 pitches**.

| Cohort | Decline onset | Pulp / tip at 100 pitches |
|---|---|---|
| Elementary (12) | **none through 70** | — |
| High school (17) | ~60 pitches | **−16% / −14%** |
| **Collegiate (19)** | **10–20 pitches** | **−22% / −24%** |

Group × pitch-count interaction p < 0.01. Velocity correlated with pulp decline **r = −0.61 (p < 0.001)**; spin rate **r = −0.64 (p < 0.001)**. Muscle stiffness, fatigue VAS and Borg scale correlated with **nothing**.

⚠️ **THE CONFOUND, AND IT IS FATAL TO THE HEADLINE.** The abstract says "exploratory correlation analyses" and **never states that they were run within-group.** Pooled across three cohorts, the slowest throwers (elementary) are also the ones who do not decline, and the fastest (collegiate) decline most. **An r = −0.64 between velocity and pinch decline across a pooled age gradient is, on the evidence presented, an age effect wearing a velocity label.** The claim "harder throwers fatigue their fingers more" **is not established by this design**, and the within-collegiate correlation — the only one that would speak to Tommy's population — is not reported.

⚠️ Also: **conference abstract, no full paper, no velocity value reported anywhere.** Japanese collegiate; the comparable Taiwanese collegiate sample in §2.4 threw 77.9 mph. **Treat the −22% magnitude as a real measurement on an unknown-velocity population, and the velocity correlation as unestablished.**

**What survives, and it is a lot:** in a collegiate cohort, **pinch strength — the exact tissue that supplies the normal force in μ × F_normal — falls by roughly a fifth to a quarter across 100 pitches, starting inside the first 20.** That is a measured within-athlete decay of a term in the spin equation.

### 2.6 The mud, for completeness (F-437)

**Pradeep S, et al. (2024).** *Soft matter mechanics of baseball's Rubbing Mud.* **PNAS 121:e2413514121.** PMCID **PMC11588067**. **Read in full 2026-09-21.** Real paper, real journal. Mudded leather shows **roughly double the adhesive force (≈20 nN vs ≈10 nN)** of clean leather and elevated μ that rises with sliding speed until sand grains shear off. *"Spreads like skin cream, and grips like sandpaper."* **Irrelevant as a lever for Tommy** — ball preparation is not in a college pitching coach's control — but it establishes that **the ball side of the interface is a real variable**, which matters for §4's dispute about ball-to-ball variation.

## 3. THE COACH INTERROGATES THE OTHER TWO

**Q1 — "You just told me destroying friction costs 620 rpm. So what does rosin BUY my guy on a dry day in Oxford?"**

**Anatomist + biomechanist, jointly: close to nothing in spin, and that is the headline.** F-427 is explicit that on a dry hand rosin does not reliably raise μ. The 620 rpm in F-425 is the gap between a **soaked** hand and a chalked one, and the no-application condition sat between them. **Anyone quoting −28.8% as rosin's upside has inverted the study.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Rosin isn't grip juice. It's a thermostat. On a dry day it does nothing for your spin and I don't care if you use it. On a humid night in June, it's the difference between your slider and a hanger."*
> **The drill is:** a **deliberate wet-hand bullpen** — 10 pitches with a lightly dampened throwing hand, no rosin; then 10 with the bag. Not to add spin; to let him feel the slip signature and learn that the bag is the fix.
> **On video the failure looks like:** the fastball leaving **up and to the arm side**, with a visibly **longer, pushed arm path** — he is lengthening the radius to protect the grip (F-429, F-430). If you see a guy's arm suddenly get long and his misses go glove-side-high in the 6th on a humid night, **check his hand before you check his mechanics.**
> **Here's how we know it's working:** see Q3.

**Q2 — "Should I buy the grip trainers? Everybody's selling them."**

**No, and the best evidence against them is inside the study the sellers cite.** F-431: in a sample permissive enough to span 70–86 mph, **hand grip strength correlated with neither spin nor velocity.** The pinch and finger-RFD correlations that *were* significant are (a) uncorrected across 20+ tests, (b) cross-sectional — **nobody has ever trained finger strength in a pitcher and measured spin**, and (c) drawn from a 77.9 mph sample where range inflation is doing unknown work.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Nothing. I'm not adding a finger program."* **This is a marker, not a lever, and it is not close.**
> **What I would actually watch:** F-432's pinch decline is the more interesting use of the same tissue — not as something to train, but as something to **measure** (Q4).

**Q3 — "Give me a measurement I can actually run this fall."**

**The rosin A/B, and it must be powered for VARIANCE, not for the mean — because F-427 says the mean is where this effect is *not*.**

Protocol: same pitcher, same session, same device, blocks alternated **rosin / no-rosin / rosin / no-rosin** (never one block of each — that confounds with fatigue, and F-432 says fatigue is moving inside 20 pitches).

Detection arithmetic, computed this cycle at 80% power, α = 0.05, two-sided, for a **mean** four-seam spin difference:

| within-pitcher spin SD | to detect 30 rpm | 50 rpm | 75 rpm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 rpm | 63 / condition | 23 | 10 |
| 80 rpm | 112 / condition | 40 | 18 |
| 100 rpm | 174 / condition | 63 | 28 |

⚠️ **The corpus does not hold a within-pitcher four-seam spin SD for an 85+ arm — that is a new registered gap.** The table is bracketed for that reason. **And the honest prediction is that the mean test comes back NULL** (F-427), so it is the cheap half.

**The variance test is the real one and it is expensive:** detecting a ~20% reduction in spin SD needs on the order of **200 pitches per condition**. That is a season of charting, not a bullpen. **Say so out loud rather than running 30 pitches and believing the answer.**

> **Here's how we know it's working:** on the humid-day subset, **the SD of spin rate and the SD of vertical release angle narrow under rosin, while the means do not move.** If the mean moves, be suspicious — that is not what the source predicts.

**Q4 — "Is there anything here that changes how I read the 6th inning?"**

**Yes, and it is this cycle's best new idea — and it is a prediction, not a finding.** Put F-432 next to F-425: pinch strength (the normal-force term) falls **22–24% by pitch 100** in collegiate arms, and spin is **μ × normal force** applied tangentially. That predicts the **friction-dependent component of spin should decay within an outing**, and should decay **faster on a humid night**, when μ is already compromised.

**Nobody has measured it.** Registered as F-433 and as a coverage gap. It is, notably, **free to measure** — every program's TrackMan already logs spin rate against pitch number.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing yet. **This is not ready to be a cue.**
> **What I do is chart it:** spin rate by pitch number, split by game-day humidity, across a season. **If spin decays with pitch count and the decay is steeper on humid nights, that is a real finding this program could publish.** If it is flat, F-433 dies and the corpus is better for it.

## 4. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**① Biomechanist → Anatomist. "You called friction 'the only true lever in the spin literature.' It is a lever on a 6-man, 80.8 mph sample instructed to hold velocity constant. Defend the word."**

**PARTIALLY CONCEDED, DEFENDED ON DESIGN.** Conceded: n = 6 and n = 8 are tiny, the population is below the floor (F-428), and the constant-velocity instruction means **the velocity result is an artifact of the instruction, not a capacity finding** — a man told to throw 130 who feels slip will throw 125. **The velocity numbers should not be quoted at all.**

Defended: the word "lever" attaches to the **causal structure**, not the magnitude. The variable was **manipulated within-subject in randomised order with washout**, and the outcome was measured on an instrument. That is a design this corpus almost never sees. The **direction** (less friction → less spin, more slip) is supported by a mechanism (F-426), by an independent bench measurement (F-427), and by a league-scale natural experiment (F-434). **Direction: high confidence. Magnitude in an 85+ arm: unknown, and the corpus should never quote 28.8% as applying to Tommy's guys.** → **Dispute #35.**

**② Coach → Biomechanist. "You are reporting effect sizes of r = −1.369. A correlation cannot exceed 1. Why is this number in my brief?"**

**CONCEDED IN FULL — and it is worse than a typo.** PMC11950353 reports r = −1.369, 1.461, 1.187, 1.278 repeatedly. The authors acknowledge it in their own limitations — *"There were instances where the effect size r exceeded 1... such a large effect size might result from the small sample size"* — which is **not an explanation but a restatement.** The standard Wilcoxon/Dunn effect size is r = Z/√N; obtaining r > 1 means **N in the denominator was the number of participants (6) while Z came from a larger set of observations.** It is a computational error, self-reported and uncorrected in a peer-reviewed paper.

**STANDING RULE ADDED: from PMC11950353, read the p-values and the direction. Never quote an effect size.** The ranking of conditions is unaffected. Registered as **F-436**.

**③ Anatomist → Coach. "You are about to hand Tommy a rosin A/B whose own source predicts a null. Is that a protocol or a ritual?"**

**DEFENDED, WITH THE SCOPE NARROWED — and this is Dispute #33 firing again.** The anatomist is right that a mean-spin A/B on dry hands is predicted null by F-427, and running a study to confirm a null is a weak use of a fall. **But the recommendation as written is not the mean test — it is the *variance* test on the humid subset**, and F-427's replicated result is specifically a **variance** result. The protocol matches the source's own claim.

⚠️ **The anatomist's deeper charge lands, though, and it is #33 by name: this corpus has now relocated a null into a variance/command claim for a THIRD time** (F-406 sleep, F-419 pre-game, and now F-427/F-433). **Conceded as a method concern.** The defence is that this time the variance claim is *in the source*, not inferred by the corpus — Yamaguchi 2020 measured between-individual friction variation directly. **But three cycles is a pattern and it goes to #33.** → **Dispute #36.**

**④ Biomechanist → Coach, on the field sweep. "Your scouted 'pinch strength explains 40% of spin efficiency' — where is that from?"**

**CONCEDED — DOWNGRADED TO UNVERIFIED.** It traces to a blog (`velouniversity.com`), which appears to be paraphrasing a **Louisiana Tech master's thesis** (Watson C, `digitalcommons.latech.edu`). **Not read — host not reachable from this sandbox.** And the claim has drifted in transmission: the peer-reviewed study on this question (Yeh 2024, F-431) measured spin **rate**, not spin **efficiency**, and found **no grip-strength relationship at all**. Per F-159, efficiency is ~two-thirds fixed and moves via arm slot. **Verdict: UNPROVEN, and flagged as a live re-import vector.**

## 6. WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK

**One thing, and it costs nothing.**

**Run the 20-pitch friction-sensitivity screen on each arm.** 10 four-seams with a lightly dampened throwing hand, 10 with rosin, alternating in blocks of 5, TrackMan or Rapsodo running.

**Why this and not the A/B:** F-425's most under-appreciated result is the **individual differences** — participant f was immune to a soaked hand; b and d fell apart. **Friction sensitivity is a trait, it varies a lot between pitchers, and twenty pitches identifies it.** You are not measuring a group effect; you are finding out *which of your guys owns a humid-night problem.*

**What you learn, in one number per arm:** the spin gap between wet and rosin. A guy near zero has a seam-catch technique that survives a bad hand. A guy at several hundred rpm is a **June risk**, and that is actionable roster information before you ever get to June.

**How you'd know it's real:** with a within-pitcher spin SD of ~80 rpm, 10 pitches per condition detects only a gap of ~**150 rpm or larger** (from the Q3 table, extrapolated). **That is fine for this purpose** — you are screening for the big fallers, not estimating a population mean. **Do not report a 40 rpm difference from 20 pitches as anything at all.**

3. **Does ball-to-ball variation in leather finish exceed the rosin effect?** PNAS (F-437) establishes the ball side is a real variable. **If new-ball variance is larger than the agent effect, every friction A/B in this topic — including the one recommended above — is underpowered for reasons nobody has priced.**
