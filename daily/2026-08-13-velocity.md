## The one thing to know today

**There is not a single published, controlled training study whose sample mean baseline velocity is at or above 85 mph. Not one. And the two studies nearest our population are the two that found NO velocity gain.**

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** The absolutism is wrong. **Two controlled studies clear the 85 mph floor.** (1) **Ake 2016** — the study named in the table directly below — has a baseline of **87.25 ± 2.32 mph (weighted-implement group) / 86.80 ± 1.32 mph (control)**, n = 56 NCAA D1 SEC. The corpus had recorded that baseline as "not reported"; it was **in the paper**. It is the highest-velocity controlled sample in the literature, and it is still null. (2) **Lee, Choi & Jeon 2026** — n = 29 Korean collegiate, baseline **max 86.2 mph**, a 12-week rotational/medicine-ball trial — is the **first positive controlled velocity result above 85 mph** (max velocity p = .003; not randomized, comparator close to a straw man). The *structural* argument survives — the large positive effects still come from 67–75 mph samples, and there is still **no controlled trial in PROFESSIONAL pitchers with velocity as an outcome** — but "not one" must not be re-imported. See `library/velocity-development.md` §1 and F-226 (also F-023, F-024, F-034) in FINDINGS.md.

| Study | Level | Baseline | Control | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marsh 2018 (Driveline, *PeerJ*) | Collegiate + pro | **78.5 mph** | No | **No change.** 9 of 17 gained, 8 lost |
| Ake 2016 (thesis, Ole Miss) | **NCAA D1**, n = 56 | NR | Yes | **No gain, p = .071; no group diff, p = .271** |

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** The Ake 2016 baseline is **not** "NR." It is **87.25 / 86.80 mph** (n = 56 NCAA D1 SEC). See `library/velocity-development.md` §1 and F-226 in FINDINGS.md.

**Every large positive effect comes from samples averaging 67–75 mph.** The gradient runs one way:
**67 mph HS (+2.2 mph, RCT) → ages 10–17 underload (+4.8 mph, uncontrolled) → NCAA D1 (null) → collegiate/pro (null).**

Confirmed outside baseball: underload gains are **11–12% in novices vs 2–3% in experienced throwers** (Fredriksen & van den Tillaar 2024, *Sports Med Open*).

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** **This claim was REFUTED 0–3 on adversarial vote (deep pass 2026-08-17).** Do not cite the 11–12% / 2–3% split as review-level support for diminishing returns at 85+. Note also that Fredriksen & van den Tillaar 2024 (*Sports Med Open* 10:122) is a review of **throwing-implement** training only — general strength training was excluded by design — so it cannot carry a general novice-vs-experienced dose claim. The diminishing-returns idea itself remains an *inference* from restriction-of-range statistics: **no study stratifies velocity response by baseline velocity.** See `library/strength-training-velocity.md` ("Claims REFUTED on adversarial vote" #1) and F-240 in FINDINGS.md.

→ **Every mph figure you've been quoted was measured on someone slower than your athlete.** Honest prior for a mature 85+ arm: **2–3%, roughly 1.5–2.5 mph in a well-executed year, with a real chance of zero.**

## The asymmetry that should organize the program

> **Adding strength training to an 85+ arm has never been shown to add velocity in a controlled trial. Removing it demonstrably costs velocity.**

**Gdovin 2025, *JSCR* (n = 12 NCAA D1):** an 8-week fall throwing program run **without** resistance-training access produced a **significant DECREASE in ball velocity, p < .001** — while arm slot, arm velocity, and elbow varus torque were all unchanged.

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** **This is not a removal experiment and the asymmetry above does not hold as stated.** Gdovin JR, Hogan B, Williams CC (2025), *JSCR* 39(3):347-351, PMID 39495235, is titled ***"Limiting Access to Resistance Training Equipment During the Off-Season"*** and is an **uncontrolled 8-week pre-post observational study with NO control group.** The PMID, n = 12 and p < .001 are all correct; the **mph magnitude is paywalled and was never obtained**, so the corpus is quoting a p-value with no effect size. With no control arm it cannot separate strength loss from concurrent throwing-volume loss, seasonal timing, or regression to the mean — and its own data has no kinematic correlate (arm slot, arm velocity, varus torque all p > .05). **"Removing it demonstrably costs velocity" is not supported.** No controlled detraining trial in pitchers exists. The study design was inferred from the p-value; never do that. See `library/velocity-development.md` §5.5, `library/strength-training-velocity.md`, and F-039 / F-240 / F-242 in FINDINGS.md.

**The honest pitch is floor-protection, not gain.** "Lifting is what you're standing on" — not "lifting will add mph."

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** The floor-protection framing rested on Gdovin and was **largely resolved as a design artifact** (Dispute #14). The defensible position is narrower: **bigger, more powerful pitchers throw harder** (body mass r = 0.58, lean mass r = 0.52, n = 33 D1, King 2025) — but **whether making a given pitcher bigger and more powerful makes *him* throw harder is untested at 85+.** The honest justification for lifting is general athletic development, durability, and the mass/impulse association, not a demonstrated velocity return or a demonstrated floor. See `library/strength-training-velocity.md` and F-039 in FINDINGS.md.

## Physical qualities: absolute output, not relative

The jump-testing literature only looks contradictory until you sort it correctly.

| Metric family | Predicts velocity? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Body mass / lean mass** | **Yes — strongest of all** | r = 0.58 / 0.52 (n = 33 D1, King 2025) |
| **CMJ concentric impulse / absolute peak power** | **Yes** | r = 0.71 / 0.68 (n = 19 D1, Sakurai 2024) |
| **Absolute cycle-ergometer power** | **Yes, replicated** | r = 0.44 (D1) and r = 0.44 (n = 27 MiLB) |
| **Drop jump / RSI** | **Yes — the only jump quality surviving in pros** | R² = 0.30, p = .005 (n = 53 pro, Wong 2023) |
| **Stride-leg unilateral jump** | **Yes** | r ≥ 0.65 (n = 19 D1) |
| Bilateral CMJ set, in professionals | **No** | R² = 0.10, p = .28 |
| Power ÷ body mass | **No** | r = 0.19 NS |
| **Jump height** | **No** | **r = 0.07 NS** |

**Why:** momentum is mass × velocity. **Divide power by body mass and you delete the term carrying the signal.** Jump height is a mass-normalized quantity in disguise.

→ Train and test **absolute** output and **reactive strength**. Never per-kilogram. Never vertical jump height.

**Asymmetry note:** pitchers jump significantly higher on the **stride leg** than the drive leg (η² = 0.34), and stride-leg jump is the better velocity correlate. **Do not coach single-leg asymmetry toward zero.**

## The two "free" mechanical levers — mph with no torque cost

Both from large professional samples. These are the only two.

1. **Stride length toward ≥80% of body height** — **+2.0 mph per 10% BH, NO elbow-torque increase** (n = 315 pro). One contradicting collegiate null (n = 99) and a normalization dispute; treat the direction as solid, the threshold as system-specific.

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** **Stride length is a MARKER, not a lever. It is not "free mph" and should not be coached as one.** The +2.0 mph per 10% BH figure (Manzi JE et al. 2021, *J Sports Sci* 39(23):2658-2664, PMID 34240663, n = 315 professional — note **first author is Manzi, not Dowling**) is a **cross-sectional association**, and two independent groups have since manipulated stride length experimentally: **Ramsey, Crotin & White 2014** (*Hum Mov Sci* 38:185-196, PMID 25457417; randomized crossover, ±25% of self-selected stride, n = 19) found **hand and ball velocity equivalent across all conditions**; and **Matsuda 2025** (*Front Sports Act Living*, ±20%) found the pitcher's **own normal stride significantly FASTER than a lengthened one.** The %BH normalization is also disputed (Yanagisawa 2020: absolute stride r = 0.55 p = .02, but %BH r = 0.36 **p = .15, NS**). The cross-sectional association is real; its causal reading died. Demoted from recommendation #2; the "~1–1.5 mph sitting there" line was deleted from the library. See `library/velocity-development.md` §4.4/§7, `library/open-disputes.md` #13, and F-043 / F-044 / F-045 in FINDINGS.md.
>
> This also means **"the two free mechanical levers" is now one.** The pelvis-at-foot-contact finding below is unaffected by this correction, but note it is likewise cross-sectional.

2. **Open pelvis at foot contact** — **87.5 vs 85.9 mph, p = .029 (~+1.6 mph), NO torque increase** (n = 157 pro).

**Everything else with a clean effect size is paid for:** lead knee extension +1.05 mph/deg (+0.27 N·m/deg) · trunk rotation at BR +1.34 mph/10° · sagittal trunk tilt +0.81 mph/10° · early arm path +0.79 mph/30 cm.
⚠️ Lead-knee magnitude estimates differ **~23×** between studies (marker vs markerless conventions). **Take the direction, not the decimal.**

## Where velocity is produced — and lost

- **Trunk power ALONE predicts ball velocity, R² = 0.731** (n = 16 pro + 15 HS, Aguinaldo & Escamilla 2019).
- **Trunk torques are the energy SOURCE; shoulder and elbow are TRANSFER elements.** **72.7% of 329.2 J reaches the hand and ball — ~27% is lost** (Naito 2021, n = 16 collegiate).
- **Trail leg generates; lead leg transmits and brakes.**
- Shoulder IR peak power **~11,838 W** with **−201 J stored elastically** in cocking — far beyond contractile capacity. **The shoulder is a spring, not a motor.** ⚑ (Roach & Lieberman 2014, 62 mph target throws — mechanism only.)
- **GRF: the peak is the wrong measurement.** Discrete peaks are weak predictors in skilled throwers; using time-series SPM in **n = 105 high-velocity pitchers**, velocity predicted braking GRF specifically at **27–35% of foot-contact-to-release.** **Timing, not magnitude.**
- **"Get down the mound faster" is mostly wrong.** In **332 in-game D1 pitchers**, max forward COM velocity showed only a weak trend; only early "body drift" (0–1%, 4–7% of motion) mattered.

## In-season velocity RISES. It does not decay.

MLB starting pitchers, 2007–2011 five-year average by month: **Apr 90.1 → May 90.4 → Jun 90.5 → Jul 90.6 → Aug 90.7 → Sep 90.7.** **+0.6 mph across the season.**
Corroborated in MiLB: velocity rose linearly across the first 8 games (n = 12, R = 0.91, p < .001).

→ **The belief in monotonic in-season decay is wrong.** In-season lifting protects a *rising* curve. **An individual's mid-season velocity drop is a signal worth investigating, not the expected pattern.**

## The ceiling, measured

**Statcast 2021–2023, n = 1,163 pitcher-season pairs** (MLB Data Warehouse):
- **≥ +2 mph: 2.5%** · **≥ +1 mph: 12.1%** · ≤ −1 mph: 16.4% · **mean −1.15 mph**
- **By baseline: the 92 mph bin is the ONLY bin above 91 with a positive mean (+0.26). 93–97 negative. 98+ negative.**

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 — TWO ERRORS IN THE LINE ABOVE. Do not quote the mean.** (a) **The sampling unit is wrong.** n = 1,163 is **pitcher × pitch-type × consecutive-season PAIRS**, not pitcher-seasons — one pitcher can contribute several rows and the rows are **not independent**. (b) **The −1.15 mph mean is internally inconsistent with the source's own distribution** — 12.1% gaining ≥1, 16.4% losing ≥1, and 1.2% losing >2 cannot average −1.15 mph. This is a probable **source error** (single-author Substack, no code published). **Flag it; do not cite the mean.** The percentile figures (2.5% ≥ +2 mph, 12.1% ≥ +1 mph) are the usable part. See `library/velocity-development.md` §6.2 and F-073 in FINDINGS.md.

**Driveline's own ≥88 mph professional subset (n = 58): +1.35 mph mean, 41.95% gained ≥1 mph, 18.39% LOST >1 mph.** Their general population: +1.8 mph, 52% gained. **That gradient is the ceiling inside one organization's own data** — and they exclude 45+ FV prospects from their value model as *"closer to their velocity ceiling."*

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** The figure is **+0.65 mph, not +1.35.** The published cohort went **89.6 → 90.3 mph** (n = 58, mean age 23, mean 67 days of training; Driveline, 4 Apr 2019), verified two ways — the article's own summary table and its binned distribution, which reconstructs to +0.63. The **41.95% gained ≥1 mph / 18.39% LOST >1 mph** figures are correct. Also: the article describes "58 high-level athletes," **not explicitly professionals** (byline Neiswender, closing credit Aucoin). **This makes the ceiling HARSHER, not softer** — the best-resourced program in the sport moved 88+ mph arms about two-thirds of a mile an hour over roughly nine weeks, with nearly one in five going backwards. See `library/velocity-development.md` §5.1 and F-072 in FINDINGS.md.

**Explanatory power collapses with selection:** HS kinematic model **R² = 0.925** → professional (n = 337, 85.7 mph) **R² = 0.536** → largest in-game D1 sample (n = 322) **R² = 0.29, RMSE 2.70 mph — with height carrying 81.2% of variable importance.**

**Aging:** within 0.5 mph of peak ages 23–28; **starters down a full 1 mph by age 26**; ~3.75 mph career loss. **But ~40% of pitchers hold velocity essentially flat from 21 to 38 (−0.3 mph total). That 40% is the achievable target for a mature arm.**

## Overrated

- **Weighted balls for a mature arm** — both collegiate/pro studies null. Driveline's famous **+7.1 mph "MaxVelo"** figure came from a **68–72 mph junior-high cohort.**
- **Medicine ball training** — the Szymanski literature everyone cites is **high school HITTERS with bat-speed outcomes.**
- **Grip strength** — in the best population match available (**n = 87 D1 at 84.83 mph**), **no significant association with velocity.** The circulating r = 0.705 appears fabricated.
- **Trunk mobility / movement screens** — trunk rotation vs velocity **r = 0.131, p = .491** (n = 30 D1).
- **Rate of force development** — contributed nothing where max force explained 12–13%.
- **"Fix the sequence"** — timing of peak trunk rotation is retained in HS models and **drops out of the professional model entirely.** It saturates by our band.
- **Long toss as evidenced velocity work** — no training study exists; the paper everyone cites measured no velocity outcome.
