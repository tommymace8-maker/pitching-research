## 1. ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY — does this work given the tissue?

**The mechanism is the most plausible one in the whole velocity-training space, and that is exactly the problem.** F-065 (Roach & Lieberman 2014) establishes the architecture: the hip rotators supply ~30% of the power reaching the shoulder and do ~90% of the work of rotating the torso; selectively restricting torso rotation costs 5 ± 6% of ball speed. F-066 (Aguinaldo & Escamilla 2019) puts trunk power alone at R² = 0.731 against ball velocity. F-067 makes the trunk the energy *source* and the arm a transfer element. If the trunk is the generator, a loaded max-intent rotational throw is the most face-valid trunk-power exercise anyone has invented.

**Here is where I break with the coach.** Face validity is the one property this corpus has learned to distrust most. The med-ball throw's time domain is the right one (ballistic, <250 ms, max intent, no deceleration against an external resistance) and the movement plane is the right one. **But the load is in the wrong place on the force–velocity curve for a 145 g ball.** A 6-lb (2.72 kg) implement is ~19× the mass of a baseball. The pitcher is training the system to deliver impulse to a mass it will never see again, and the corpus already holds the general result for this class of error: F-035 (wearable resistance above the elbow, 6 weeks, null), and the whole weighted-implement literature where the acute velocity change and the trained velocity change dissociate.

**Where I break with the biomechanist.** He will say the r is an artifact of body mass (F-751) and therefore empty. That does not follow. **Body mass is itself a trainable quantity in this population**, and the corpus's own size confound (`strength-training-velocity.md` §"The size confound — the most important finding here") runs the other way: if the med-ball throw is partly a size test, and size is partly causal for velocity, then a correlation mediated by mass is not a spurious correlation, it is a correlation with a known mediator. It just means the exercise is not the lever — **the mass is**, and you add mass with food and a barbell, not a 6-lb ball.

**Adaptation timeline, honestly:** no med-ball trial in any population ran longer than 12 weeks, and the only one above the 85 mph floor (F-034) is a non-randomized package. Nothing in this literature can speak to a training year.

**Cost line, one sentence as the brief requires.** A max-intent rotational throw loads the obliques and the posterior shoulder in the same plane as a pitch, every coaching source in the sweep warns against introducing them early in the offseason for exactly that reason, and **no study has ever reported a workload or soreness outcome for them** — so they are an unmeasured addition to the throwing ledger, not free conditioning (Dispute #85).

### Check 2 — control group, and did anyone manipulate it?

**Four-angle sweep for "manipulated med-ball training, measured BASEBALL PITCHING velocity." All four empty.** Zero trials, in any population, at any level (F-752). What exists:

- **Manipulated, velocity outcome, wrong implement:** Raeder 2015, n = 28 female handball, 6 weeks, **+14% vs +3.7% control**, plus +15% concentric shoulder IR at 180°/s (d = 0.9). ⚠️ The intervention was **MB throws combined with regular handball throws preceded by elastic tubing** — a co-intervention, by the authors' own description. And a handball is ~425 g against a baseball's 145 g.
- **Manipulated, bat outcome, wrong population:** Szymanski 2007, n = 49, age 15.4; Kobak 2018, n = 27 prepubescent.
- **Manipulated, pitching velocity, package not isolated:** F-034 only, non-randomized.
- **Manipulated med-ball dose with a pitch-velocity outcome: zero. In any population, ever.**

So the practice is **CROSS_SECTIONAL end to end.** Taniyama is explicitly "a descriptive study to determine correlational relationship strength." Nobody has ever raised a pitcher's med-ball throw velocity and measured his fastball.

### Check 3 — does the number measure what the sentence says it measures?

Three separate problems, and together they are the finding of the day.

**(a) The interval, not the point estimate. DERIVED, source-independent.** r = 0.62 at n = 15 has a 95% CI of **r ∈ [0.16, 0.86]**, i.e. **R² ∈ [2.5%, 73.8%]** (Fisher z, SE = 1/√(n−3)). "Explains 38% of the variance" is the midpoint of an interval that runs from *nothing* to *three quarters*. And r_crit at df = 13 is **0.5139** — the result clears significance by 0.106. ⚠️ **Also: the reported p = 0.02 is consistent with df = 12 (t = 2.737), not df = 13 (t = 2.849, p ≈ 0.014).** Every retrievable summary says 15 pitchers; the p-value says 14 usable pairs. **Unresolvable without the paper** (F-749).

**(b) Restriction of range, and a prediction that landed. DERIVED.** Thorndike case-2 correction on r = 0.62, from a D-III velocity SD of 4.0–5.0 mph down to an 85+ staff's 2.0–2.5 mph:

| 85+ SD | D-III SD | corrected r | corrected R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 4.0 | 0.367 | 13.5% |
| 2.0 | 4.5 | 0.331 | 11.0% |
| 2.0 | 5.0 | 0.301 | 9.1% |
| 2.5 | 4.0 | 0.443 | 19.6% |
| 2.5 | 4.5 | 0.402 | 16.2% |
| 2.5 | 5.0 | 0.367 | 13.5% |

**Predicted band for an 85+ staff: r ≈ 0.30–0.44.** Tread Athletics, reporting its own unpublished **NAIA** data in the field sweep, gives **r ≈ 0.30–0.44** and 10–35% of variance. ⚠️ Snippet-of-a-blog, no n stated, not peer-reviewed — **but it is a vendor voluntarily reporting a SMALLER number than the published literature, which is the opposite of the usual direction, and it lands inside a band computed from Taniyama's own coefficient and nothing else.** Fourth arrival of the prediction audit (after F-406, F-004, F-132), and the fourth time it has **lowered** an estimate rather than raised one (F-750).

**(c) The test may not measure rotational power at all.** Three independent results, all snippet-level:
- Sell et al. 2015 (*Isokinetics and Exercise Science* 23:151–160): rotational MB toss ICCs 0.835–0.909 — **"excellent reliability but not related to isokinetic strength."** A test named for core strength that does not correlate with measured trunk strength.
- Body mass dominates the related overhead MB throw: **r = 0.78** with body mass in college football players; partialling body mass dropped its correlations with strength (0.59) and clean-and-jerk (0.72) but left them significant; **partialling body mass AND gender made them non-significant.** Power expressed *relative* to body mass or lean mass was **non-significant** against throw distance (r = 0.27, 0.28). In adolescent handball, **%body fat alone explained 37%** of MB-throw variance while height and body mass did not predict it significantly.
- A 2026 *Scientific Reports* paper (`s41598-026-58353-w`) proposing height-to-mass normalisation states that throw distance is **"directly confounded by body mass... systematically biasing performance rankings and talent identification protocols."**

🚨 **No med-ball-versus-pitch-velocity study has reported a partial correlation controlling for body mass.** That is one line of code on data Taniyama's authors already hold, and it is the cheapest decisive item in the topic (F-751).

### Check 4 — is the R² a geometric identity restated?

Not for Taniyama's r = 0.62 — two separate measured tasks, no shared term, no identity. **But the number the rotational-power industry actually quotes is F-066's R² = 0.731** (trunk power → ball velocity), and that one has two defects this corpus has named: it is fitted across a **13.2 mph competition-level gap** (16 professionals at 81.2 mph pooled with 15 high schoolers at 68.0 mph, n = 31), which is **F-510's Type D defect** — the exact structure Dowling 2024 showed moving a slope by ~23× — and trunk power and ball velocity are computed from overlapping angular velocities in the same trial. **F-066 is annotated today as a Type D candidate, not corrected** (F-761).

## 3. PITCHING-COACH — four pointed questions

**Q1. Half of college baseball does med-ball throws for velocity. Tell me in one sentence whether I'm wasting the time.**
You are not wasting it and you are not buying velocity with it. **There is no evidence that it adds a single mph to a baseball**, because nobody has ever tried the experiment (F-752). The defensible sentence is: *a max-intent rotational throw is a cheap, well-tolerated, ballistic trunk exercise with the right time domain and the right plane, whose transfer to a 145 g ball has never been measured in anybody.* Keep it as general ballistic work. Stop calling it a velocity driver, and stop putting an athlete through a med-ball block *instead of* the things that do have intervention evidence above the floor (F-033 lumbopelvic-hip strength, F-034's package, the strength work in `strength-training-velocity.md`).

**Q2. We test the rotational throw at intake and the number goes in the athlete's file. Is the number worth anything?**
As a **cross-sectional screen of who in the room is a big explosive athlete**, yes — with the caveat that it is likely measuring body mass as much as rotational quality (F-751). As a **per-athlete tracking variable**, almost certainly not yet, and the arithmetic says so. The only published SEMs for the rotational toss are 24.56 and 17.44 (units unstated in every retrievable source, presumably cm), which give **MDC₉₅ = 68.1 cm and 48.3 cm** — on a throw of a few metres, roughly a 10–14% change before you can say one athlete improved. And **there is no ICC, SEM or MDC anywhere for the velocity-based rotational throw** — Taniyama's actual test — **in any baseball sample** (F-755). Nineteenth entry in the F-264 / F-703 pattern.

**Q3. If it's a hitting test, say that plainly.**
Plainly: in the one study that generated the practice, the test correlated **better with bat swing velocity (r = 0.65, p = 0.003) than with pitching velocity (r = 0.62, p = 0.02)**; the authors' own stated mechanism was long-axis rotation through the hips and trunk **shared with the swing**; and every intervention in the literature whose outcome moved was a **bat** or a **handball**, never a baseball pitch (F-748). The pitching industry adopted a hitting test. That is not a reason to throw it out — it is a reason to stop quoting r = 0.62 at pitchers as though it were about them.

**Q4. Does it buy me command?**
No, and this is the rare case where somebody actually looked. **Two independent interventions that raised velocity both measured accuracy and both found nothing:** Raeder 2015 raised handball throwing velocity 14% with **throwing precision unaffected**, and F-034 raised peak pitch velocity 1.3–2.0% with **no improvement in control or accuracy in either group**. **Dispute #27's eleventh venue, and the first one where the accuracy outcome exists and is null rather than absent** (F-753).

### Translation — the four sentences

**(1) "So what I tell the pitcher is**… the med ball is trunk conditioning and it is cheap, it is not how you find three miles an hour, and we are going to find out this fall whether your number means anything at all." **The drill is** a 6-lb rotational throw for velocity, 3 max-intent reps, measured once at intake and once at the end of the block, logged **alongside body mass**. **On video the failure looks like** an arm-only throw with the back hip never clearing — the thing that makes the number go up without anything upstream changing. **Here's how we know it's working:** it isn't "working," it is being *tested* — see the audit below.

**(2) "So what I tell the pitcher is**… this throw goes on the throwing sheet, not the lifting sheet." **The failure looks like** oblique soreness on day 2 of a fall block nobody attributed to the med ball. **We know it's working when** nobody is sore from an exercise that is supposed to be free (Dispute #85).

**(3) "So what I tell the pitcher is**… the reason we lift and eat is that the thing your med-ball number is mostly measuring is how much of you there is." **The check:** body mass and the throw velocity should move together; if the throw moves and mass doesn't, you got better at the throw (F-751).

**(4) "So what I tell the pitcher is**… if you want your bat speed up, this is actually your exercise — the evidence is better for the swing than for the mound." Relevant for the two-way arms on the roster, which is a real population at this level.

## 4. THE APPLIED OUTPUT — THE MED-BALL AUDIT (F-759)

**It is free, it uses the radar gun and the scale already in the building, and it is a falsification test rather than a training block.** This is the only executable item in the topic, because the training question is unanswerable on one roster (below).

**Per pitcher, once:** (a) 6-lb rotational med-ball throw velocity, 3 max-intent reps, best kept, same surface, same warm-up, radar at a fixed distance; (b) body mass to 0.1 kg the same morning; (c) current best fastball from the existing radar log (not a fresh pen — F-706's feedback confound).

**Three analyses, all in a spreadsheet:**
1. Rank-order correlation of RMBTV against best fastball across the staff.
2. **The same correlation with body mass partialled out.** This is the one that matters.
3. Across the offseason block: Δ RMBTV against Δ fastball, within athlete.

**The prediction, stated before the data, from F-750:** on an 85+ staff the raw r will be **≈ 0.30–0.44**, not 0.62; and partialling body mass will pull it toward zero. **If it does, the test is a size test and must stop being used as a velocity diagnostic on individuals.**

**The honest power statement, which is the point of running it this way.** With 15 pitchers, r_crit at df = 13 is **0.514**. So his own staff **can falsify the strong claim as sold** (r = 0.62, 38% of variance — that size is detectable) and **cannot confirm the weak one** (r ≈ 0.35 — that size is not). Both halves get said out loud. A null at n = 15 is not evidence of no relationship; it is evidence that the published number does not reproduce here, which is all the audit claims.

**What cannot be run, and why. DERIVED.** A two-arm trial of a med-ball block for a true 1.0 mph gain, at an 85+ between-subject SD of 2.5 mph (d = 0.40), needs **99 pitchers per arm, 198 total**; at SD 2.0 mph, **63 per arm, 126 total**; at SD 3.0, **142 per arm**. A college staff has ~15. **Fifth independent arrival of the detection wall** (after F-506, F-476, F-623, F-681) and the recommendation to promote it to a standing rule now stands at five (F-758).

**MOVED, marginally — Dispute #80 (registry-wide pooled-SMD defect).** A fourth instance arrived unprompted: the AJPMR 2024 core-training meta-analysis pools **g = 0.701 [0.339, 1.063], 10 RCTs, P < 0.001** for standing throwing velocity, across handball, baseball and rehab populations, with step and jump throws **non-significant**. Converting that g to mph requires an SD from populations whose throwing velocity ranges from ~45 to ~86 mph. **Carry the raw units; discard the standardised effect.** The 2023 broader core-training meta-analysis gives **ES = 0.30, p = 0.14** on throwing/hitting velocity and a 2025 one gives a non-significant pooled effect at **I² = 93%** — the three pooled estimates disagree, which is itself the argument.
