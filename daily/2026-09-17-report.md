## §1 — THE ANATOMIST'S PASS: what is the frontal-plane hip actually doing?

The pitching delivery is usually taught in the sagittal and transverse planes — down the mound, then rotate. The frontal plane gets one sentence about "staying closed." But two of the three largest muscle groups at the hip act there.

**The trail leg.** During the stride the drive limb pushes **laterally** against the rubber. The hip abductors (gluteus medius, minimus, upper gluteus maximus via the iliotibial tract) resist contralateral pelvic drop while the athlete is in single-leg stance at maximum leg lift, and contribute to the lateral impulse that starts the body toward the plate.

**The lead leg.** After foot contact the stride limb is abducted relative to the pelvis, and the adductor group — **adductor magnus, longus, brevis, pectineus, gracilis** — acts first eccentrically and then concentrically to pull the pelvis over the front foot. This is the frontal-plane half of the block that `F-061`/`F-068` describe in the sagittal plane.

**The anatomist's substantive point, and it is a caution rather than a case:** *adductor magnus is not primarily an adductor.* Its posterior head is innervated by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve and functions as a powerful **hip extensor**. So "hip adduction strength" measured in a seated squeeze is partly indexing a hip extensor — which is the quality the sagittal-plane literature already credits (F-068: the trail hip is where generation happens). **A seated adduction dynamometer may be measuring the thing everyone already agrees matters, through a frontal-plane window, and getting credit for a new mechanism.**

**Adaptation timeline.** Eccentric adductor loading produces strength gains on the ordinary 6–10 week neural-then-structural curve, and the Copenhagen literature lands at 8 weeks (F-383, link 1). That is one off-season block. It fits the F-085 scheduling constraint: an October start has room for it; a January start does not.

## §2 — THE BIOMECHANIST'S PASS: the numbers, with their actual populations

### 2.1 The one on-population dataset, and the number its authors did not compute (F-377)

Zipser 2021, **n = 68 MiLB pitchers vs 50 MiLB position players**, same organisation, same pre-season week, same strapped dynamometer. Pitchers: 187.4 cm, 94.7 kg — **an almost exact match for the ASMI professional cohort of F-088.**

| | Pitchers | Position players | d | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead-leg hip ABD torque | 163.3 ± 39.4 N·m | 191.8 ± 50.4 | 0.64 | < .001 |
| Trail-leg hip ABD torque | 166.3 ± 35.0 N·m | 196.6 ± 42.0 | 0.79 | < .0001 |

The paper reports these descriptives and **never compares the groups.** Both t-tests computed in-cycle. And the pitchers are **8.4 kg heavier and 5.9 cm taller**, so the deficit grows under normalisation: **−15% raw, −22% per kg, −25% per kg·m.** Torque flatters the taller athlete, so the underlying force gap is the largest of the three.

Direction replicates in a second professional sample (Laudner 2010, ⚠️ snippet-only through the reference list of a paper read at source).

**Restriction-of-range note, and it cuts the friendly way for once:** this is a *between-group* comparison of two populations, not a within-population correlation, so the Luera collapse (r = .17–.29 in all-hard-throwing samples) does not apply to it. The effect sizes are large and the samples are on-population. **This is one of the cleanest facts in the whole topic — and it has no performance variable attached to it at all.**

### 2.2 The paper's own hypothesis failed, and that is a free negative (F-378)

Zipser's real question was whether hip abduction torque predicts single-leg step-down movement quality. **30 correlation cells, 20 independent. Two significant, at p = .049 and p = .021.** Expected under the null: 1.0, with **P(≥2) = 26%.** Largest |r| anywhere in the table: **0.28, R² = 7.8%.** Position players: **all ten cells null.**

The abstract concludes *"Hip abduction strength contributes to dynamic control of the trunk and legs."*

**If a single-leg step-down score is being used as a hip-strength screen in your building, it shares 6–8% of its variance with the thing it is believed to measure — in exactly this population.** That deletes a test from the battery rather than adding a drill, which is the cheapest kind of finding there is. *Third time this month an abstract has stated what its own table refuses (after F-373 and F-353).*

### 2.3 The flagged lead, taken apart (F-379, F-380)

The paper's own methods sentence: *"Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the correlations between **lower extremity function** after the simulated game and **pitching performance** in the 9th inning."* Lower-extremity function = **7 variables**; pitching performance = **2**. **The paper never says how many correlations it ran. The floor its design licenses is 4; the ceiling its own sentence licenses is 14.** One was reported, p = 0.011.

| Tests | Bonferroni α | p = .011 survives? | P(≥1 sig under null) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | .0125 | barely | 18.5% |
| 7 | .0071 | **no** | 30.2% |
| 14 | .0036 | **no** | **51.2%** |

**Fisher-z 95% CI: r ∈ [0.16, 0.82] ⇒ R² ∈ [2.5%, 68%].** And it is a **correlation between two change scores in one session** — "the guys who fatigued most fatigued most in both measures" fits it exactly as well.

**⚠️ And the instrument cannot support the paper's own discussion (F-380).** The dynamometer sat **between the thighs with the athlete seated**; both legs act on one device. **There is no per-limb measurement anywhere in the study** — while the entire discussion reasons about *pivot-leg* abductors during wind-up and *stride-leg* adductors after foot contact. **When a paper's prose is more anatomically specific than its instrument, the specificity came from the reference list.**

*What survives and must not be over-corrected: the velocity decline itself (−1.55 mph by the 9th, p = .001, a priori, Dunnett-corrected); the adduction drop (d = 0.47, p = .001, survives Bonferroni across the seven LE measures); and **F-359's squat-jump null, untouched** — the jump mat is still blind to pitching fatigue.*

### 2.4 The "replication" is twelve untrained men throwing from ten feet (F-381)

A search summariser offered Aso & Kagaya 2024 as the follow-up establishing that hip strength falls after 135 pitches. Read at source: **n = 12 men the authors classify as "nonstrenuous training level" — *"those who did not engage in physical training and played baseball once or twice a week"* — 67.5 kg, 170.4 cm, throwing 135 balls at a 1.1 × 1.1 m target from THREE METRES, with no ball velocity measured at all.**

And the result refutes itself: **all eight measures fell, both legs, every direction.** The **non-throwing-side abductors — which do essentially nothing in a 3-metre target throw — lost 15.0%**, and in two of four comparisons the non-throwing side lost *more* than the throwing side.

| Study | Population | Task | Hip strength loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mullaney 2005 ⚠️ second-hand | **Professional** | Real game, 99 ± 29 pitches | **null** |
| Yanai 2018 ✅ read | 81 mph collegiate | 117 max-effort mound pitches | −5 to −7% |
| Aso 2024 ✅ read | **Untrained recreational** | 135 throws from **3 m** | **−15 to −23%** |

**The effect grows as the athlete gets worse and the task gets easier. A dose-response cannot have that shape.**

*One line on injury, then move on: Aso's trunk lateral tilt at ball release rose 36.0° → 43.0° by the 9th (d = 0.77, Bonferroni-corrected). Contralateral trunk tilt at release is a positive contributor to normalized elbow varus torque (F-101). Fatigue drifts a thrower toward a torque-raising posture, and a back-view camera sees it before a radar gun does.*

### 2.5 The command null that cannot bear weight (F-386)

Same paper, same 18 athletes: strike rate **53.1% (1st) → 44.4% (9th)**, reported non-significant under Dunnett across 8 comparisons.

**Power computed in-cycle for that 8.7-point drop at n = 18:** d = 0.38/0.45/0.57 across a within-pitcher inning-to-inning correlation of r = 0.3/0.5/0.7 ⇒ **uncorrected power 35%/47%/66%, and after their own Dunnett correction 11%/15%/30%.**

**A null with 15% power is not evidence of absence.** It is the only within-outing test of F-127's ordering claim in existence, it is cited as though it settles something, and it cannot answer the question in either direction. ⚠️ Note which way it actually leans: the point estimate goes the way F-127 predicts, and **the continuous variable (velocity) reached p = .001 on the same 18 athletes while the binary one could not** — the F-374 asymmetry, fourth worked example.

## §3 — 🚨 THE CITATION ERROR (F-382)

Gauthier, Unverzagt & Davies (2025), IJSPT 20(1), PMC11698006 — a clinical commentary by three university PT faculty. The stride-phase paragraph ends:

> *"Drive leg hip abduction strength has been shown to impact performance as well, as peak ground reaction force (GRF) of the drive leg is strongly associated with ball speed.²⁰ **This suggests that a stronger push off the pitching rubber via hip abduction increases pitch velocity.**"*

**Reference 20, from the same downloaded file:**

> McNally MP, Borstad JD, Oñate JA, Chaudhari AMW (2015). ***Stride leg** ground reaction forces predict throwing velocity in adult **recreational** baseball pitchers.* J Strength Cond Res 29(10):2708.

**Four defects in one sentence:**
1. **WRONG LEG** — a *drive-leg* claim cited to a *stride-leg* study, per the cited paper's own title.
2. **WRONG VARIABLE** — ground reaction force *during the pitch* substituted for isometric hip abduction strength *in a gym*. ⚠️ **F-061 is directly on point: once bodyweight was controlled the lead-leg block explained 4–6% of between-pitcher velocity variance, and roughly half the raw force/velocity correlation was body mass.** The substituted variable is mostly the athlete's weight.
3. **ASSOCIATION → CAUSATION** — "strongly associated" becomes "increases," two sentences before the review recommends a strengthening program.
4. **POPULATION** — *recreational* adults, in a review about professional and elite arms.

Same paragraph: Laudner's strength finding is reported as pitchers being weaker *"**requiring** increased demand on the trunk and upper extremity to generate force."* The requirement was never measured. It is an inference in a finding's grammar.

**This is not a fabrication, and that is the point.** Journal real, authors real, citation correctly formatted, cited paper exists. Every defence that caught all 16 logged fabrications passes cleanly. **It is the failure mode the 2026-08-13 rule was written for — a correct citation attached to a sentence it does not support — and it is the F-245 pattern one altitude higher.**

**If someone in your building says "push harder off the rubber with your hip abductors to throw harder," this sentence is where it came from.**

## §5 — 🧮 THE PRICE AND THE DETECTION (F-384, F-385)

**THE PRIZE.** Fade ≈ **−0.2 mph/inning after the first** (Yanai's ramp) ⇒ average deficit over a 6-inning start ≈ **0.5 mph**. At this corpus's **0.15–0.40 runs/mph/9 IP** and a 90-inning season:

| Scenario | Runs / season |
|---|---|
| Fade eliminated | **0.75 – 2.0** |
| Fade halved | **0.4 – 1.0** |
| Fade cut a quarter | 0.2 – 0.5 |

**The whole topic is worth about one run a season** — the times-through-the-order tier (F-288), an order of magnitude below a gross mix error (F-272). ⚠️ Three stacked brackets; **the ordering is the output, the cells are not.**

**THE DETECTION.** R = mean(last 15 FB) − mean(first 15 FB) in a standardised ~90-pitch simulated outing.

| Sessions per side | σ_p = 0.8 | σ_p = 1.0 | σ_p = 1.2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.96 | 1.07 | 1.21 mph |
| **4** | **0.83** | **0.93** | **1.05 mph** |
| 6 | 0.68 | 0.76 | 0.86 mph |

**Four outings each side detects "we cut the fade by two-thirds." It does not detect "we cut it by a quarter" — and a quarter is what is realistically on the table.** ⚠️ Eight 90-pitch maximum-effort outings is a real workload imposition to measure something worth under a run.

**And the strength DROP is not measurable at all: ≈ 271 starts** to detect a halving. Change scores compound two noisy quantities; levels do not. ⚠️ **NEW GAP: σ_s, the session-to-session SD of within-outing velocity retention, has never been measured for any population. Tenth entry in the "already in every radar log, published by nobody" pattern.**

## §7 — THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS

**Q1 → BIOMECHANIST: "You've just told me the one clean fact on-population is that my pitchers are weaker here than my shortstop. Why isn't that the end of the argument in favour of training it?"**

*Biomechanist:* Because it is equally the end of the argument against. F-377 is a between-population descriptive with **no performance variable in the study at all.** Two readings fit identically: pitchers are under-loaded in the frontal plane, **or** the quality the industry says drives pitching velocity is one at which the specialists are worse than their teammates — which is an argument it is not load-bearing. **Nothing cross-sectional separates them.** That is Dispute #28.

> **TRANSLATION.** *What I tell the pitcher is:* "You're probably weaker side to side than the shortstop. So is nearly every pro pitcher who's been measured. We're fixing it because it's cheap and it protects your groin — not because I can tell you what it does to your fastball." *The drill is:* Copenhagen Adduction, 8 weeks, 2–3×/week, short-lever → long-lever → loaded. *On video the failure looks like:* nothing at the hip — watch the **back view at ball release** for late-outing tipping toward the glove side (36° → 43° in the only study that measured it). *Here's how we know it's working:* a dynamometer retest at 8 weeks showing an eccentric adduction gain **well clear of the ~10% MDC** (Zipser's MDC₉₀ was 44.2 N on a ~450 N measure; Ishøi's effect was +35.7%). **One athlete, one retest. That is the entire verification, and nothing about his seventh inning is part of it.**

**Q2 → ANATOMIST: "Forget the studies. Mechanically, should a guy's groin be the thing that gives out in the seventh?"**

*Anatomist:* Honestly — **probably not first, and here is my own problem with my own case.** The adductor group is large, and adductor magnus's posterior head is a hip **extensor** innervated by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve. So a seated squeeze test is partly measuring the trail-hip extension capacity that F-068 already credits, through a frontal-plane window. **I may be re-labelling a known mechanism, not finding a new one.** What I will defend: the lead leg's frontal-plane demand is a high-rate eccentric-to-concentric event repeated 90+ times, and that is the profile of tissue that fatigues. What I will not defend is that it fatigues *before* the rotator cuff or the trunk.

> **TRANSLATION.** *What I tell the pitcher is:* "Late in a start your body finds the easiest way to get the ball to the plate, and the easiest way is to tip over toward your glove side instead of holding your front hip. That costs you a little velocity and it adds elbow." *The drill is:* the same Copenhagen block, plus lateral-bound work with a **held** landing (not a rhythmic bound). *On video the failure looks like:* trunk lateral tilt at release growing across an outing, back view, 240 fps. *Here's how we know it's working:* frame-match innings 1 and 6 from the same camera position across **four starts** — a single-outing comparison proves nothing.

**Q3 → BIOMECHANIST: "Give me the honest number. If I run this block and it works perfectly, what do I get?"**

*Biomechanist:* **Between 0.75 and 2.0 runs a season if you eliminate the fade entirely, which you will not. Realistically 0.4 to 1.0.** Same tier as the third-time-through penalty. **And you cannot measure it** — four standardised 90-pitch simulated outings on each side of the block detects about a 1 mph change in retention, against a realistic effect of a quarter of that. **The measurement costs more than the thing is worth.**

> **TRANSLATION.** *What I tell the pitcher is:* "This is a durability and health block. If it buys you a tick in the seventh, great — I won't be able to prove it and neither will you." *Here's how we know it's working:* the strength number, and only the strength number.

**Q4 → BOTH: "Somebody's going to hand me the paper that says adductors keep your velo. What do I say?"**

*Biomechanist:* That the correlation is **one of up to fourteen tests**, n = 18, in an **81 mph** sample, with a confidence interval from r = 0.16 to r = 0.82, between **two change scores measured in one session**, on an instrument that **squeezes both legs at once** and therefore cannot say which leg fatigued.
*Anatomist:* And that the follow-up everyone cites is **twelve untrained men throwing from three metres**, in which the **non-throwing leg lost 15% of its abduction strength.**

> **TRANSLATION.** *What I tell the pitcher is:* nothing. This one is for the strength coach, and the sentence is: **"the direction's plausible, the number isn't real, and nobody has ever run the study."**
