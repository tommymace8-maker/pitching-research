## 4. BIOMECHANIST — the numbers, and what they kill

### 4.1 The per-PA noise floor

Standard wOBA weights, ordinary outcome distribution (BB .085, HBP .012, 1B .145, 2B .045, 3B .004, HR .033, outs .676):

- mean wOBA **0.329**, **per-PA SD 0.531**
- Sensitivity: SD is 0.492 at a 2% HR rate and 0.576 at 5%. Since n scales with σ², **every result below is stable across that whole range.**

### 4.2 One pitcher's own TTOP is unmeasurable (F-286)

Two-sample, α = .05 two-sided, 80% power:

| True individual penalty | PA per group | College seasons of 3rd-time PA |
|---|---|---|
| 0.010 wOBA (league) | 44,200 | **790** |
| 0.020 (2×) | 11,100 | 197 |
| 0.030 (3×) | 4,900 | 88 |
| 0.050 (**5×**) | 1,800 | **32** |
| 0.100 (10×) | 440 | 8 |

Budget: 14 starts × ~22 BF = **~308 BF/season**, of which **~56** are third-time-through.

> **790 college seasons to detect the league-average effect in one arm. Thirty-two seasons at five times the league average.**

**Third in a series** — F-273 (mix: 60,000 pitches), F-282 (full counts: 17 seasons), this (790 seasons). The most extreme yet, and the pattern is not pessimism: **the quantities a coach most wants to know about ONE athlete are systematically the ones one athlete cannot supply the events to answer.**

### 4.3 The composition bias — the finding of the cycle (F-287)

First and second times through are **complete passes** through all nine slots. The third pass is **truncated** at whatever batter he is pulled on — so it is drawn disproportionately from **slots 1–4, where the best hitters are.**

Lineup talent by slot (.345 .350 .355 .350 .330 .315 .300 .290 .275; mean **.3233**):

| Pulled after *k* batters, 3rd pass | TTO3 sample talent | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | .3450 | +.0217 |
| 2 | .3475 | +.0242 |
| **3** | .3500 | **+.0267** |
| **4** | .3500 | **+.0267** |
| **5** | .3460 | **+.0227** |
| 6 | .3408 | +.0175 |
| 7 | .3350 | +.0117 |
| 8 | .3294 | +.0060 |
| 9 (complete) | .3233 | **0** |

**Typical k = 3–5 → +0.025 wOBA of pure artefact.** Against a reported real effect of 0.010 per cycle:

> ### The artefact is **2.5× the entire real effect**, and it points in the direction that creates it.
> True two-cycle effect 0.020 + bias 0.025 → **observed 0.045, of which 56% is lineup slot.**

**Two things this is not.** It is **not** an attack on the published work — Brill/Deshpande/Wyner adjust for batter quality, and any competent analysis does. It is an attack on **the calculation that actually reaches a dugout**: the season split, the scorebook tally, the broadcast graphic.

And it is **bias-in-expectation, not noise.** It does not average out. Fourteen more starts give the same +.025, more precisely estimated. **More data makes this error more confident, not less** — the opposite of every instinct a coach has about sample size.

**The fix is free:** compare the third time through **against the same lineup slots** the first and second times through. Slots 1–4 vs slots 1–4. That removes the entire bias — and most of the sample, which returns you to §4.2.

## 5. ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY — does the tissue support a step?

**Short answer: no, and that is informative.**

Nothing in the fatigue physiology produces a **discontinuity at batter 19**. The corpus already holds the relevant mechanisms and they are all **smooth**:

- **F-125** — voluntary activation of the infraspinatus is *centrally* inhibited by pitching-induced fatigue. Central deficits accumulate; they do not step.
- **F-127** — the degradation sequence is command drift → breaking-ball finish → slot drop → **velocity last**, phased roughly by pitch count (1–25 stable; 25–60 the deceptive window; 60–90 posterior-cuff activation drops and command degrades before velocity; 90+ passive restraints take a larger share).
- **F-128** — load per unit of output *rises* late in an outing. Kinetics do not fall proportionally with velocity.

**So the physiology predicts a continuous decline indexed to cumulative work, which is exactly the shape the snippets attribute to the data.** That is a genuine convergence between an independent mechanism and a reported result, and it is the strongest thing in this cycle that is not arithmetic.

**But it cuts against the coach.** If the mechanism is fatigue, then *"third time through"* is a label with no causal content, and the pull rule should be written in **pitch count**, not in lineup turns. The anatomist pressed exactly this (§8, Challenge 1) and it was conceded.

## 6. PITCHING COACH — the decision, which needs neither answer (F-288)

The pull decision is **not** "is there a TTOP." It is a comparison of two arms.

Let **Δ** = the tired starter's penalty right now, **G** = how much worse the fresh reliever is in true talent. **Leave him in whenever Δ < G.**

At the reported magnitude, Δ at the third time through ≈ **0.020 wOBA**.

> ### The break-even is a **20-wOBA-point talent gap.**
> In a college bullpen, arm #6 is very often far more than 20 points worse than a tiring Friday starter. **In that case the penalty is entirely real and leaving him in is still correct.**

**What the whole question is worth.** At ~1.15 runs per PA per unit wOBA:

- 10 wOBA pts/cycle = **0.87 runs per 100 PA per exposure**
- Cost of an **equal-talent** starter facing 4 batters a third time instead of a fresh **equal-talent** arm: **0.070 runs/start**
- Over 14 starts: **≈ 1.0 run per season.**

Compare **F-280**: optimising 0-2 counts is worth ~0.2 runs a season. **Both are real, both are arithmetically confirmed, and both are too small to spend a coaching week on.**

> **A college program's third-time-through problem is a BULLPEN-DEPTH problem wearing a pitching-mechanics costume.** The leverage is in *G*, not in Δ.

## 7. What you CAN see inside an outing — and the field claim it kills

### 7.1 The detection asymmetry (F-289)

Fastballs per bucket to detect a within-outing velocity drop (α = .05, 80% power):

| within-outing FB SD | 0.5 mph | 1.0 mph | 1.5 mph | 2.0 mph |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8 | 40 | **10** | 5 | 3 |
| **1.0** (working assumption) | 63 | **16** | 7 | 4 |
| 1.2 | 90 | **23** | 10 | 6 |

> **A 1.0 mph velocity decline: visible in a SINGLE OUTING, ~16 fastballs at each end.**
> **A 10-wOBA-point outcome decline: invisible in 790 SEASONS.**
> **About 10,000 starts between them. Decide on inputs. Never on outcomes.**

Third independent domain in which this corpus has derived the F-257 asymmetry. **It should probably be promoted from a repeated observation to a standing rule.**

**The caveat that cuts against the recommendation:** F-127 puts velocity **last**. The cheapest instrument watches the **latest-arriving** signal. The earlier signals — command drift, slot — are exactly the ones with no detection threshold, because **F-173's study (within-outing release-speed SD vs vertical miss) still has not been run by anybody**, and it is computable in an afternoon from data every program already owns.

### 7.2 Killing the "41% false alarm" claim on arithmetic alone (F-291)

`sportsnaut.com` reports that a 1.5 mph velocity-drop rule *"triggers a false alarm 41% of the time."* **No page opened.** But this needs no page:

With within-inning FB SD = 1.0 mph and 15 pitches/inning, the SE of an inning-to-inning mean difference is **0.365 mph**. A 1.5 mph drop is **4.1 SD**; P(noise) ≈ **2 × 10⁻⁵**. Across SD 0.8–1.2 and n 15–25: **3.4 to 6.6 SD**.

> **Noise produces that drop about once in fifty thousand innings. A 41% false-alarm rate cannot be measuring the instrument.**

If the figure is real it is measuring one of two other things, and the article's framing fits neither:

1. **Strategic pacing** — velocity genuinely fell and genuinely came back because he was managing effort. A real and interesting finding about **intent**, not a criticism of the radar gun.
2. **A circular label** — "false alarm" needs a ground truth for *fatigue*. **There is none in Statcast.** If the label is defined by subsequent performance, the metric is validated against precisely the quantity §4.2 shows is unmeasurable.

**Verdict UNPROVEN. The instrument is fine. Do not repeat the 41%.**

## 9. TRANSLATE — what to actually say and do

**The one thing to use this week, with an 85+ arm.**

> **What I tell the pitcher:** *"I'm not taking you out because the lineup turned over. I'm taking you out when your fastball tells me to. Your number is 1 mph."*
>
> **The drill / protocol:** For each start, log every fastball velocity — you already have this in the radar log, it costs nothing. Compute the mean of the **first 16 fastballs** and the mean of the **most recent 16**. A gap of **1.0 mph or more is a real, detected decline**, not noise (§7.1). Below 0.5 mph you cannot tell, and you should stop pretending you can.
>
> **What the failure looks like on video:** it does *not* look like velocity first. Per F-127 the order is **arm-side misses and elevated fastballs → the breaking ball stops finishing → visible slot drop → longer between pitches → velocity last.** If you are seeing the velocity drop, you missed three earlier signs. Watch for the slot at 240 fps.
>
> **How we know it's working:** the check is on the **input**, never the outcome. ~16 fastballs per bucket per start gives you 80% power on a 1 mph drop. Pooling three starts gets you to ~0.6 mph. **Do not look at what hitters did — F-286 says that answer takes 790 seasons.**
>
> **And the dugout question that replaces "is it the third time through":** *"who's coming in, and is he twenty points of wOBA worse than this guy right now?"* If yes — and in a college pen he usually is — **the penalty is real and leaving him in is still correct** (F-288).

**One thing to stop doing this week.** Stop reading third-time-through splits off the scorebook or the season page. **56% of what you are looking at is lineup slot** (F-287). If you want the comparison, run it **slots 1–4 versus slots 1–4** — and then note that you have about 25 plate appearances and should not act on it either.
