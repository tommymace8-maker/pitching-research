## STEP 1 — THE RESEARCH

### 1.1 The claim the industry is actually running on

**"The Role of the Lead Hip in Collegiate Baseball Pitching: Implications for Ball Velocity and Upper-Extremity Joint Moments,"** *J Appl Biomech* 40(5):399, 2024. Secondary analysis of previously collected optoelectronic motion capture on **n = 99 collegiate pitchers**.

> For every **10° increase in internal lead hip rotation**, ball velocity increased **0.6 m/s** (P < .001, **r² = .26**) and the elbow varus moment increased **5 N·m** (P < .001, **r² = .33**). The hip angle is taken **at maximum shoulder external rotation**.

0.6 m/s = **1.34 mph**, so the slope is **0.134 mph/deg**. This is the number circulating in the field right now (see Step 2).

**Check 1 — actual sample velocity: NOT RETRIEVABLE.** A search snippet offered "collegiate pitchers show mean ball velocity of 93.2 ± 3.1 mph" but that figure belongs to a different study in the same result set and **must not be attached to this one.** Unknown whether this sample clears 85.

**Check 2 — design: CROSS_SECTIONAL.** A secondary analysis of an existing archive. **Nobody increased a pitcher's lead hip internal rotation and measured what happened.**

**Check 3 — does the number measure what the sentence says?** ⚠️ **No, and this is the cycle's central trap.** "Internal lead hip rotation" here is an **in-pitch mocap joint angle at a single instant**, not a mobility measurement. It is not a goniometer on a table. Every field write-up this cycle found treats it as a mobility prescription. **It is a description of what his pelvis was doing over a planted front leg at MER.**

Worse: **lead hip internal rotation at MER is, to first order, pelvis axial orientation re-referenced.** Lead hip IR is the femur–pelvis relative angle about the femoral long axis. At MER the lead foot is planted, so the lead femur's axial orientation is largely fixed by foot placement — which means more pelvis rotation toward the plate *is* more lead hip internal rotation, nearly by construction. **This is the fifth time this corpus has caught a variable wearing the trunk's clothes** (Dispute #38 head/eye, #47 glove arm, #51 arm slot, F-535 release point, now this). Not a proven identity — the stride and foot angle supply real independent variance — but r² = .26 is exactly the size you would expect from a partial restatement of a known pelvis effect.

**Check 4 — is the r² a geometric identity?** Partially, per above. Not fully: 26% leaves room.

✅ **One thing to the paper's credit: n = 99 PASSES this corpus's F-439 n ≥ 97 rule.** It is the only ROM-adjacent finding in the registry that does. (F-015's null is n = 30; Robb 2010 is n = 19.)

### 1.2 The scale bar — derived, source-independent (F-565)

Quoting "1.3 mph per 10°" without the observed spread is the error that made "+1.8 mph per foot of extension" the most misleading sentence in the sport. Back the spread out of the paper's own two numbers:

`SD_x = r · SD_y / b`, with `b = 0.1342 mph/deg` and `r = √.26 = .510`.

| Assumed collegiate FB velocity SD | Implied SD of lead hip IR | Mean → +2 SD is worth |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 mph | 7.6° | **2.0 mph** |
| 2.6 mph | 9.9° | 2.7 mph |
| 3.5 mph | 13.3° | **3.6 mph** |

**So ±1 SD of lead hip internal rotation across 99 college pitchers is roughly ±8–13°, and going from average to the top of the observed distribution is worth 2.0–3.6 mph.** The dial has about 20–26 degrees of total travel in the entire population, not an open-ended scale. (Velocity SD is assumed, not read — the paper's SD is not retrievable.)

### 1.3 The anatomist's intervention: how much of hip rotation ROM is bone? (F-566)

**Takeuchi S, Sugimoto K, Goto H, et al. (2020),** *Orthop J Sports Med*, doi 10.1177/2325967120966319, PMID 33415173. **n = 149 elite male baseball players** (age 20.0 ± 1.8; 64 pitchers, 85 position players). Femoral torsion angle by ultrasonography:

> Lead hip **20.5° ± 9.2°**; trail hip **19.6° ± 9.8°**; no side difference (**P = .276**).

Population spread of passive hip IR ROM in baseball, from the **2025 *J Sport Rehabil* 34(7):691 systematic review**: trail-leg IR at 0° hip flexion **49.4° ± 12.6°**; at 90° flexion **46.9° ± 13.3°**.

**The arithmetic.** Femoral torsion SD ≈ 9.5°. Passive hip IR ROM SD ≈ 12.6°. If torsion mapped 1:1 onto IR ROM, the osseous variance share would be `9.5² / 12.6² = 57%`. Under a conservative 0.7 attenuation it is `(6.65)² / 12.6² = 28%`.

> **Between roughly 28% and 57% of the between-pitcher spread in hip rotation ROM is the shape of his femur, and the shape of his femur is 0% trainable at skeletal maturity.**

Both brackets are soft in known directions and they push opposite ways: the 12.6° comes from a review pooling across studies with different raters, so it carries between-study variance and **inflates the denominator (deflating the osseous share)**; ultrasound FTA has its own measurement error, which **inflates the numerator**. ⚠️ **The coefficient that would settle this is printed in Takeuchi 2020 and I could not retrieve it. Verification queue item 1.**

Corroborating magnitude from the orthopaedic literature: in increased-anteversion populations, mean maximum hip IR is **68.33° ± 6.34°** versus **38.95° ± 13.66°** in controls — a **~29° gap from bone alone.** Modeled bony impingement limits IR at a mean of **63° (range 30–85°)**, intraoperatively **49° (range 20–70°)**. Compare: the 4-week stretching RCTs below move ROM by **5–10°.**

**This is F-115 at a second joint.** This corpus already retired *"stretch out his GIRD"* because humeral retrotorsion is osseous and locked at maturity. **The identical argument applies to hip internal rotation and the industry has not made it.**

### 1.4 The asymmetry is the trainable part; the level is the bone (F-567)

Takeuchi found the femoral torsion **symmetric** lead vs trail (P = .276). **McCulloch PC, Patel JK, Ramkumar PN, Noble PC, Lintner DM (2014),** *Orthop J Sports Med*, doi 10.1177/2325967114521575 — *"Asymmetric Hip Rotation in Professional Baseball Pitchers"* — reports hip rotation ROM that is **not** symmetric.

**If the bone is symmetric and the motion is not, the asymmetry is soft tissue, adaptation, or measurement — and only the asymmetry is movable.** That is structurally identical to F-116's argument for the shoulder (TRM asymmetry beats raw GIRD *because* it partially controls the osseous term). **Fifth independent arrival at Corpus Rule 3: coach the position, measure the trend, never the norm.**

### 1.5 The permissive-ceiling model, and why a linear fit is the wrong model (F-568)

**n = 11 collegiate pitchers, full-body inertial motion capture, fastballs:** asymptomatic collegiate pitchers **"approach their extremes of passive hip rotation when executing a fastball"** — lead hip internal rotation and trail hip external rotation specifically. (Snippet-only; study identity not pinned beyond the n and the method.)

If in-pitch demand sits at the passive maximum, **passive ROM is a binding ceiling, not a dial:**

- Below the in-pitch demand → the joint restricts the delivery.
- At or above it → additional degrees buy nothing, because nothing is asking for them.

**That predicts a threshold with a plateau, not a slope.** And a straight line fitted through a mixed population is then **driven entirely by the restricted tail** and **systematically overstates the benefit for every athlete already above threshold** — which, in an 85+ selected sample, is most of them. Same structural error family as **F-510** (a slope fitted across a level gap) and **F-524** (an inverted U read as a line).

⚠️ **No study in this literature has fitted a threshold, spline or segmented model to hip ROM against velocity.** Everything is Pearson r or OLS.

**The biomechanist's counter, which I concede is unresolved:** if in-pitch rotation actually *exceeds* the table measurement — plausible, since pelvis contribution, muscle activation and soft-tissue creep all differ — then the table number is not a ceiling at all, it is simply **not measuring the same construct**, and it is uninformative in the other direction. Either reading damns the goniometer as a velocity instrument. **Dispute #55.**

### 1.6 The cross-level +7° is a Type D candidate (F-569)

From the same 2025 review: hip IR ROM is reduced in baseball populations versus general norms, most in high-school and college athletes, **with an increase of over 7° in the professional population compared with the college-aged population.**

**Do not quote this as a target.** It is a difference between two sets of *different studies*, with different raters and goniometric protocols, not a within-athlete change. It is equally explained by survivorship (restricted hips wash out or get hurt), maturation, professional ROM-maintenance programs, or inter-rater drift across the pooled papers. **This is F-510's pooled-gradient failure in a new venue — Type D in the 2026-09-30 taxonomy.**

### 1.8 The one trial that did measure pitching output — and it went the wrong way (F-562, F-563)

**"Osteopathic manipulative treatment for enhanced pitch performance in collegiate baseball players: a feasibility study on shoulder and hip interventions,"** *J Osteopath Med*, doi 10.1515/jom-2025-0106, **PMID 41588695**; registered **NCT07216300** and **ISRCTN 51846048**.

**Design: randomized, controlled. 13 pitchers randomized; 6 OMT / 5 control after dropouts (n = 11 analyzed). Single application of OMT. Follow-up at 5 weeks.** `CAUSALITY: INTERVENTION` — the scarcest tag in this registry.

**It moved ROM:**
- Shoulder internal rotation **+7.16°** over control immediately post (**p = 0.017**)
- Left hip flexion **+6.76°** immediately and **+12.87°** at 5 weeks
- Right hip flexion **+5.89°**, significant at 5 weeks only

**What happened to the pitching:**
> **The only significant pitch-performance difference was an immediate 0.74 mph REDUCTION in "effective velocity" in the OMT group (p = 0.048), not sustained at 5-week follow-up.**

**Injury, reported but unusable as a magnitude:** overuse injuries developed in the control group only, preventing 2 of 5 controls from continuing; reported OR **9.29**. ⚠️ **That is two events in total.** The direction is interesting; the 9.29 is not a number anybody should repeat. One line, and move on.

**Now the four checks, because they matter more than the headline:**

1. **Sample velocity: NOT RETRIEVABLE.** Unknown whether these pitchers clear 85.
2. **Design: randomized and controlled — genuinely an intervention.** But **n = 11 fails this corpus's n ≥ 97 rule by a factor of nine**, and the paper calls itself a feasibility study. With six subjects in one arm, a p = 0.048 on one of several performance metrics is what selective significance looks like.
3. **Does the number measure what the sentence says? NO — and this is Check 3 landing for the fourth time in eight cycles.** **"Effective velocity" is a derived metric, not ball speed.** The paper describes itself as measuring *"pitch metrics beyond release velocity"* — which means release velocity was measured and is **not** among the significant differences. So the honest statement is: *release velocity did not change; a derived composite moved 0.74 mph in the unhelpful direction.*
4. **Geometric identity: not applicable.**

⚠️ **And a mismatch nobody has noted: the ROM this trial moved was shoulder IR and hip FLEXION. The ROM the velocity literature points at is hip ROTATION.** The trial increased the wrong degrees.

**What it licenses, stated conservatively:**
> **The only existing trial that increased an 85-mph-population pitcher's range of motion and measured his pitching produced no velocity gain, and its single significant performance result pointed down.** That is not "mobility work costs you velocity" — at n = 11 it cannot support that. It is: **the one time anyone checked, the gain did not appear.**

### 1.9 What actually degrades in an outing is strength, not range (F-570)

After roughly **35 pitches**, adolescent pitchers showed measurable declines in hip strength **including lead hip internal rotation strength**, along with **decreased torso rotation angle and reduced hip–shoulder separation** — and the authors characterize it as **loss of strength and coordination rather than loss of range.**

⚠️ **SAMPLE MISMATCH — adolescent. DIRECTIONAL ONLY.** But it is the only in-game account of hip variables available, and it points at the strength-endurance channel, not the mobility channel. **Testable prediction that separates the two: mobility work should do nothing to the back half of an outing; hip-rotation strength endurance should.** Nobody has run it.

## STEP 3 — TRANSLATE: THE COACH ASKS FOUR QUESTIONS

### Q1 (coach → biomechanist): "If I buy the 1.3 mph per 10°, what do I tell a kid to do on Monday?"

**Biomechanist:** Nothing, because the sentence does not contain an instruction. The 10° is **his pelvis's orientation over a planted front leg at maximum shoulder external rotation**, measured by motion capture in a lab, compared across 99 different men. It is not a number on a goniometer and it is not a number he can change by stretching. Three separate reasons it fails as an instruction: it is cross-sectional (nobody moved it); r² = .26 means 74% of the velocity variance is elsewhere; and the total observed travel in the entire college population is about 20–26° (F-565), so even the full dial is 2–3.6 mph *between different people*, not within him.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is: nothing about his hip internal rotation.** The drill is **no drill.** On video the failure looks like **a coach measuring a pitcher's hip on a table and then naming a degree target for his pelvis in the delivery — two different measurements, one word.** And here's how we know it's working: **it isn't a claim, so there is nothing to check.** The honest move is to stop quoting the number.

### Q2 (coach → anatomist): "A guy comes in with 32° of hip internal rotation and the internet says he needs 45. Can I get it?"

**Anatomist:** Mostly no, and you need to know *why* no before you spend a block on it. His femoral torsion is roughly 20° ± 9.5° (Takeuchi, n = 149 elite ballplayers), it is set at skeletal maturity, and it accounts for somewhere between a quarter and a half of why his number is 32 and someone else's is 48 (F-566). The orthopaedic range makes the scale brutal: anteverted hips average 68° of max IR against 39° in controls — **29° from bone**, where your best 4-week stretching program yields 5–10°. And this corpus already fought this exact fight at the shoulder and lost it (F-115): *"attempting to stretch a 23-year-old's internal rotation toward a normative number is attempting to remodel bone with a towel, and the tissue that actually yields is the anterior capsule you did not want to lengthen."* The hip's version of that unwanted yield is the anterior capsule and the labrum.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is: "That number is mostly the shape of your hip, not the state of your hip, and we're not chasing somebody else's number."** The drill is **a one-time measurement used to pick his delivery, not a mobility block aimed at a norm.** On video the failure looks like **a pitcher forcing a closed-off landing his hip has no room for, and decelerating through his low back instead of his hip.** And here's how we know it's working: **we don't track his degrees toward a target at all — we track his own side-to-side gap and his own trend (F-567), because that is the only part that can move.**

### Q3 (coach → both): "Fine. If I run a mobility block anyway, how would I know it did anything?"

**Biomechanist, on the ROM side.** Same-rater hip ROM reliability in baseball players: intra-rater ICC 0.95–0.98, **SEM 2.2° for internal rotation and 3.2° for external rotation.** ⚠️ Those come from a **youth** sample — SAMPLE MISMATCH, directional only, and probably optimistic for a 95 kg college arm. Minimal detectable change at 95%: `MDC₉₅ = SEM × 1.96 × √2 = 2.77 × SEM`.

| Measure | Single measurement | **Mean of 3 reps** |
|---|---|---|
| Hip IR | **MDC₉₅ = 6.1°** | **3.5°** |
| Hip ER | **MDC₉₅ = 8.9°** | **5.1°** |

> **A single-rater re-measure has to move more than about 6° of internal rotation before you are allowed to call it a change** — and 6° is roughly the entire yield of a 4-week stretching program. **Three reps averaged, one rater, same time of day, cuts that to 3.5° and is the difference between a protocol that can detect its own effect and one that cannot.** Most programs are run at single-measurement precision and are therefore unfalsifiable on their own terms.

**And on the velocity side.** Suppose he genuinely gains 6° of passive IR *and* the (unproven, wrongly-imported) 0.134 mph/deg transfer were real: that is **0.8 mph**. Paired detection of 0.8 mph at 80% power, α = .05:

| His within-athlete FB velocity SD | Pitches needed per condition |
|---|---|
| 1.0 mph | **13** |
| 1.5 mph | **28** |
| 2.0 mph | **50** |

⚠️ **This cannot be narrowed, because `INDEX.md` already flags that no within-outing fastball velocity SD has ever been published for an 85+ arm.** The bracket is the honest answer. Note what it means: **the velocity side of this check is cheap — one or two bullpens of tracked pitches. It is the ROM side, at MDC 6°, that is hard.** That is the opposite of how mobility programs are usually audited.

### Q4 (coach → both): "Then is hip mobility worth a training block at all for an 85-mph arm?"

**Anatomist:** Not as a velocity block. **Possibly as a selection input, once, for free.**
**Biomechanist:** And not as a fatigue intervention either — what degrades across 35 pitches is lead hip internal rotation **strength** and coordination, not range (F-570). If his bullpen falls apart in the fifth, more range is answering a question nobody asked.
**Coach:** Then the whole topic collapses into a measurement, not a program.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is: "We're going to measure your hips once, and then we're going to pick the delivery your hips can actually hold."** The drill is **the delivery he already has, stress-tested against his own structure** — per Cressey/Tread, a high-IR hip tolerates a more closed landing and a low-IR hip wants a squarer one. On video the failure looks like **a closed-off landing in a pitcher with limited internal rotation, who then dumps deceleration into his lumbar spine, loses the hip as a brake, and drifts his release point late in outings.** And here's how we know it's working: **not velocity — late-outing release-point consistency and his own hip-rotation strength-endurance trend across a bullpen.** ⚠️ **Honest flag: this recommendation is PROMISING and UNTESTED. It is the best-reasoned idea in the topic and it has zero trials behind it.**

## STEP 4 — CROSS-EXAMINATION

### Challenge 1 — BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST
**The claim I doubt:** "28–57% of the between-pitcher spread in hip rotation ROM is osseous" (F-566).
**Why:** You built it from two numbers that came from different studies, different populations, and different instruments, and you supplied the mapping slope yourself. The 12.6° SD is from a systematic review **pooling across studies with different raters** — a sizeable chunk of that variance is inter-rater noise, which inflates your denominator. And you have assumed femoral torsion maps onto IR ROM at between 0.7 and 1.0 when **the actual coefficient is printed in the paper you could not open.** Stripped down, this is **F-473's standardized-effect import fallacy in metric clothing**: you converted between two populations' spreads without establishing that the conversion holds.
