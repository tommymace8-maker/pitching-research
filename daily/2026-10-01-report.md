## 1 — ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY pass: what tissue is actually being asked to move?

**The finding that reorganizes the topic (F-533).** Statcast arm angle is the inclination, relative to horizontal, of the **straight line from the throwing shoulder to the ball at release**. 0° = sidearm, 90° = over the top.

That means it is **not a shoulder joint angle.** It is a composite of three anatomically independent contributors:

| Contributor | Effect on the number | Cost profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Trunk lateral tilt** toward the glove side | more tilt → **lower** arm angle | **expensive** — costs command (F-176), raises varus torque (F-070's own stated failure mode) |
| **Shoulder abduction** | less abduction → lower arm angle | the "real" arm change; near-free on velocity (F-538) |
| **Elbow flexion at release** | more flexion shortens the vector | small; not consciously controlled |

The anatomist's verdict: **"raise your arm slot" is not one instruction. It is three instructions with opposite price tags, and the pitcher will pick whichever is easiest — which is the trunk.**

**The adaptation question nobody has asked (F-545).** Every torque finding in the registry measures pitchers **at their established slot** — i.e. after adaptation. None describes a transient. But changing the humeral position at which peak internal-rotation torque is absorbed changes the posterior cuff's moment arms and its length–tension state at the critical instant, and the tissue has thousands of reps at the old position and **none** at the new one. A single college-program anecdote (snippet-only) reports exactly that: a slot change producing inflammation, managed with pool work. **WEAK evidence, coherent mechanism, and it is the one practical detail no analytics piece on this trend mentions.** One line, per the mission: make the change in the offseason or an early-season low-leverage block, at reduced volume for 2–3 weeks. Graded WEAK; not a protocol.

## 2 — BIOMECHANIST pass: the identity underneath the whole topic

This is where the day paid, and it needed no egress.

### 2.1 The identity (F-534)

Because shoulder-to-ball distance at release is nearly fixed for a given athlete:

> ### **z_ball = z_shoulder + L · sin θ**

**Deriving L** on the ASMI professional anthropometry (F-151, height 189.7 ± 5.8 cm), Winter segment fractions: upper arm 0.186·H = 0.353 m; forearm 0.146·H = 0.277 m; hand 0.108·H = 0.205 m; elbow→ball ≈ 0.482 m; elbow flexion at release ≈ 22° (ASMI norm 20–25°).

L = √(0.353² + 0.482² + 2·0.353·0.482·cos 22°) = √(0.1246 + 0.2323 + 0.3155) = **0.820 m = 32.3 in**

**The exchange rate: dz/dθ = 0.564 · cos θ inches per degree**

| arm angle θ | in. of release height per 1° of slot |
|---|---|
| 20° | 0.53 |
| 30° | 0.49 |
| 40° | 0.43 |
| **50° (≈ MLB avg)** | **0.36** |
| 60° | 0.28 |
| 70° | 0.19 |

**Checks run:** finite difference 50°→45° gives 32.3 × (0.7660 − 0.7071) = **1.90 in = 0.38 in/deg**, agreeing with the derivative to 5% ✅. At 185 cm, L = 0.80 m and the 50° figure becomes 0.35 in/deg — **±5 cm of height moves the table ±4%; the slot angle matters far more than the body.**

**Check #4 applied to our own work:** this is an identity, so **release height and arm angle are NOT independent variables.** Any model using both is fitting a near-collinear pair. A high R² between them is largely geometry restated — exactly the failure the program caught at R² = .945 previously. **The residual, not the correlation, is the information.**

### 2.2 🚨 What the identity does to the sport's headline number (F-535)

F-070 holds both of these, side by side, as one trend:
- "MLB release points ~2 in lower than 2016"
- "arm angle down 1.41 deg since 2020"

**1.41° × 0.362 in/deg = 0.51 in.** Observed: **2.0 in**. → **arm angle accounts for ~25%.**

⚠️ **Baseline mismatch, stated honestly:** 2 in from 2016, 1.41° from 2020. This is the **generous** case for the arm-angle story, and it cannot be cleaned up because Statcast arm angle does not exist before 2020.

**Independent route, same place.** F-536's validation study: Δvertical release vs Δarm angle **r = 0.74 → R² = 0.55**, i.e. **45% of year-over-year release-height variance is not arm angle.** One derived route and one empirical route converge on *half to three-quarters of release-height movement is a non-slot channel.*

> **"The league is dropping its arm slot" is at most a quarter true. What the league is mostly doing is releasing from a lower shoulder.**

**Not pedantry:** the two channels have opposite cost profiles (§1). **The sport has been recommending the cheap channel using the expensive channel's data.** F-070 is annotated in place with a dated flag; nothing was overwritten.

### 2.4 🔢 Why the combined proxy LOST to vertical release alone (F-537)

Geometry says horizontal offset from the shoulder is **y = L·cos θ**, so **|dy/dθ| = 0.564·sin θ = 0.43 in/deg at 50°** — against **0.36 in/deg** for vertical. **Horizontal should be the BETTER proxy, by 1.2×.** It measured far worse (−0.38 vs 0.74).

**The reconciliation is nuisance variance, and the two channels are not symmetric in it.** Horizontal release moves by many inches when a pitcher shifts on the rubber or changes stride direction/landing closure — **neither of which touches arm angle.** Vertical release has no nuisance source of comparable size (shoulder height moves inches; rubber position moves a foot).

**And that explains the otherwise strange middle result:** the combined geometric proxy sits *between* its two inputs because it imports the horizontal channel's noise alongside the vertical channel's signal. **A proxy using more of the geometrically correct information performed worse — a noise-weighting result, not a geometry result.**

**Falsifiable prediction, free to run for anyone with Hawk-Eye or Trackman: control for rubber position and stride direction, and horizontal release becomes a good arm-angle proxy.** If it doesn't, this explanation is wrong.

## 4 — PITCHING-COACH pass: four pointed questions

### Q1 → Biomechanist: "I can see a guy's arm slot on video. Why are you telling me I can't coach it?"

**Answer:** You can see the *number*. You cannot see which of three tissues produced it (F-533). And the arithmetic says the sport mostly isn't changing the arm at all — ~75% of the league's release-point drop is the shoulder getting lower (F-535). That matters because the trunk route is the expensive one: F-176's plus-command professionals sit **5° higher with less lateral trunk flexion** (p = .009 and p < .001, velocity-matched at p = .055, n = 338), and F-070's own stated failure mode is that "lowering the slot" by adding trunk tilt **raises** elbow varus torque.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We're not going to talk about your arm slot. We're going to find out whether you changed your arm or just tipped your torso over, because those cost different things."*
> **The drill is:** measure it — §6 protocol below.
> **On video the failure looks like:** the slot number moved, but the arm looks the same relative to the torso, and the whole torso is leaning further toward the glove side at release. He didn't lower his arm; he tipped over.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** release height and arm angle tracking **together** — the residual shoulder-height drop staying under ~2 in — across **≥200 pitches** (F-186), not one bullpen.

### Q2 → Biomechanist: "Straight answer. Does dropping down cost velocity?"

**Answer:** No, and this is the one axis of Dispute #10 that today actually resolved. Between pitchers it's a wash — **n = 207 professionals at 86.3 mph, not significant**, replicated in a second sample where **only underhand was slower**. Within a pitcher who moves it, **−0.15 mph** season over season, which is ~1/10th of the between-outing SD and well under the ~1.6 mph floor at which you could detect it on one arm.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"It'll cost you a tenth or two of a mile an hour and neither of us will be able to see it. Velocity is not the reason to say no."*
> ⚠️ **What must NOT be said is "it's basically free."** Dispute #52: −0.15 mph is the cost among **pitchers it worked for** — self-selected changers, with reverters either dropped from the sample or recorded at their reverted slot. **The honest version:** *"the average cost among guys it worked for was about a tenth of a mile an hour, and nobody has published what it cost the guys who went back."*

### Q3 → Anatomist: "If it's that cheap, why isn't everybody doing it?"

**Answer:** Three reasons, in descending order of evidence.
1. **Command (F-542), and it is the binding constraint.** F-176's velocity-matched n = 338 contrast is the strongest thing in the topic. Mechanism via F-171: a release angle generated by a heavily tilted trunk converts a 1° trunk error into a 1° release-angle error, and **1° ≈ 12 inches at the plate.** ⚠️ Cross-sectional — it describes who commands well, it is not a dial.
2. **Platoon (F-071).** Lower slot is a **same-handed** weapon and is contraindicated against a predominantly opposite-handed lineup. For a college starter facing whole lineups, this is a real constraint that the MLB reliever-driven trend hides.
3. **The transition (F-545).** A soreness window nobody has measured. WEAK.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The price isn't your velo, it's your strike-throwing — and it's bigger if you're a starter than if you're a one-inning guy against same-handed hitters."*
> **How we know it's working:** ≥200 tracked pitches, and his command gets compared against **his own** pre-change baseline, not a league number.

### Q4 → Both: "Is there anything here I can use this week?"

**Answer: yes, exactly one thing, and it is new — §6.** It survives a bullpen, needs no force plate, and answers a question that could not previously be answered.

## 6 — 🎯 WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK

### "Did he change his ARM, or did he just get LOWER?"

**Need:** release height (any tracking system), arm angle, his height. **That's it.**

> ## 1° of arm angle ≈ 0.35 in of release height.
> ## Anything beyond that is his shoulder, not his arm.

**Worked example.** A pitcher at 50° drops to 45° (−5°):
- Expected release-height drop **from the arm alone**: 32.3 × (sin 50° − sin 45°) = **1.9 in**
- Observed: **4.0 in**
- **Residual: 2.1 in of shoulder drop** → he executed the change by adding trunk tilt, not by changing his arm.

**Why you care which it was:**

| Executed at the... | Velocity | Command | Elbow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **arm** (abduction) | ~free, −0.15 mph | unknown — never measured | probably down (F-070, **direction flagged, F-540**) |
| **trunk** (added tilt) | ~free | **the expensive channel** (F-176) | **up** (F-070's own failure mode) |

**On video the failure looks like:** slot number moved, arm unchanged relative to torso, torso leaning further to the glove side at release.

**How you'd know it's working:** the residual shoulder-height drop stays under ~2 in, across **≥200 tracked pitches** (F-186's minimum to detect a ~2 in command change). **Honest limit: the back-calculation carries a ±1.5 in error bar. This detects inches, not tenths. A 2 in residual is real; a 0.5 in residual is noise.** One bullpen proves nothing.

**And the same algebra is a free league-wide analysis nobody has run (F-543):** `z_shoulder = z_ball − L·sin θ`, on public Savant data, decomposing the 2020–2026 release-height decline per pitcher. **F-535 predicts the shoulder term carries ~75%.** MLB holds the exact value — Statcast computes arm angle *from* the shoulder position — and publishes it for nobody.
