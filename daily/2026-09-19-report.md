## §2 — THE ANCHOR, READ IN FULL: sleep loss costs SKILL three to seven times what it costs POWER

**Craven J, McCartney D, Desbrow B, Sabapathy S, Bellinger P, Roberts L, Irwin C (2022). "Effects of Acute Sleep Loss on Physical Performance: A Systematic and Meta-Analytical Review." *Sports Med* 52:2669–2690. PMCID PMC9584849. DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y. PMID 35708888.**
**SOURCE-VERIFIED — full text retrieved from the PMC OA bucket and read.**

**Design, verbatim from the methods.** Inclusion required **controlled trials employing repeated-measures experimental designs**; "control" = normal sleep, >6 h in any 24 h; "intervention" = sleep loss, ≤6 h in any 24 h. Studies using stimulants or sedatives during the trial were **excluded**. Search ran from inception to **September 2020** — the anchor is already six years stale. 227 outcome measures from 69 publications; **n = 959, 89% male**.

**This is a genuinely manipulated literature.** Unlike ~30:1 of this corpus, sleep loss was *done to people* under control conditions. The marker/lever problem does not arise for the direction of effect. It arises hard for the magnitude, and for the population.

### Table 2, the numbers that matter (mean %Δ in performance under sleep loss, 95% CI)

| Category | Outcomes | Overall | Early restriction (**late bedtime**) | Late restriction (**early wake**) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **All categories** | 227 | **−7.56 (−11.9 to −3.13)**, I² = 98.1 | −5.85 (−13.4 to +1.66), **p = 0.125 NS** | −7.39 (−10.1 to −4.66) |
| **Anaerobic power** (≤6 s max effort: CMJ, squat jump, 20 m sprint, Wingate peak) | 58 | −6.26 (−9.10 to −3.41) | **−0.50 (−2.00 to +1.00), p = 0.477, I² = 0.04** | −7.47 (−11.1 to −3.85) |
| **Strength** (1RM, MVC) | 66 | −2.85 (−4.47 to −1.23) | **−1.16 (−2.57 to +0.25), p = 0.102, I² = 0.02** | −4.45 (−9.30 to +0.41) |
| Speed/power endurance | 32 | −2.90 (−4.97 to −0.82) | +0.49 (−2.05 to +3.04) | −4.38 (−7.15 to −1.62) |
| **Skill** (tennis serving, rugby passing, free-throw shooting, rifle shooting, **dart throwing**) | 13 | **−20.9 (−27.0 to −14.9)**, I² = 94.1 | **−23.9 (−33.6 to −14.2), p < 0.001** | 1 outcome — unmeasurable |

Skill: **nine studies, n = 146, 80% male, 13 outcome measures.** AM tests −14.2 (−26.7 to −1.68); PM tests −22.9 (−29.7 to −16.0).

### Three things in that table, in order of importance to a pitching coach

**(1) The skill/power ratio is the finding.** Skill −20.9% against anaerobic power −6.26% and strength −2.85%. Same subjects, same nights, same literature. **Whatever a short night takes, it does not take it evenly, and it takes the most from the category whose example tasks are "tennis serving" and "throwing darts."**

**(2) A late bedtime with a protected wake time is, for POWER, free — and these are precise nulls, not underpowered ones.** Anaerobic power under early restriction: −0.50%, CI −2.00 to +1.00, **I² = 0.04**. Strength: −1.16%, CI −2.57 to +0.25, **I² = 0.02**. Near-zero heterogeneity across 11 and 26 outcomes respectively. The literature is not failing to find an effect here; it is finding its absence tightly.

**(3) The same protocol destroys skill.** Early restriction, skill: **−23.9% (−33.6 to −14.2), 8 outcomes, p < 0.001.** The one sleep-loss pattern that leaves max-effort output completely alone is the one that hits target accuracy hardest in this dataset.

**Translated with the appropriate hedge:** in populations that are not pitchers, on tasks that are not pitches, **a late night appears to cost accuracy without costing peak output.** That pattern, if it holds for pitching, says a short night does not take your fastball. It takes your strike zone.

### The deflation the biomechanist insists on, and it is in the paper's own data

Table 4, meta-regression of %ΔEP on **hours awake before the test**:

| Category | Restriction | Early restriction | Late restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| All categories | **−0.36%/h (−0.52 to −0.19), p < 0.001** | −0.10 (−0.27 to +0.09), p = 0.323 | **−0.55%/h (−0.82 to −0.28), p < 0.001** |
| Anaerobic power | −0.47 (−0.79 to −0.14) | +0.16 (−0.47 to +0.15), p = 0.266 | −0.56 (−1.09 to −0.02) |
| **Skill** | −0.15 (−1.39 to +1.09), p = 0.782 | −0.02 (−1.28 to +1.25), p = 0.975 | 1 outcome |

**Read those two rows together.** Power performance degrades with hours awake and barely cares which end of the night you lost. Skill performance is hammered and **does not scale with hours awake at all** (−0.02%/h, p = 0.975).

Two consequences:
- The early/late dissociation for POWER may be nothing but a time-awake artifact. An athlete who wakes early has been up longer at a fixed test time. That is the simpler explanation, and Craven's own regression supports it over any sleep-architecture story. **Opened as Dispute #32.**
- The skill decrement is NOT a fatigue-hours phenomenon. Something about having slept badly, not about having been awake long, degrades target accuracy. That is a different mechanism and it is the one that matters for a 7 pm first pitch.

### What the biomechanist will not let pass

**I² = 98.1% overall and 94.1% on skill.** A pooled point estimate sitting inside that much heterogeneity is a location parameter of a cloud, not an effect size. **−20.9% is not a number to plan around.** The sign and its consistency across five unrelated task types are the finding; the magnitude is not.

**And the metric is not commensurable across categories.** %ΔEP is a percentage change *of the task's own score*. Wingate peak power has a baseline near 700 W; a free-throw task has a baseline near 40–70%; Cook's rugby pass plateaus at 9.0/10. A fixed absolute decrement produces a much larger %Δ on a low-baseline percentage score than on a watts score. **Part of the skill/power gap could be a denominator, not a physiology.** This is the same class of error as the R² = .945 geometric identity the corpus caught earlier. **Opened as Dispute #31.** It is a reasoned objection, not a demonstrated artifact — Craven's per-study data are not in the retrieved text.

## §3 — THE BEST POPULATION MATCH IN THE LITERATURE, READ IN FULL

**Cook CJ, Crewther BT, Kilduff LP, Drawer S, Gaviglio CM (2011). "Skill execution and sleep deprivation: effects of acute caffeine or creatine supplementation — a randomized placebo-controlled trial." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 8:2. PMCID PMC3049131. PMID 21324203.**
**SOURCE-VERIFIED — full text retrieved from the PMC OA bucket and read.**

**Sample.** Ten professional rugby backs, **age 20 ± 0.5 y, 90 ± 4 kg, 1.81 ± 0.02 m.** Elite, young, heavy, power-trained. Closest thing in this literature to a college or pro pitching staff.

**Design.** Within-subject, randomized, placebo-controlled. Ten test sessions over ten weeks, ≥3 days apart, **all at 1130 h** (time of day held constant — a real strength). Sleep manipulated: **7–9 h for two nights before five tests; 3–5 h on the single night before the other five** (with >7 h the night prior). That is one night of realistic partial restriction, not a lab vigil.

**Task.** 20 m sprint; at the 10 m mark, pass a rugby ball through a **1.5 m hoop 10 m away**, alternating left and right, **20 repetitions per trial** (10 per side).

**Result.** Sleep deprivation with placebo produced **a significant fall in accuracy on both dominant and non-dominant sides (p < 0.001)**. Caffeine at 1 or 5 mg/kg and creatine at 50 or 100 mg/kg all produced significantly better performance than placebo (p < 0.001); sleep-state × treatment interactions were significant on both sides (dominant F(4,90) = 7.978; non-dominant F(4,90) = 6.026).

⚠️ **THE MAGNITUDE IS NOT IN THE TEXT.** The means live only in Figures 1 and 2, which are images this environment cannot read. **The direction and the p-values are source-verified; the size of the skill decrement is not retrievable.** The corpus should record this as a verified direction with an unavailable magnitude and not import a number for it from anywhere else.

⚠️ **A second thing the text leaves genuinely ambiguous.** The paper reports a treatment **main effect** ("all of the caffeine and creatine doses produce a significant enhancement in skill performance when compared to placebo") alongside a significant interaction. **Whether caffeine improved accuracy in the RESTED state cannot be resolved from the text.** That distinction is the whole difference between "caffeine rescues a sleep-loss deficit" and "caffeine is a command drug," and this cycle cannot settle it. It is not settled below either.

### The finding inside this paper that has nothing to do with sleep, and may be the most useful thing in the cycle

**Table 1 — accuracy out of 10, by familiarisation trial:**

| | 1st trial | 5th trial | 12th trial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant | 7.3 ± 0.8 | **9.0 ± 0.7** | 9.0 ± 0.4 |
| Non-dominant | 5.7 ± 0.8 | **8.3 ± 0.8** | 8.2 ± 0.7 |

"Skill execution on both sides improved significantly (p < 0.001) across the first 5 sessions and then was unchanged between session 5 and 12." Within-individual variability on non-deprived days was **<5%**; between individuals, **<15%**.

**Read that as a measurement result and it is brutal.** In **professional athletes**, on a **simple** accuracy task, performance climbed **+1.7/10 on the dominant side and +2.6/10 on the non-dominant side** before it plateaued — and it took **five sessions.** Anyone who runs a pre/post accuracy study with fewer than five familiarisation sessions and no control group is measuring learning and calling it an intervention. That is a direct, source-verified, elite-population instrument for evaluating every uncontrolled sleep-extension study in §4 — and for evaluating the corpus's own bullpen-based protocols.

**Note also:** the plateau sits at 9.0/10, near ceiling. A decrement measured from there is unlikely to be regression to the mean.

What that verified extraction says: **n = 11, one group, pre–post, no control, unblinded**, ≥10 h in bed with napping encouraged, following a 2–4 week baseline, over a **5–7 week** extension period during a competitive basketball season. ## §5 — READING A SLEEP-EXTENSION STUDY AT SOURCE: what the review's table does not show you

**Swinbourne R, Miller J, Smart D, Dulson DK, Gill N (2018). "The Effects of Sleep Extension on Sleep, Performance, Immunity and Physical Stress in Rugby Players." *Sports* 6(2):42. PMCID PMC6026815. PMID 29910346.**
**SOURCE-VERIFIED — full text retrieved from the PMC OA bucket and read.**

n = 25 professional rugby union players, two three-week pre-season blocks: **a control phase, then the sleep-extension phase, in the same athletes, always in that order.** The intervention is therefore confounded with pre-season training progression — the same structural flaw as Mah.

**The compliance number is the most useful thing in the paper.** Target: **10 h of sleep**. Achieved (actigraphy, n = 18):

| | Control (n1 → n2) | Sleep extension (n1 → n2) |
|---|---|---|
| Time in bed | 516 → 488 min | 529 → **549 min (9.1 h)** |
| **Total sleep time** | 391 → **382 min (6.4 h)** | 422 → **441 min (7.4 h)** |
| Sleep efficiency | 75.0 → 79.4% | 79.9 → 80.8% |
| Wake after sleep onset | 103 → 113 min | 93 → 99 min |

**A ten-hour prescription produced seven hours twenty-four minutes.** The gain over control was ~55–60 min/night, bought with **9.1 hours in bed**. At ~80% sleep efficiency, every hour of sleep a coach wants costs about 75 minutes of schedule. **Write the schedule against efficiency, not against the target.**

### Three things wrong with the headline result, found by reading it

1. **The reaction-time result has no control comparison.** Table 5 is labelled "changes … **after sleep extension** during a three-week block" — pre vs post **inside the intervention arm only**. The control block's PVT is not tabulated. Mean RT 228.8 ± 24.5 → 220.0 ± 18.4 ms; fastest 10% 180.4 → 175.4 ms; errors 2.6 ± 3.0 → 0.8 ± 1.1. **A PVT administered three times in three weeks has practice effects.** This uncontrolled pre–post is the result the 2023 review counts as positive evidence for sleep extension on cognitive performance.
2. **Table 5 reports n = 27 in a study that enrolled 25.** Table 1 n = 25, Table 3 n = 18, Table 4 n = 23, Table 5 n = 27. The reaction-time table's n exceeds the enrolled sample.
3. **A units error in the same table.** "Slowest 10% time (**ms**): 3.0 ± 0.5 → 3.3 ± 0.5." Those are not milliseconds. The values are consistent with reciprocal speed (responses·s⁻¹), in which case the "moderate increase" the text reports is an improvement and the label is wrong. **Either way, the published table does not say what its own column header says.**

Inference was by **magnitude-based inference with 90% confidence limits** throughout ("moderately larger improvement in sleep quality (−24.8%; ±54.1%)" — a ±54.1% limit around a −24.8% point estimate is an interval spanning zero by a wide margin, reported as a "moderate" effect).

**None of this makes sleep extension wrong.** It makes the published evidence for it weaker than the review's own summary table conveys — and the review, to its credit, said exactly that.

## §6 — ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY: why skill and power come apart, and what tissue has to do with it

**The mechanism claim in F-138 — "sleep is when the majority of growth hormone is secreted" — is true and nearly irrelevant to next-day output.** GH is secreted in pulses tied to slow-wave sleep, which is concentrated in the first third of the night and is homeostatically defended: delay bedtime and SWS pressure rises, so the shortened sleep is *more* SWS-dense, not less. That predicts exactly what Craven found — **early restriction spares maximal power almost perfectly** (−0.50%, CI −2.00 to +1.00). The GH story survives, and it buys the coach nothing for tomorrow's start. It is a chronic tissue-remodelling argument on a timescale of weeks, not a Friday-night argument.

**What actually fails on a short night is the cortical side of a motor skill, not the contractile side.** A maximum-effort throw is a largely pre-programmed ballistic action: recruit everything, fire the sequence. Its limiting quantities — motor-unit recruitment at max, rate of force development, elastic return through the lead-leg block — are properties the nervous system can produce in a degraded state because they are all-out and brief. Target accuracy is not that. It requires sustained attentional set, online correction, and the suppression of variability across repetitions — precisely the functions that degrade first under sleep pressure.

**That makes a testable prediction the corpus should hold onto:** under sleep loss, an 85+ arm's **peak** velocity should be nearly intact while his **pitch-to-pitch variability** — release point, location scatter, and the late-inning drop-off — should widen. Cook's own observation points the same way: "a general trend to a drop-off in accuracy later in the repeats (second 10 of the 20 repeats)." **The deficit shows up as a failure to hold the skill, not as an inability to produce it.**

**One line on injury, then done, per the mission.** F-138's 1.7× injury-odds figure is adolescent and off-population; nothing today improves it. The performance case stands on its own and does not need it.

## §7 — THE STRUCTURAL RESULT OF THE CYCLE: the transfer step, and which half of the sleep literature survives it

This is the part the corpus should keep longest, and it comes from joining today's reading to findings already in the vault.

The sleep literature reports two kinds of outcome:

**Power outcomes** — CMJ height, squat jump, Wingate peak, sprint times. These are what Mah measured (282-ft sprint), what the Baylor D1 baseball study measured (weekly peak power output), what Craven's largest categories measure. **To reach a pitching coach, every one of them must pass through a transfer step from jump/sprint power to ball velocity.** The corpus has already priced that step and it is bad:
- **F-004:** bilateral vertical jump height has **no significant relationship** with fastball velocity (r ≈ 0.07). "An athlete who added two inches to his vertical moved a variable with r = 0.07."
- **F-005:** relative CMJ power r = 0.19 NS; only *absolute* power reaches r = 0.43.
- **F-359:** in a source-verified simulated nine-inning game, **117 maximum-effort pitches moved ball velocity and hip strength and did not move a single squat-jump variable.** The jump test is blind to pitching fatigue.

**Skill outcomes** — dart throwing, tennis serving, rugby passing through a hoop, free throws, rifle shooting. **These require no transfer step at all.** They *are* target accuracy under competitive-athlete conditions. The construct is command.

**So the two halves of the sleep literature are not equally useful, and they are useful in the opposite order from how the industry quotes them.** The half everyone cites at pitchers — sleep and power — is the half whose outcome variable this corpus has independently shown does not predict velocity and does not even register pitching fatigue. The half nobody cites at pitchers — sleep and target skill — is the half with the direct construct match, the largest effect in the meta-analysis, and the one elite-population randomized trial.

**Conclusion, stated at the confidence it deserves:** the defensible sleep claim for an 85+ arm is a **command** claim, not a velocity claim. It is EMERGING, not ESTABLISHED, because it rests on an off-population construct match. But it is better supported than the velocity claim, which is not merely unproven — it is unproven *and* routed through a transfer coefficient the corpus has already measured as near zero.

## §8 — CIRCADIAN TIMING: one crossover worth noticing and one causal claim formally contested

### (a) Chronotype × game time in MLB pitchers — WEAK, and the asymmetry is the problem

⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY. Not opened at source.** A 2010 SLEEP-meeting presentation (reported via AASM and ScienceDaily, both egress-blocked; content reached only through search summaries) reports, for MLB pitchers by questionnaire chronotype:

| Game start | Morning types | Evening types |
|---|---|---|
| Before 7 pm | **3.06 ERA** | 3.49 ERA |
| 7 pm or later | 4.15 ERA | **4.07 ERA** |

The crossover form is the right signature — a real circadian effect should flip with start time rather than favour one group everywhere. **But the two halves are wildly unequal: 0.43 ERA in day games, 0.08 ERA at night.** A crossover carried almost entirely by one cell is equally consistent with "morning types happen to pitch better in day games for non-circadian reasons." **No sample size, no innings count, and no confidence interval is retrievable.** ERA is among the noisiest outcomes in baseball; a 0.43 ERA gap needs thousands of innings to separate from nothing.

**Conference abstract, twelve-plus years old, magnitude unverifiable. Registered WEAK. Not actionable.** It is exactly the "real-but-thin source" the standing brief warns about: the study is real and the number is not evidence for a magnitude.

### (b) Jet lag in MLB — a real paper whose causal reading was contested in print

**Song A, Severini T, Allada R (2017). "How jet lag impairs Major League Baseball performance." *PNAS* 114(6):1407–1412.** 20 seasons, 1992–2011. Reported effect: **eastward** travel degraded performance for both home and away teams, **specifically through pitchers allowing more home runs**, with few other pitching or defensive effects — an effect the authors describe as large enough to erase home-field advantage. Eastward > westward asymmetry is the authors' argument that the mechanism is circadian rather than travel per se.

**And then, in the same journal:** Joly, "Baseball and jet lag: Correlation does not imply causation," *PNAS* 114(16):E3168, arguing that the paper systematically presents correlations as causal and noting that **the word "correlation" does not appear once in the original**. Song, Severini and Allada replied ("Inferring causation from comprehensive analysis of observation") that retrospective analysis in natural settings is valuable where controlled trials are not possible.

⚠️ **All three items are SNIPPET-ONLY.** `pnas.org` is blocked; the letter and reply (PMC5402441, PMC5402407) returned `KeyCount 0` in the OA bucket.

**Why the corpus should care about the exchange more than the result.** This is an unusually clean external instance of the exact failure mode this program was built around: a large, careful, correctly-analysed observational study, published in a top journal, **written in causal language without a manipulation** — and formally called on it by a peer. The vault's own stride-length and extension corrections are the same error in a smaller pond. **This is a CROSS_SECTIONAL finding wearing INTERVENTION clothes, and the field noticed out loud.**

**Applied value for Ole Miss: near zero.** The SEC is one time zone wide, with occasional two-zone trips. The effect sizes are for three-plus-zone MLB travel. Filed for the argument, not the number.

## §9 — TRANSLATION: the coach's four questions

### Q1 (Coach → Biomechanist): "If my Friday starter sleeps five hours Thursday, do I lose velocity?"

**Answer: probably not much, and you cannot get a number out of this literature honestly.**

Anaerobic power under acute sleep loss: −6.26% overall (−9.10 to −3.41), but **−0.50% (−2.00 to +1.00) if he went to bed late and still woke at his normal time.** Then the chain breaks: those outcomes are jumps and Wingates, and **F-004 says jump height has r ≈ 0.07 with fastball velocity** while **F-359 says squat-jump variables do not move even after 117 maximum-effort pitches**. There is no transfer coefficient from a jump-power %Δ to mph, and the corpus has no business inventing one.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** a short night is not going to take your fastball, and I am not going to let you use it as an excuse for one. **The drill is:** nothing — this is a scheduling decision, not a training one. **On video the failure looks like:** it doesn't. Peak velocity is the wrong place to look for sleep loss, which is precisely why people who look there find nothing and conclude sleep does not matter. **Here's how we know it's working:** we don't measure this. Any velocity change you see after one bad night is inside the 0.8–1.2 mph within-outing SD the corpus already brackets (F-289). **One outing cannot detect it and neither can five.**

### Q2 (Coach → Anatomist): "Then what DOES it cost me?"

**Answer: the strike zone, on the best available evidence, which is off-population.**

Skill tasks −20.9% (−27.0 to −14.9) against power's −6.26%. Cook's elite rugby players lost accuracy on both sides at p < 0.001 after one 3–5 h night. The tasks are darts, serves, passes and free throws — target accuracy under pressure with no transfer step to command. And the loss appears late in a repetition set, not at the start.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** short sleep doesn't take the arm, it takes the aim — and it takes it in the sixth inning, not the first. **The drill is:** none. This is a behaviour, not a skill. **On video the failure looks like:** the misses stop being of one kind. His command misses have a signature when he's rested — arm-side, or up, whatever his pattern is. Tired, the misses scatter: the *variance* widens more than the *mean* moves. Watch release point scatter, not release point location. **Here's how we know it's working:** location SD in inches across a fixed pitch mix, split by the previous night's sleep — §10 has the count.

### Q3 (Coach → both): "Do I put the staff on a sleep-extension program?"

**Answer: not as a performance program. As a floor-protection policy, yes.**

The extension literature is **four studies, 57 athletes, three of them uncontrolled single-group pre–post, none in baseball, none with a throwing outcome, and the systematic review's own verdict is that not one is at low risk of bias.** Mah's 9% shooting gain sits on 11 players with no control group across five to seven weeks of in-season basketball practice — and Cook's Table 1 shows elite athletes gaining 1.7–2.6 points out of 10 on an accuracy task from repetition alone.

The **loss** literature is the opposite: 69 publications, 227 outcomes, controlled repeated-measures designs required for inclusion, sleep genuinely manipulated.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** I am not going to promise you velocity for sleeping more, because nobody has ever measured that. I'm going to protect your floor, because the evidence that losing sleep costs you is real and the evidence that gaining it above your normal helps you is four studies and fifty-seven guys. **The drill is:** a wake-time floor, not a bedtime rule — see Q4. **On video the failure looks like:** nothing on video. It looks like a 6 a.m. lift on the schedule the day before a start. **Here's how we know it's working:** Swinbourne's number — a 10-hour target produced 7.4 hours of sleep from 9.1 hours in bed. **If you're not tracking time in bed at ~80% efficiency, you are prescribing a dose you are not delivering.**

### Q4 (Coach → Biomechanist): "Bedtime or wake time — which one do I actually fight for?"

**Answer: wake time, and the reasoning is cleaner than the effect size.**

Three reasons, in descending confidence:
1. **Late restriction (early wake) is where the power cost sits**: anaerobic power −7.47% (−11.1 to −3.85) vs early restriction's −0.50% (−2.00 to +1.00) with I² ≈ 0.
2. **Time awake at first pitch scales the decrement**: −0.36%/h for restriction (−0.52 to −0.19), −0.55%/h for late restriction. A 6 a.m. wake before a 6 p.m. first pitch is 12 hours awake; a 9 a.m. wake is 9 hours. Three hours ≈ **1.1% (0.6–1.6%)** of additional exercise-performance decrement.
3. **A wake time is enforceable by a schedule. A bedtime is not.** You control the 6 a.m. lift. You do not control what a 20-year-old does at midnight.

⚠️ **Do not convert that 1.1% into mph.** It is a %Δ in pooled exercise performance, and §7 explains why that number cannot be routed to a radar gun. **The reason to do this is that it is free, not that the number is big.** Say it that way to the athlete or you will be selling him something you cannot deliver.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** I don't care much when you go to bed the night before you throw. I care a lot when you get up. Nothing on your schedule before 9:30 the day of a night start. **The drill is:** move the lift, don't move the bedtime. **On video the failure looks like:** nothing — it's on the team calendar, not the mound. **Here's how we know it's working:** we don't, at the individual level, and I'll say that out loud. It's free, it's mechanistically supported, and it costs one line on a schedule.

## §10 — DETECTION: what it would cost to settle this on your own mound

**Every recommendation above needs a measurable check with the sample size to detect it. Here it is, and one of these numbers is bad news.**

### Design A — binary command scoring (a target circle). **Unaffordable.**
A 25-pitch block scored as "% inside a 10-inch circle," baseline ≈ 40%. If the effect is Craven's skill estimate (−20.9% relative → 40% to 32%, an 8-point drop):

n per condition = (1.96·√(2·0.36·0.64) + 0.84·√(0.40·0.60 + 0.32·0.68))² / 0.08² ≈ **563 pitches per condition**, ~1,130 total. At 25 pitches a block, that is **45 blocks.** One pitcher cannot pay for that in a season. **This is the same arithmetic that killed zone rate as a leverage outcome in F-307 (~392 pitches per condition) and it kills it here.**

### Design B — continuous location SD. **Affordable, and this is the one to run.**
Detecting a **20% increase in location SD** (the continuous analogue of a 20% skill decrement) via a variance-ratio test at 80% power, α = 0.05:

n ≈ 2(z₀.₉₇₅ + z₀.₈₀)² / [ln(1.20²)]² = 2(2.80)² / (0.3646)² ≈ **118 pitches per condition.**

**Five 25-pitch bullpens after a short night and five after a normal night, per pitcher.** That is a few weeks of ordinary side work. **A 10% SD increase needs ~431 per condition** — so this design detects a large effect cheaply and a moderate one not at all. Say which you are testing before you start.

⚠️ **The assumption that makes Design B work is the corpus's own inference, not a finding:** that a 20% decrement on a pooled skill score corresponds to a 20% increase in pitch-location SD. **Nobody has measured that correspondence in any sport.** If the true correspondence is half that, the cost quadruples.

### Design C — the staff pool. **The realistic one.**
Twelve pitchers × ~47 pitches per condition ≈ 563 per condition, recovering Design A's binary scoring in **about three weeks of normal bullpen work** — at the cost of a between-athlete design with all the confounds that carries.

### Three instrument warnings before anyone starts
1. **Five familiarisation sessions minimum before the first scored block** (Cook Table 1, elite athletes, +1.7 to +2.6 points out of 10 from repetition alone). Anything measured before session five is learning.
2. **Do not use a jump mat as the readiness instrument.** F-359: 117 maximum-effort pitches moved velocity and hip strength and moved **no** squat-jump variable.
3. **Do not use zone rate.** F-309: it is contaminated by the catcher; F-310: ~2,500 taken pitches per catcher to resolve a half-inch. Release-point SD and location SD are continuous, catcher-independent, and cheaper per pitch — the corpus has now flagged this **seventh** time (F-264, F-289, F-295, F-300, F-307, the leverage gap of F-375, and here).

## §12 — CROSS-EXAMINATION

### BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST. "Your skill/power dissociation may be a denominator."
**The claim challenged:** that sleep loss physiologically spares peak output and attacks accuracy (§6).
**Why I doubt it:** Craven's primary outcome is %Δ of each task's own score. Wingate peak power baselines near 700 W. A free-throw or serve-accuracy task baselines at 35–70%. **The same absolute decrement produces a far larger %Δ on the accuracy task.** Part or all of the 20.9% vs 6.26% gap could be arithmetic. Heterogeneity of 94.1% on the skill pool is consistent with a metric that behaves differently across tasks.
**What would settle it:** re-express all 13 skill outcomes in within-subject SD units (Hedges' g) and re-pool. If the gap survives standardisation, it is physiology. Craven does not report standardised effects in the retrieved text.
**ANATOMIST — PARTIALLY CONCEDES.** The objection is valid and unanswerable with what was retrieved. **But the dissociation does not rest only on the pooled magnitudes.** Cook 2011 is a single study, single metric, single sample, and it found a significant accuracy fall in elite athletes after one 3–5 h night with no power measure to inflate against. And Craven's early-restriction cell is the load-bearing one: anaerobic power −0.50% with **I² = 0.04** is a precise null, not a small number on a big scale. **The direction stands; the ratio 20.9 : 6.26 is withdrawn as a quantity.** → **Dispute #31.**

### ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST. "Your time-awake deflation doesn't cover the skill data."
**The claim challenged:** that the early/late restriction split is a time-awake artifact rather than a sleep-architecture effect (§2).
**Why I doubt it:** the deflation works for power — but the skill meta-regression on time awake is **−0.02%/h, p = 0.975**, a flat line, while skill under early restriction is −23.9%. **If hours awake explained the pattern, skill should scale with it and it does not at all.** So there are two phenomena, and the biomechanist has deflated one of them.
**What would settle it:** a study crossing sleep timing (early vs late restriction) with test timing (fixed hours awake) on the same accuracy task. It does not exist.
**BIOMECHANIST — CONCEDES for skill, HOLDS for power.** The time-awake explanation is offered for the power dissociation only, where the regression supports it and the sleep-architecture story does not. For skill the deflation fails and I withdraw it. **Neither of us can explain why skill decrements are independent of hours awake.** → **Dispute #32.**

### COACH → BOTH. "You're about to hand me a command recommendation built on darts and tennis serves."
**The claim challenged:** §7's structural conclusion that sleep's defensible claim is a command claim.
**Why I doubt it:** the construct match is an argument, not a measurement. A rugby pass through a hoop after a 20 m sprint and a slider to the back foot of a right-handed hitter share a word — "accuracy." Nobody has shown they share a mechanism, a timescale or an effect size. **And the corpus has been burned exactly here before:** stride length and extension both died as levers because a plausible construct match was treated as a measured one.
**BIOMECHANIST — CONCEDES the class of risk, DEFENDS the asymmetry.** The concern is correct and §7 should not be read as a finding about pitching. **But the alternative is worse, and that is the actual argument.** The velocity claim needs the *same* leap of construct faith PLUS a transfer coefficient that F-004 measures at r ≈ 0.07 and F-359 shows does not register pitching fatigue at all. **The command claim is one unverified step from the data. The velocity claim is two, and the second one has been measured and failed.** The conclusion is comparative, and it is stated as EMERGING throughout.
**ANATOMIST — CONCURS, adds the falsifiable version.** §6's prediction is testable and cheap: under sleep loss, peak velocity holds and pitch-to-pitch variability widens. **If a pitcher's location SD does not move after a short night, §7 is wrong and the corpus should say so.** Design B in §10 is a five-bullpen test of exactly that.

### COACH → ANATOMIST. "You just told me to move a lift for 1.1%."
**Why I doubt it:** 1.1% of a pooled exercise-performance %Δ is, by §7's own argument, a number that cannot be routed to a radar gun. Selling a schedule change on it is the thing this program keeps catching other people doing.
**ANATOMIST — CONCEDES in full, and the recommendation survives on different grounds.** The 1.1% should not appear in anything said to an athlete, and Q4 was rewritten so it does not. **The wake-time floor is justified because it is free and mechanistically coherent, not because the effect has been sized.** A recommendation whose cost is one line on a calendar does not need a measured effect to clear its own bar. **A recommendation that costs training time would.**
