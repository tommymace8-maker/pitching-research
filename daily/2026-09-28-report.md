## 2. ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY PASS

**The mechanism by which mass could matter is momentum, and it is not mysterious.** The corpus already holds it: F-003 reports lean/body mass vs pitching linear momenta at **r = 0.71–0.83**, and F-005 is the null that proves the point — CMJ power *per kilogram* does not predict velocity (r = 0.19 NS) while the same sample's *absolute* power does (r = 0.43, p = .014). Momentum is mass × velocity. Divide the mass out and you delete the term carrying the signal.

**But that mechanism does not license a prescription, for one anatomical reason:** the mass that appears in the momentum term is the mass being *accelerated down the mound and then decelerated by the front leg*. Added mass that is not contractile does both jobs on the debit side — it must be accelerated by the same motor pool and arrested by the same lead-leg braking impulse. This is why F-002 (lean mass, r = 0.52) sits *below* F-001 (total mass, r = 0.58) and not above it, which is the opposite of what the "add lean tissue" story predicts. Nobody has explained that inversion. It may be noise at n = 33 — the two correlations are not statistically distinguishable at that sample size.

**On the deficit side, the adaptation timeline is the thing a coach can actually use.** Chronic underfeeding does not first take your peak force; it takes your capacity to repeat and your capacity to recover. The Espinar review states this directly and against the popular narrative: LEA "does not consistently impair maximal aerobic capacity (VO2max), peak power output, or anaerobic threshold, particularly during short-term or moderate energy restriction"; decrements appear in "training tolerance, fatigue, neuromuscular function, and explosive power... often arising through indirect mechanisms such as impaired recovery, reduced training quality, or low carbohydrate availability rather than uniform declines in maximal performance markers."

**Translated to a pitching staff: underfueling should not show up first in peak velocity. It should show up first in the back half of an outing, in the second start of a weekend, and in the fourth week of a block.** That is a specific, testable prediction, and it is the opposite of what the industry measures.

**RMR as a marker is a dead end for us.** The review notes the RMR-ratio < 0.90 criterion is inconsistent — "some athletes display metabolic suppression despite apparently adequate EI, whereas others maintain normal RMR despite confirmed LEA." It also needs indirect calorimetry. Not a dugout tool, and not a reliable one in a lab either.

## 3. BIOMECHANIST PASS — THE NUMBERS, AND THREE THINGS WRONG WITH THEM

### 3.1 The only controlled nutrition intervention in D1 baseball is a null (F-494)

**Cholewa/Rossi et al. 2015, ISSN Conference P44, PMC4595292, READ IN FULL.**

n = 30 resistance-trained NCAA D1 baseball players, **82.4 ± 8.2 kg, 183 ± 6.3 cm, 13.7 ± 5% body fat**. Twelve weeks, off-season. Fifteen received a 90-minute sports-nutrition education intervention plus reinforcement every three weeks; fifteen **matched for position** served as controls. All thirty did the same monitored strength (4 h/wk), conditioning (3 h/wk) and skills (20 h/wk) program.

| Outcome | Change (pooled) | Between groups |
|---|---|---|
| Fat-free mass | **+3.7 ± 3.6 kg** | **no difference** |
| Body mass | **+3.3 ± 4.8 kg** | **no difference** |
| Back squat 1RM | +25.5 ± 15.9 kg | **no difference** |
| Vertical jump | +0.144 ± 0.09 m | **no difference** |
| Broad jump | +0.135 ± 0.1 m | **no difference** |
| Body fat % | −1.2 ± 2.3 (intervention) vs +0.3 ± 1.7 (control) | intervention only |

Knowledge rose (56 ± 11% → 70 ± 9%). Intake rose to meet the assumed energy requirement (35.5 → 41.2 kcal/kg vs a 45 kcal/kg target) and protein rose (1.7 → 2.2 g/kg). **Carbohydrate did not move and never approached target: 3.6 → 3.8 g/kg against a 6 g/kg reference.**

**Read that table honestly.** Everything that moved, moved in both arms. The education arm got smarter, ate more protein, and lost a point of body fat. It did not gain more mass, squat more, or jump higher than the control arm. **The strength and mass gains belong to the 27 hours a week of training, not to the nutrition talk.** There is no velocity outcome anywhere in the study.

⚠️ **This is a conference abstract.** The sample is retrievable (n = 30, mass, body fat, design), which clears the corpus's abstract bar, but the peer-reviewed version — Rossi et al. 2017, *J Sports Sci Med* 16(1):60–68 — **could not be read** (PMC5358033 is not in the OA subset; jssm.org returned 000). A public summary of the 2017 paper matches the abstract on every claim above, but that is a snippet. **If the full text reports a between-group difference in FFM or body mass, F-494 is withdrawn with a dated correction.** Top of the verification queue.

### 3.2 The number worth more than the null: the SD of change (F-495, F-506)

The intervention result is a null. **The standard deviations are the finding.**

- **SD of 12-week body-mass change = 4.8 kg (10.6 lb)**
- **SD of 12-week fat-free-mass change = 3.6 kg (7.9 lb)**

That is what individual variation in mass response looks like in D1 baseball over one off-season block, and the corpus has never held it. It sets every detection threshold in this topic.

**The arithmetic (derived in-cycle, 80% power, α = .05 two-tailed):**

A pre-post team mean change is detectable only when it exceeds `2.8 × 4.8 / √n`:

| n | Smallest detectable team-mean mass change |
|---|---|
| 10 | 4.3 kg (9.4 lb) |
| **15** | **3.5 kg (7.7 lb)** |
| 20 | 3.0 kg (6.6 lb) |
| 30 | 2.5 kg (5.4 lb) |

And to compare **two strategies** with fifteen pitchers per arm: `2.8 × 4.8 × √(2/15)` = **4.9 kg (10.8 lb)**.

**So a college program cannot run a controlled test of its own nutrition plan on its own pitchers. Not this year, not ever.** To separate two reasonable nutrition strategies by 1 kg of mass change you would need roughly **362 pitchers per arm**. There are not 362 D1 pitchers in the SEC.

⚠️ **Registered against interest:** this SD comes from an abstract, from an off-season block, and includes BodPod measurement error, so the *biological* SD is smaller and these thresholds are conservative. Not by enough to change the conclusion. **The unit of decision here is the individual athlete's own trend, not the team's mean.**

### 3.3 A null nobody should cite, and an impossible number beside it (F-496, F-497)

**Merfeld et al. 2024, PMC11205132, READ IN FULL.** n = 12 collegiate players (6 pitchers, 6 position), 86.4 ± 11.1 kg, pre-post across a summer league season.

Table 3, verbatim:

| | Baseline | Post | ES | % change | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Body fat (%) | 11.36 ± 7.20 | 11.04 ± 5.57 | 0.05 | −2.82 | −4.05, 3.42 |
| Fat-free mass (kg) | 76.08 ± 9.56 | 76.04 ± 12.44 | 0.00 | −0.05 | **−4.40, 4.33** |
| Lower body power (W/kg) | 37.64 ± 9.24 | 54.50 ± 14.71 | 1.41 | **+44.79** | 7.36, 26.36 |

**The FFM row is the cleanest example of F-439 in the registry.** A change of −0.05 kg with an effect size of exactly 0.00 looks like the strongest possible evidence that players hold lean mass across a season. **Its confidence interval is ±4.4 kg.** The study could not have detected a 9-pound swing in either direction. It is not evidence that mass is held; it is evidence that n = 12 sees nothing. Anyone quoting "a study found no change in lean mass across a baseball season" is quoting an empty interval.

**And the power row cannot be physiology.** A **+44.79%** rise in countermovement-jump peak power, ES = 1.41, p = 0.01, across one summer league, in already-resistance-trained collegiate athletes, with **no training intervention described anywhere in the paper** — measured on force plates at 1000 Hz, same device, same protocol, hands-on-hips start with arm swing permitted at both visits. The paper offers no explanation and does not flag it.

The internal evidence is sufficient without appealing to any external norm: **the post value and the baseline value come from the same twelve men on the same equipment ten weeks apart, and they differ by nearly half.** Either the baseline test was contaminated (no familiarization session is described; these men had *just finished* a full collegiate season and were tested at the start of another one) or the processing changed. **Whatever it is, it is not a summer-league training effect.**

⚠️ **STANDING WARNING, and it reaches past this paper:** the corpus recommends force-plate monitoring (F-003, F-449). **A pre-post force-plate design without a familiarization session can manufacture an effect the size of a year of training.** Whoever runs F-449's within-athlete impulse design at Ole Miss must build in familiarization trials, or the first block will show a fake 40% gain.

### 3.4 The reductio that kills a 2025 headline (F-498, F-499)

**Iio et al. 2025, PMC11792141, READ IN FULL.** n = 92 Japanese collegiate baseball players, median body mass 75 kg, food-frequency questionnaire.

- **Median energy intake: 2,077.4 kcal/day** (IQR 1,959.0–2,262.4)
- Stated requirement for their body weight: **3,555 kcal/day**
- **Only 4 of 92 consumed ≥ 3,000 kcal**
- Median protein: **79.0 g/day** against a 105–150 g target
- Paper's conclusion: *"The study population had a large deficiency in energy."*

**Run the deficit forward.** 3,555 − 2,077 = **1,478 kcal/day**. At ~7,700 kcal per kg of tissue that is **0.19 kg/day = 1.34 kg/week = about 27 kg across a 20-week season.**

**These men are not losing 27 kg a season. 40.2% of the sample has a BMI between 25 and 30, and another 2.2% is above 30.** They are, if anything, heavy.

**Therefore the deficit is not real. The FFQ under-reports, by roughly 40%.** Which is precisely the documented behaviour of food-frequency questionnaires in athletes, and it means the protein figure is understated by the same factor — 79 g/day reported is plausibly ~110–130 g/day eaten, i.e. *inside* the target band, not far below it.

The paper's limitations section says only that "the data obtained were based on participants' own report. This may have introduced bias." **It never states the magnitude and never runs this check.** The abstract, the results, the discussion and the conclusion all treat 2,077 kcal as an intake.

🚨 **OPERATING RULE ADDED — F-499. WHEN A PAPER REPORTS AN ENERGY DEFICIT, CONVERT IT TO KILOGRAMS PER SEASON AND ASK WHETHER THE ATHLETES WOULD STILL EXIST.** It is one line of arithmetic. It just invalidated the headline conclusion of a 2025 peer-reviewed paper in the journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. It joins F-489 (evaluate a published regression at its own sample mean) and F-465 (difference the cells against the printed contrasts) as a thirty-second check that has now caught a published error on first use.

⚠️ **SAMPLE MISMATCH regardless:** Japanese collegiate, median 75 kg, no velocity reported anywhere, and the sample includes fourth-team players. **Directional only. Never a norm.**

### 3.5 Where the LEA threshold came from, and why it does not apply to a pitcher (F-500, F-501, F-502)

**Espinar et al. 2026, PMC12899827, READ IN FULL.**

`EA = (EI − EEE) / FFM`, in kcal·kg⁻¹ FFM·day⁻¹. The **< 30 kcal/kg FFM/day** cutoff that the entire RED-S industry runs on is, in the review's own words, **"Derived from controlled laboratory studies"** in **regularly menstruating women** (Loucks & Thuma 2003; Loucks & Heath 1994), and "should not be interpreted as a rigid diagnostic criterion." Its summary table is blunter: *"Derived from laboratory-controlled studies and sedentary women; not validated in free-living athletic settings; inconsistent threshold for determining LEA. Use as a conceptual tool, not a diagnostic cut-off value."*

The Melin/Burke paper adds that controlled LEA induction **in males** has been done at **15 kcal/kg FFM for 4–6 days**, with narrow outcome panels, and states plainly: *"It is possible males have a higher tolerance to LEA severity and/or duration and gender-specific thresholds are required."*

**There is no validated male threshold. There is certainly none for a 95 kg power athlete whose sport is 100 maximal efforts a week.**

**And the construct has a denominator problem that this corpus should recognise on sight.** EA subtracts exercise energy expenditure in the numerator. **A high-training day is mechanically a low-EA day at constant intake.** Vardardottir et al. 2024 (PMC11194298, read in full) confirms it empirically: across 19 male athletes with 6–7 complete days, *"The number of LEA days were associated with higher EEE."* **A starter's bullpen day and his start day are LEA days by construction.** This is a cousin of the geometric-identity hazard the corpus caught in the R² = .945 claim — a variable that moves because of what you put in its own formula.

In the same 19 athletes, with LEA days ranging 0–5 out of 7: ***"None of the body composition nor any of the other physiological measures were associated with the number of LEA days"*** and ***"no participant had clinically low levels of testosterone."*** ⚠️ n = 19. Far below F-439's n ≥ 97. **An uninformative null, registered as one** — it does not show LEA is harmless, it shows nineteen men cannot tell you.

**And the screening tool failed (F-502).** Melin/Burke et al. 2022, PMC9101736, n = 310 across ten countries, 64% elite. Verbatim: *"Sub section and total LEAM-Q scores were not different between LEA cases and control cohorts, with the exception of the sex drive score."* The single best-performing item, "sex drive in general" against total testosterone, runs **87% sensitivity and 26% specificity** — it flags nearly everyone, so a positive means nothing. The authors' own framing: *"The difficulty in validating this screening questionnaire may be due, in part, to the difficulty of identifying LEA in males."*

⚠️ **And check #1 disqualifies it for us anyway: the validation sample's VO2max is 68.1 ± 7.2 mL/kg/min.** That is a field of distance runners and cyclists. **SAMPLE MISMATCH, severe.** Do not put LEAM-Q in front of a pitcher. There is no validated instrument for this population and buying one is buying nothing.

### 3.6 The seasonal data says the mass loss is not the pitchers' (F-503)

**No n, no kg, no confidence intervals recovered. A lead, not a finding.** And note that a "pitchers did not change significantly" result in a roster-sized sample is exactly the underpowered null F-496 warns about — absence of significance in ~15 pitchers means very little.

## 4. THE MARKER-VS-LEVER STATEMENT (F-504) — the point of the whole cycle

> **Body mass is the strongest single physical correlate of fastball velocity in this corpus (F-001, r = 0.58, p = .0004, n = 33 NCAA D1, CROSS_SECTIONAL).**
> **Nobody has ever measured a within-athlete mass-change → velocity-change slope, in any population, at any level.**

Searched this cycle. Every returned source on "weight gain and pitching velocity" is a training-facility blog or a forum thread. There is no study. There is no coefficient. **There is no mph per pound and one cannot be quoted.**

This matters because the corpus has already run this exact failure and corrected it in writing. **F-442, 2026-09-22:** jump height is body mass divided out of impulse, body mass is the strongest correlate of velocity in the same sample, and reading the between-athlete correlation as a within-athlete transfer coefficient closed four channels that should never have been closed. **F-013, F-046:** stride length. **F-049:** open pelvis at foot contact, flagged in INDEX as sitting in the same position right now.

Body mass is the largest of these and the most commercially loaded. **Fifteenth entry in the missing-within-athlete-number pattern** (F-264 / F-289 / F-295 / F-307 / F-320 / F-336 / F-348 / F-363 / F-374 / F-385 / F-396 / F-457 / F-471 / F-493).

**What a coach is allowed to say:** *"Heavier college pitchers throw harder than lighter ones. That is real and it is the biggest single relationship in the literature. Nobody has ever shown that a given pitcher throws harder after he gains weight. Those are different sentences and I am not going to promise you the second one."*

## 6. THE COACH ASKS — translation

### Q1 (to the biomechanist): "Every year I watch guys come off the scale lighter in May than they were in January. You are telling me that is not a pitcher problem?"

**Answer:** I am telling you the published data does not show it *for pitchers*, and that the industry's number is uncited. Team-wide, body mass and lean mass fall across a season. Split by role, the decline sits with the **position players** (F-503) — who play five games a week to your starter's one outing. That is what the physiology predicts, and it is why the anatomist and I both expect the position-player result. I am **not** telling you your pitchers hold weight. F-496 shows the literature cannot see a 4 kg change, so "no significant change in pitchers" may simply be n = 15 failing to see something real.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"I'm not guessing about your weight. You're going to weigh yourself every morning, same conditions, and we're going to look at your seven-day average, not today's number."*
> **The drill is:** morning weight, post-void, pre-food, same scale, logged. Seven-day rolling mean. Flag at **−3 lb from a man's own four-week baseline**, not from a roster target.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing. **This one is invisible on video, which is the whole reason to weigh.** The visible tell, if it exists, is late in outings — see Q3.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** the seven-day mean stops trending down. And be honest about resolution — day-to-day hydration swing is on the order of a kilogram, so a single morning weight is noise and a seven-day mean is roughly the cheapest reliable signal you can buy. It is free.

### Q2 (to both): "Should we run our fueling plan as a team experiment? Measure it, prove it, sell it to the staff?"

**Answer: no, and the arithmetic is not close.** With SD of block mass change at 4.8 kg (F-495), fifteen pitchers detect a team mean change of **3.5 kg (7.7 lb)** and nothing smaller. Comparing two strategies at fifteen per arm needs **4.9 kg (10.8 lb)** of separation. Separating two *reasonable* plans by 1 kg would take roughly **362 pitchers per arm** (F-506). **You will never have the sample.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We're not proving this on the team. We're managing it on you."*
> **The drill is:** individual weekly trend, individual intake targets, no team-level A/B.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a coach presenting a team body-comp slide in May and claiming the plan worked. That slide cannot distinguish the plan from the calendar.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** you do not, at the team level, and you should stop pretending otherwise. **At the individual level you know it worked when his own seven-day mean holds inside ±3 lb across the season while his velocity band holds.** That is an honest claim; "our nutrition program added 4 pounds of lean mass" is not.

### Q3 (to the anatomist): "If a guy is underfed, where do I see it first? Give me something I can look at on Friday night."

**Answer: not in his peak velocity.** The review is explicit that short-to-moderate energy restriction does not consistently move peak power or maximal capacity; what moves is training tolerance, recovery and repeatability. **So the prediction is a back-half problem, not a first-inning problem.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"If you're not eating enough, the gun won't tell me in the first inning. Your fifth and sixth innings will tell me, and your second start of the weekend will tell me."*
> **The drill is:** log **fastball velocity by pitch number**, every outing, and compute each man's **innings 1–2 mean minus innings 5–6 mean** as one number per start. Overlay his seven-day weight trend.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing mechanical, early. Late, it is the ordinary fatigue signature the corpus already holds (F-127: command degrades *before* velocity).
> **Here's how we know it's working:** ⚠️ **be very careful here.** The within-outing fade is ~0.2 mph/inning from an 81 mph sample (F-384) and start-to-start SD is bracketed at 0.8–1.2 mph (F-289). **A single start proves nothing.** To see a 0.5 mph change in a man's own fade across a block you need on the order of **20–30 logged starts** — more than a season for one starter. **So this is a staff-level pattern check across a dozen arms, not an individual diagnosis.** I am registering that as a limit, not selling it as a test.

### Q4 (to the biomechanist): "Everyone is talking about energy availability and RED-S. Do I need to screen for it?"

**Answer: no, because there is nothing valid to screen with.** The 30 kcal/kg FFM threshold comes from short-term lab studies in regularly menstruating women and the 2026 review that surveys it says to use EA "as a conceptual tool, not a diagnostic cut-off value" (F-500). The only male-specific questionnaire failed its own validation on every subsection except sex drive, and its best item runs 26% specificity, in a sample whose VO2max is 68 (F-502). And the construct falls on hard training days by construction (F-501), so your best pitchers on their best weeks will score worst.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We're not doing a questionnaire. We're doing the scale and the plate."*
> **The drill is:** body mass trend + a protein floor. F-140 already holds the dosing: **~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day, split across 3–5 feedings**, and the corpus should note Rossi's D1 sample sat at **1.7 g/kg before any education and 2.2 g/kg after** — i.e. these athletes were already inside the band at baseline. The gap in Rossi was **carbohydrate**, which never got above 3.8 g/kg against a 6 g/kg reference, in either arm, after twelve weeks of education.
> **On video the failure looks like:** n/a.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** weight trend flat, and the man can complete his week. **Not a questionnaire score, and not an EA number.**

### Q5 (to the biomechanist, unprompted): "You just told me the mass correlation is the biggest thing in the file and also that I can't use it. What is it for?"

**Answer: it is for not taking mass away.** The asymmetry matters. There is no evidence that adding mass adds velocity within an athlete. There is a strong between-athlete association and a coherent momentum mechanism (F-003, F-005), which together make **unintentional mass loss** a defensible thing to defend against — you are protecting a variable that sits at the centre of the only mechanism anyone has. **Protecting a correlate is a much weaker claim than moving one, and it is the only claim this literature supports.** That distinction is the whole cycle.
