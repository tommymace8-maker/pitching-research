## §2 — THE RESEARCH

### §2a — The recovery deficit PLATEAUS. The industry's mechanism is the wrong shape. **[DERIVED]** (F-327)

Per-pitch depletion `d`, interval `T`, exponential resynthesis with time constant `τ`:

> **D_{n+1} = (D_n + d)·e^(−T/τ)** ⟹ **D\* = d / (e^(T/τ) − 1)**

Using the corpus's own F-132 half-time bracket (20–30 s ⟹ τ = 28.9–43.3 s):

**95% of steady state is reached by pitch 4.3–6.5 at the NCAA's 20 s, and by pitch 5.8–8.6 at MLB's 15 s.** Always inside the first inning or two.

**⟹ Whatever the clock costs metabolically is paid in the first inning and is FLAT thereafter. It cannot generate a progressive late-outing decline.**

Steady-state deficit vs an unclocked 25 s interval:

| Interval | Deficit | Spread across the **entire** τ bracket |
|---|---|---|
| 20 s (NCAA) | **1.35×** | 1.33 – 1.38 |
| 15 s (MLB empty) | **1.94×** | 1.89 – 2.02 |
| 12 s (Yang harm arm) | **2.53×** | 2.45 – 2.67 |
| 8 s | **4.03×** | 3.85 – 4.31 |

**The ratios survive not knowing τ** — the NCAA figure moves only from 1.33 to 1.38 across the whole bracket. **This is an F-307/F-317-class result: it survives not knowing the thing nobody knows.** Third instance; this is now the characteristic output of a blocked cycle, and arguably what one should be hunting for.

**And the penalty is convex, not linear.** 25→20 s costs +35%; 20→15 s another +44%; 20→12 s +88%. **There is no "seconds of rest" currency — 20 s is a materially different place on the curve from 12 s.**

⚠️ **Every figure is a multiple of `d`, which nobody has measured (F-336). The ratios are the output. There is no cell here to quote.**

### §2b — A clock removes the CHEAPEST seconds **[DERIVED]** (F-330)

dF/dt = (1/τ)e^(−t/τ) is decreasing, so: **the last 3 seconds of a 20-second interval buy 5.0 points of fast-phase recovery; the first 3 buy 8.0 — a ratio of 1.60×.**

A clock truncates the *end* of the interval. **It is the least damaging available way to remove a given quantity of rest — an argument for the rule that nobody in the sweep made.**

Cutting mean tempo by 2/3/5 s removes 6.7% / 10.1% / 16.8% of a ~50-minute in-game recovery budget. ⚠️ **And that budget is dominated by between-inning breaks, which the clock does not touch.** If inter-effort recovery were truly binding, the intervention with leverage would be the inning break.

**⟹ The only experiment ever run on this variable found no harm at the interval college baseball actually plays under.**

### §2d — The rule supplies its own natural experiment **[DERIVED from snippet inputs]** (F-328)

**Nobody in the sweep used the best available instrument, which the rule itself creates.** MLB 2023 ran **15 s bases empty / 20 s runners on** — the same pitchers, same season, a 5-second rule-imposed difference. Differencing removes everything common to the season.

Reported 2022→2023: **−0.3 mph bases empty, −0.2 mph runners on.**

> **Diff-in-diff = −0.10 mph per 5 s ⟹ ≈ 0.020 mph per second of rest.**
> **NCAA 20 s vs an unclocked 25 s ≈ 0.10 mph.**

**And it is an UPPER bound, because the confound shares its sign** — pitchers throw with higher intent with runners on, inflating that arm. F-069 bounds the delivery-type confound as small; nothing bounds intent.

⚠️ **Do not quote −0.3 / −0.2 as measurements.** What is durable is the **method** and the **direction**: the differenced effect is far smaller than the headline before/after.

### §2e — Why the headline claim fails **[DERIVED check on snippet inputs]** (F-333)

*"Of the 26 pitchers who improved tempo 5+ seconds, 22 (85%) lost velocity, vs 65% of everyone else."*

Checked: SE = 0.0935, **z = 2.10, p ≈ .036.** Marginal — then three objections:

1. **Forking paths.** "5 or more seconds" is a post-hoc cutpoint. **F-297** shows a modest scan produces a "significant" cell on a population with *no* true effect.
2. **Selection on the prior variable.** Those 26 are *by construction* 2022's slowest workers — plausibly older, where F-073/F-078 make velocity loss expected anyway.
3. **The baseline is also treated.** 65% of the "others" lost velocity too, in a season when everyone was clocked. **No untreated comparison group exists.**

**VERDICT: UNPROVEN. Do not repeat 85% vs 65%** — it is the most shareable number in the topic and will be quoted at this program.

⚠️ Also note: the raw −0.3 is an **uncontrolled pre-post on a league-wide treatment**. With ~300k pitches the league mean has SE ≈ **0.004 mph**, so the decline is statistically certain — **and certainty about a descriptive change says nothing about cause.** *Precision is not identification.* Known Correction #6 (Gdovin) in a new venue.

## §3 — TRANSLATE: the coach interrogates the other two

### §3a — *"Is the clock costing my guy velocity? Yes or no."*

**BIOMECHANIST:** On the best instrument available — the rule's own two-tier structure — **at most about 0.10 mph for the entire NCAA-versus-unclocked difference, and that is an upper bound** (F-328). That is inside the day-to-day noise of one radar gun.
**ANATOMIST:** And the mechanism agrees. The deficit plateaus by pitch ~5 and the clock removes the flattest seconds of the curve (F-327, F-330).
**COACH:** **So what I tell the pitcher is: the clock is not taking your velocity, and if you believe it is, that belief is costing you more than the clock is.** The drill is nothing — this one is a conversation, not a training block. **On video the failure looks like** a pitcher blaming the buzzer for a fastball that was 91 in February and 91 now. **How we know it's working:** we don't need to know; there is nothing to detect, which is the point.

### §3b — *"Then what DOES the clock cost me?"* **← THE CYCLE'S ONE EXECUTABLE ITEM** (F-331)

**ANATOMIST:** It costs you an instrument. F-127's fatigue sequence is *command drift → breaking-ball depth → slot drop → **longer time between pitches** → velocity last.* **The clock caps sign #4.**
**BIOMECHANIST:** And it censors it worst where you need it most. A pitcher at 18 s baseline whose fatigue would push +6 s can express only **+2 s — you see 33% of the signal.** The fast worker keeps the tell; **the slow worker, whom the clock actually binds, loses two-thirds of it.**
**COACH:** **So what I tell the pitcher is nothing** — telling him he's being timed changes the thing being measured. **The drill is a charting assignment:** one person on the scoreboard clock recording **seconds remaining when he starts his motion**, every pitch, one start. **On video the failure looks like** a guy starting at 8–10 seconds in the first and working at 2–3 in the fifth, with a compressed pre-pitch routine and a set position he never really settles into. **And here is how we know it's working: 31 charted pitches split early-vs-late detects a 3-second margin loss at 80% power.** σ = 2 s needs 14 pitches; σ = 4 s needs 56. **All three fit inside one start. The answer arrives the same night.**

⚠️ **The sign convention is inverted from the old tell.** Un-clocked fatigue made a pitcher *slower between pitches*; under a cap it makes him arrive at the buzzer with *less margin*.
⚠️ **UNVALIDATED.** F-127's ordering is itself only EMERGING, and nobody has tested clock margin as a fatigue proxy anywhere. **Chart it for a season before it removes anybody from a game.**

### §3c — *"What about the step-off? He's using it on runners."* (F-332)

**BIOMECHANIST:** The step-off that resets the clock and the step-off that permits a pickoff **are the same card.** NCAA reportedly allows **one per batter**; MLB two per PA.
**COACH:** **So what I tell the pitcher is: the card is for the clock, not for the runner.** F-322 already priced the pickoff — it needs a **28.5%** success rate and has **1–2%**, losing by **14–28×**, with ordinary pickoff EV ≈ zero and the whole case resting on an unmeasured deterrence channel (F-324). **Adding a second, immediate use for the same card can only make throwing over worse.** The drill is a live-runner bullpen where he is allowed exactly one disengagement per hitter and has to choose. **How we know it's working:** count disengagements per batter across a season and count clock violations; both should fall.
⚠️ **VERIFY THE COLLEGE CARD COUNT WITH COMPLIANCE AND UMPIRING FIRST.** The rule text is snippet-only, no NCAA PDF was opened, and the search summary asserted the 20-second limit applies in both base states **without a quoted snippet supporting it** — exactly the F-293 texture. **The structural point survives any card count; the count does not.**

### §3d — *"Should we train him to work faster?"*

**ANATOMIST:** There is no evidence either way, and the mechanism gives no reason to expect a training effect on the recovery side — the deficit is set by the interval, not by fitness, once he is at steady state.
**BIOMECHANIST:** And the sweep found **no tempo protocol anywhere in the industry** (F-335). There is nothing to copy and nothing to cite.
**COACH:** **Then the honest answer is: train the routine, not the tempo.** Whatever those extra seconds are worth, §2b says it is not metabolic — **it is procedural, and a procedure is trainable where a recovery curve is not.** **How we know it's working:** clock-margin variance should *fall* — a repeatable routine produces a repeatable margin. ⚠️ **That is a hypothesis I am stating as one.** No study supports it.

## §4 — CROSS-EXAMINE

**BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST.** *The plateau is an assumption wearing a result's clothes.* It needs constant `d`, a single exponential, and phosphagen to be the binding resource. A fatiguing muscle is less efficient, so `d` plausibly rises — **and then the "plateau" is a slow ramp, which is the cumulative story you claim to have killed.**
**→ CONCEDED IN PART, DEFENDED ON TIMESCALE.** The plateau arrives in 4–7 pitches; an outing is 100. **Any drift in `d` operates two orders of magnitude slower than equilibration, so `T` sets a LEVEL and fatigue sets a SLOPE, and they separate.** The registered sentence — *the clock shifts the level, it does not steepen the slope* — survives. **What does not survive is any claim the deficit is literally flat.** On the bi-exponential point: a slow component raises all deficits at 25 s and 20 s alike, and the output is a **ratio**, so it moves the common factor, not the comparison. On the third: F-127 already puts late fatigue in **neural** territory — **the model never claimed PCr explains late fatigue, only that it cannot explain the CLOCK's contribution to it.**

**COACH → HIMSELF, against his own recommendation (Dispute #25b).** *Clock margin is confounded by traffic.* A pitcher works quickly with nobody on and slowly in trouble, and late innings carry more traffic. **"Margin drops late" may be pure composition with no fatigue in it.**
**→ NOT RESOLVED, AND IT EXPOSED SOMETHING THE CORPUS HAD BEEN GETTING WRONG.** ⚠️ **In §2d a same-sign confound was HELPFUL — it converted an estimate into a clean upper bound. Here the identical structure is HARMFUL, because an instrument must fire on one cause and a same-sign confound manufactures false positives.** *Whether a same-sign confound helps or hurts depends on whether you are bounding a quantity or identifying a cause* — **and this corpus has been treating same-sign confounds as uniformly convenient.** The fix — compare within base state — yields ~17 bases-empty pitches per bucket against the 16 that σ = 3 s requires. **It just barely fits and fails outright at σ = 4 s.** Unlike F-298 the clean version is *nearly* free, but "nearly" is one bad assumption wide.

**ANATOMIST → COACH.** *You are recording an agreement between your own model and an unread study (§2c) and calling it "against interest," which is a way of having it both ways.*
**→ CONCEDED, AND THE ENTRY WAS NARROWED ON THE SPOT.** F-329 now states the convergence is **suggestive and nothing more**, and the two reasons it is recorded at all are stated explicitly: the routes are genuinely different, and **the prediction was derived before the study was found.** If Yang's performance outcome turns out to be accuracy rather than velocity, the convergence evaporates entirely — **and nobody in this program knows which it is.**
