## 2. ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS — DOES THIS WORK GIVEN THE TISSUE?

**The mechanism is not muscular and that is the whole point.** A nap does not add contractile protein, change tendon material properties, or alter motor-unit architecture; 20 to 90 minutes is three orders of magnitude short of any structural adaptation timeline in this corpus. What a nap plausibly does is **discharge accumulated adenosine and reduce homeostatic sleep pressure (process S)**, partially restoring central drive and attentional capacity for the remainder of the day. It is a **central-state** intervention, not a tissue intervention.

That has three consequences and they are all testable.

**(1) It predicts the outcome class.** A central-state agent should move what central state gates. In an expert performing a maximal single-effort ballistic task there is little recruitment reserve to unlock — which is exactly the argument F-392 made about caffeine, and F-391 is the measured confirmation that the argument is right: caffeine moved endurance and reaction time and did nothing to throwing velocity. **The nap should behave the same way.** The honest prior is that a nap's effect on peak velocity in an 85+ arm is near zero, and its effect on sustained accuracy and late-outing retention is where any effect lives. **The literature has measured the first class exhaustively and the second not once.**

**(2) It predicts the moderator, and this is F-631's logic reused.** A process-S intervention works on the *gap* between current sleep pressure and rested baseline. A well-slept pitcher has a small gap; a pitcher off a bus at 2 a.m. has a large one. **This is structurally identical to F-631** — an adaptation responds to load above habitual, and an acute restorative responds to deficit below habitual. **So restriction of range applies here too, and in the same direction: the published nap magnitudes, drawn largely from sleep-restricted or non-elite samples, are INFLATED relative to a well-slept, well-resourced SEC starter.** F-631's rule now has a third venue and a third form: correlations (Luera 2020), adaptation magnitudes (F-631), and now **acute ergogenic magnitudes**.

**(3) It predicts the one real harm, and names its antidote.** Waking out of slow-wave sleep produces **sleep inertia** — a transient, measurable decrement in alertness and motor output that dissipates over tens of minutes. A 20-minute nap usually stays in N1/N2 and avoids it; a 90-minute nap is one full sleep cycle and either lands past it or wakes the athlete mid-SWS depending on onset latency. **The inertia risk is a function of two variables — nap length and time from waking to performance — and the literature has confounded them** (§3, Dispute #67).

**Anatomist's verdict:** mechanism ESTABLISHED in general physiology, and it is the rare case in this corpus where the mechanism *predicts which outcome should move* and the literature has measured the other one.

## 3. BIOMECHANIST PASS — ATTACKING THE DATA

### 3.1 The literature is big by count and tiny by subject

| Synthesis | Studies | Participants | Per-trial n |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boukhris et al. 2024, *Sports Medicine* (PMC10933197) | 18 articles | not retrieved | — |
| Boukhris et al. 2025, *Biology of Sport*, pub. 2025-08-13 (PMC12884900) | 35 | **489** | **14.0** |
| Zheng, Ye, Li & Peng 2026, *Front Physiol* 10.3389/fphys.2026.1878224 (PMID 42558951) | 12 RCTs | **179** team-ball athletes | **14.9** |

**Per-trial n ≈ 12–15. That is the entire field.** The crossover detection floor, 80% power, α = .05 two-tailed, `n = 7.849/d_z² + 1`:

| n per trial | smallest detectable `d_z` |
|---|---|
| 12 | **0.845** |
| 14 | **0.777** |
| 15 | **0.749** |

**Nothing below `d_z` ≈ 0.75 is visible anywhere in this literature at the level of an individual trial.** Per F-439 and F-616, every reported positive is therefore a candidate for "reported at its own detection floor," and the magnitudes should not be quoted as expectations.

### 3.2 The internal tell — the non-significant effects are LARGER than the significant ones

This one needs no arithmetic and no full text. From Boukhris 2025's own abstract:

- **Significant:** total mood **SMD 0.61**, fatigue **0.91**, RPE during exercise **1.62**, RPE immediately after **1.11**, perceived recovery **1.66**.
- **Non-significant:** sleepiness **SMD 1.09**, muscle soreness **SMD 1.57**, heart rate during exercise **0.62**, temperature **0.66**.

**A meta-analysis in which a pooled SMD of 1.57 fails significance while a pooled SMD of 0.61 clears it has essentially no precision.** Heterogeneity is consuming the estimates. **The practical consequence is strict: the significance pattern in this literature cannot be read as a biological ordering of outcomes.** Anyone who says "naps improve mood and recovery but not soreness" is reading noise. ⚠️ Rated **moderate** certainty for RPE in the paper's own GRADE table per one snippet; the remaining GRADE rows were not retrievable.

### 3.3 Where the significant effects actually sit, and what they are

- **Boukhris 2024** — 5-m shuttle run: highest distance **ES 1.026** (p < .001), total distance **ES 0.737** (p < .001), fatigue index **ES 0.839** (p = .008). **Muscle force: ES 0.175, p = .267 — NULL.**
- **Zheng 2026** — five domains (cognitive, agility, power, endurance, technical skill). **Agility 0.55 [−0.25, 1.34], p = .096** — a confidence interval **1.6 SD wide**. Endurance reported around **SMD 0.96**; power/strength around **0.50** with the interval crossing zero. Per-domain study counts not retrievable.
- Earlier RCT meta-analysis: cognitive **SMD 0.69 [0.37, 1.00]**, I² = 71.5%; physical **SMD ≈ 0.99**; **no effect on CMJ or KAT after a 30-minute nap.**

**Read the pattern, not the headlines.** What survives is **repeated-effort endurance and fatigue resistance**. What does not survive is **muscle force (0.175, NS)** and **CMJ (null)**. The nap's reliable effects are in the outcome class that degrades across repeated efforts — and its nulls are in the single-maximal-effort class.

### 3.4 Check 1 — what was the sample's actual velocity?

**Unanswerable, and not because of reporting. There is no sample.** No nap study in any population has a baseball pitcher in it, and none has a throwing outcome of any kind (§5, F-653). Best population match in existence:

> **Teece et al. 2023, *Sleep* 46(12):zsad133 (PMC10710987).** n = **15 professional rugby union** athletes, randomized crossover, nap vs no-nap one week apart, nap at **1200 h**, between same-day training sessions. Baseline AM testing of reaction time, wellness and a **6-second cycle-ergometer peak power** test. Result: **improved afternoon peak power and reduced perceived fatigue, soreness and exertion.**

Professional, power-trained, adult — the right *kind* of athlete. **And the outcome is a 6-second cycle peak power test, which reaches mph only through a transfer step this corpus prices at [−0.280, +0.404] mph per unit (F-004/F-438).** Detection floor at n = 15: `d_z` = 0.749. **This is the F-590 shape exactly: the outcome is right for the intervention and unpriceable for the sport.**

One adjacent result in the same population class: in elite netball athletes, **CMJ peak velocity favoured a 20-minute nap over both no nap and a nap longer than 20 minutes** — the only elite-sample dose comparison located, and it points short.

### 3.5 Check 3 — does the number measure what the sentence says?

Twice, no.

- **"Napping improves physical performance" → it improves the 5-m shuttle run.** The 5MSRT is a repeated-sprint fatigue protocol. That is one test of one quality. **Muscle force in the same meta-analysis: 0.175, NS.** A sentence that says "physical performance" and a number that measures repeated-sprint decrement are not the same claim.
- **"Napping improves recovery" → athletes said they felt recovered.** Perceived recovery **SMD 1.66** and RPE **1.62** are the two largest effects in the whole topic and both are **self-report inside an unblindable intervention.** An athlete who knows he just napped will rate his fatigue lower. **There is no placebo nap.** The large effects in this literature are concentrated precisely in the outcomes least protected against expectancy — and the objective ones (heart rate 0.62 NS, temperature 0.66 NS, muscle force 0.175 NS) are the nulls.

### 3.6 Check 4 — is any R² a restated identity?

No geometry in this literature and nothing to catch. But the widened form of Check 4 from F-598 — *can this outcome fail?* — does bite on **sleepiness**: a nap-vs-no-nap comparison scored on self-reported sleepiness is close to the test being the treatment. Note that it was non-significant anyway (SMD 1.09, NS), which tells you more about the precision than about sleepiness.

## 4. THE ONE PLACE THE LITERATURE IS GENUINELY MANIPULATED — AND THE DOSE INVERTS

**This is the finding of the day and it is a real conditional lever.** The trials are randomized, within-subject, controlled, and the variable was genuinely moved. `CAUSALITY: INTERVENTION`. In a corpus running 30:1 cross-sectional, that alone is worth the cycle.

**AFTER A NORMAL NIGHT — short is favoured, long is the risk.**
Romdhani et al., **n = 14 judokas**, post-lunch dip: a **20-minute** nap opportunity improved RAST anaerobic sprint, multiple-choice reaction time and antioxidant defence, and lowered sleepiness. A **90-minute** nap opportunity was associated with **decreased repeated-sprint performance and increased sleepiness**, which the authors attribute to **sleep inertia**. **Testing began 30 minutes after waking.**

**AFTER A SHORT NIGHT — the dose inverts and long wins.**
Romdhani's sleep-restricted work, as cited by a 2025 female-athlete RCT (*J Sports Sci*, 10.1080/02640414.2025.2464440): **both 20- and 90-minute naps promoted recovery and improved repeated-sprint performance, reaction time and mood, with the 90-minute nap having the stronger effect.** And after **total sleep deprivation** (Apunts Sports Medicine 2026, S2666506926000015, 40/60/90-minute conditions, **male university soccer players**): the **90-minute nap produced the largest gains** — higher CMJ, faster RSA (TST/MST/BST), lower HRmax, lactate and RPE, **p < .001** against control and both shorter naps.

**And one trial contradicts the first block outright.** *Impact of 30-minute and 90-minute Naps on Aerobic and Anaerobic Intermittent Performance in Collegiate Soccer Players* (PMC12034280), **normal night sleep**: **both** naps significantly improved anaerobic sprint, and **only the 90-minute nap significantly reduced the fatigue index.** Same sleep status as the judoka trial, opposite verdict on 90 minutes.

**So the dose question is open and the candidate discriminator is not dose at all — it is the wake-to-test buffer.** Reviews recommend ≥30 minutes to clear inertia; one argues ≥60 minutes after a 90-minute nap. The trial that found harm tested at **30 minutes post-wake**. Whether the other trials used a longer buffer is **not retrievable from snippets**. → **Dispute #67.**

⚠️ **One extrapolation refused.** The Apunts dose-response came after **total sleep deprivation**. A pitcher off a Sunday getaway bus is sleep-*restricted*, not deprived. Romdhani's restriction arm licenses the direction at restriction level; **the Apunts magnitudes do not transfer and are not quoted as expectations.**

⚠️ **Population, said plainly.** Judokas, university soccer players, collegiate soccer players, female athletes, netballers, physical-education students, 15 professional rugby players. **SAMPLE MISMATCH throughout — directional only.** Not one 85+ arm, not one throw.

**Three meta-analyses. 35 + 12 + 18 studies. 489 + 179 participants. Zero accuracy outcomes. Zero throwing outcomes. Zero baseball.**

| Outcome class | Cost of ≤6 h sleep (F-400) | Measured in the nap literature? |

| **Skill / target accuracy** | **−20.9%** [−27.0, −14.9] | **never** |
| Anaerobic power | −6.26% [−9.10, −3.41] | exhaustively |

**And the time-of-day split sharpens it to a point.** F-400's skill decrement is **−14.2% in the AM and −22.9% in the PM.** The afternoon is when the deficit is largest *and* it is the only window a nap can occupy. **If a nap works by partially repaying sleep pressure, then F-400 predicts the nap's largest available effect is on accuracy, in the afternoon — and that is the one cell of the table nobody has ever looked in.**

## 7. TRANSLATION — THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "Give me one number I can defend to a pitcher who asks why he should nap."**

**You cannot, and you should not pretend otherwise.** The best population match (Teece 2023, n = 15 professional rugby) reports 6-second cycle peak power, and the mph exchange rate for that class of outcome is [−0.280, +0.404] (F-004/F-438) — it contains zero. **Boukhris 2024's muscle-force result is ES 0.175, p = .267.** The defensible sentence is negative and specific: *"No nap study in any sport has ever measured a throw, so nobody can tell you what it does to your fastball — and the one thing the literature does measure well, repeated-effort fatigue, is not your problem in the first inning."*

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"This isn't a velo thing. It's about what's left of your attention in the sixth inning of a 7 o'clock game when you got up at seven for class."* **The drill is** the schedule, not a movement. **On video the failure looks like** nothing at all — which is precisely why it must never be sold as a mechanical intervention. **Here's how we know it's working:** we don't, yet, at the individual level — see question 4.

**COACH → ANATOMIST: "Twenty minutes or ninety?"**

**It depends on last night, and that is the one thing the literature agrees on.** After a normal night, short: 20–25 minutes, which stays in light sleep and avoids inertia; the only measured *harm* in the topic is a 90-minute nap after a normal night (n = 14 judokas, repeated-sprint decline). After a short night — travel, a late midweek game, a Sunday getaway — longer is better, and the sleep-restricted trials favour 60–90 minutes.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Normal night, set the alarm for twenty-five minutes. Bad night, take the full ninety and build the buffer in."* **The drill is** a phone alarm and a known wake-to-throw gap. **On video the failure looks like** the first ten pitches of warm-up — heavy, behind the ball, late out of the glove hand. **That is sleep inertia, and the fix is the buffer, not skipping the nap.**

**COACH → BOTH: "How long after he wakes up before he throws?"**

**This is the live variable and it may be the whole story.** Reviews say ≥30 minutes; one argues ≥60 after a 90-minute nap. **But note what the harm trial actually did: it tested 30 minutes after waking.** A real pre-game nap ending at 14:30 before a 19:00 start has a **four-and-a-half-hour** buffer. **Every sleep-inertia result in this literature was measured at a wake-to-performance interval far shorter than a real game-day nap, which means the inertia warning may be a laboratory artifact that does not apply to Tommy's situation at all** (Dispute #67).

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Be awake and on your feet an hour before you pick up a ball. Longer if you slept the full ninety."* **The drill is** working backwards from first pitch: 19:00 first pitch → 18:10 bullpen → **wake by 16:00 at the latest**, which puts the nap in the 14:00–15:30 window the elite-sample results favour. **Treat the buffer as free insurance against the one measured harm, not as a correction for a problem we have confirmed exists.**

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "If I wanted to actually test this on my staff, what would it cost?"**

**This is the best answer in the cycle, because the nap is one of very few questions in this corpus a college program can actually run — but only if you change the outcome variable.**

- **Velocity outcome: forget it.** F-623's wall — 106 pitchers for 1 mph in a two-arm design. A paired design is cheaper (`n = 7.849/d_z² + 1`): **88 pitchers for `d_z` = 0.3, 32 for `d_z` = 0.5.** Thirty-two paired pitchers is conceivable for a staff pooled across a fall, but only if the true effect is half a standard deviation, and §3 gives no reason to believe that for peak velocity.
- **Command outcome, continuous: this is the affordable one.** F-408 Design B — **118 pitches per condition** for a 20% location-SD increase, **431** for 10%. ⚠️ F-408's price was powered against Craven's **full-night** −20.9% skill decrement. A nap repays a *fraction* of a *smaller* deficit, and the price scales as 1/effect². **Recover half the available decrement → ~431 per condition ≈ 17 bullpens of 25 each way. Recover a quarter → ~1,700 per condition, unaffordable.**
- ⚠️ **And every one of those numbers is a FLOOR.** F-482 item 7: pitches within one bullpen are not independent, the design effect is `1+(m−1)ρ`, and ρ for pitch location within a bullpen **is unpublished**. At m = 25 and ρ = 0.1 the multiplier is 3.4×.
- **Never use zone rate** (F-309 catcher contamination; F-310's ~2,500 taken pitches per catcher to resolve half an inch). **Never use a jump mat as the readiness instrument** (F-359). **Five familiarisation sessions before the first scored block** (F-404 — elite athletes gained +1.7 to +2.6 out of 10 from repetition alone).

> **So what I tell the pitcher is** nothing — this one is for the coach. **The measurable check is** location SD or release-point SD on nap days against no-nap days, logged on equipment the program already owns. **And the honest statement of what ~200 tracked pitches buys you is: a direction, not a verdict.** At 200 pitches per condition you can see a large effect and nothing else. **One bullpen proves nothing and three bullpens prove nothing.**

### THE ONE THING TO USE THIS WEEK

**A wake-to-throw rule, not a nap program.** The pitchers are already napping; what is unmanaged is when they get up.

1. **Night game (19:00 first pitch):** nap opportunity **14:00–15:00**, alarm at **25 minutes**, **on his feet and out of the room by 16:00** — a ≥2-hour buffer to the bullpen.
2. **After a short night** (travel, late midweek game, under ~6 h): extend to **60–90 minutes**, wake by **15:30**, and hold the same ≥2-hour buffer.
3. **Day game:** no nap. The window does not exist and nothing in the literature supports a morning nap before an early start.
4. **No caffeine inside the nap protocol for a starter** — §8, and F-391 says it does nothing for throwing velocity anyway.

**Why this is defensible despite the evidence being off-population.** This is **Dispute #41's ninth encounter** — *if the magnitude cannot be measured, is "adopt it anyway" a finding or an escape hatch?* — and it is the cleanest resolution it has had, because **the thing being prescribed is not a benefit, it is a timing rule for a behaviour that already happens.** The direction comes from manipulation rather than cross-section; the cost is zero; the one measured harm has a free mitigation. **What must NOT happen is this being written down as a velocity intervention or a command intervention. It is a schedule.**

## 8. THE CAFFEINE-NAP — A REAL TRIAL AND A BAD TRADE FOR A STARTER

The "caffeine nap" (caffeine immediately before a short nap, so absorption coincides with waking) now has a dedicated trial: ***Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 2026;36(4):408**, randomized placebo-controlled, **n = 13 physical education students**, four conditions — placebo, nap, caffeine, nap + caffeine. **The combination was best for explosive strength, not for repeated sprint with change of direction. Reaction time and subjective sleepiness were unaffected by any condition.**

**Three reasons it does not survive contact with a pitching staff.**

1. **n = 13 physical-education students.** Detection floor `d_z` ≈ 0.81. **SAMPLE MISMATCH — this is not an athlete sample at all**, and per §2's restriction-of-range argument the effect in a well-resourced 85+ arm should be at the small end of anything reported here.
2. **F-391 already settled the throwing half.** Caffeine at 3 and 6 mg/kg moved intermittent running ~23–29% and reaction time ~18.5% and moved **maximal throwing velocity by essentially nothing**, with positive controls firing in the same session. **Adding caffeine to a nap adds the drug this corpus has already measured as null for the output we care about.**
3. **The cost is paid on the night of a five-day rotation.** A systematic review of **nine RCTs** in athletes reports **increased sleep-onset latency** with caffeine, and one trial reports **391.4 ± 96.9 vs 464.3 ± 48.9 minutes** of sleep — **a −72.9-minute deficit.** A 5 mg/kg dose at 15:00 for a 19:00 start is still circulating at bedtime. **Set that against F-400: the thing you are buying with caffeine is power, priced at −6.26% per short night; the thing you are spending is sleep, and the skill cost of a short night is −20.9%.** You are trading at roughly 3:1 against yourself.

**Verdict: the nap is free. The caffeine half is not, and it buys the wrong column of F-400's table.** A reliever who needs 20 minutes of alertness at 21:00 is a different question and is not answered here.

## 9. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST. The claim: "the nap's largest available effect is on accuracy" (§5).**
**Why I doubt it.** Nothing in the nap literature measured skill, so the prediction is a pure extrapolation from F-400's *weakest* cell — 9 studies, n = 146, mixed athlete and non-athlete, spanning 1975–2021, **I² = 94.1**. And the mechanism is not even the same: F-400 removed sleep, you are proposing to give a fraction of it back, and recovery of a function is not guaranteed to be symmetric with its loss. **What would settle it:** any nap trial, any population, with a target-accuracy outcome. One instrument on a trial somebody is already running. **ANATOMIST: CONCEDED as to grade, DEFENDED as to direction.** It is registered as `EMERGING`, as a prediction, with the I² and the pool stated. The asymmetry objection is real and is now in Dispute #68.

**ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST. The claim: "the whole literature reports at its detection floor" (§3.1).**
**Why I doubt its reach.** The floor argument establishes that the *magnitudes* are unusable. It does not establish that the effects are *absent*, and your own framing keeps sliding between the two. A within-subject crossover on a state variable as reactive as sleep pressure is a legitimately high-effect-size situation — `d_z` ≈ 1.0 for "twenty minutes of sleep versus none" on an alertness-gated task is not implausible biology. **What would settle it:** one adequately powered trial, n ≈ 90 paired for `d_z` = 0.3. None exists. **BIOMECHANIST: CONCEDED.** The precise claim is that **no magnitude in this topic may be quoted as an expectation**, and §3.2's inversion (SMD 1.57 non-significant, SMD 0.61 significant) is the evidence for that, not for absence.

**COACH → BOTH. The claim: the sleep-inertia warning.**
**Why I doubt it applies here.** The one trial reporting harm tested **30 minutes after waking**. A real pre-game nap has a buffer of **three to five hours**. You have both been repeating a laboratory artifact as a coaching constraint, and a coach who takes it at face value will tell a pitcher off a bus not to sleep — which is **the exact population the sleep-restricted trials say benefits most.** **What would settle it:** the wake-to-test interval in each trial, which is one methods sentence per paper. **BOTH: CONCEDED, and it changed the prescription** — §7 now frames the buffer as free insurance rather than a correction, and Dispute #67 records the confound. ⚠️ **The coach's point does not license dropping the buffer, because the collegiate-soccer trial found 90 minutes helpful and the judoka trial found it harmful under the same sleep status; until that is resolved the buffer is the cheap hedge.**

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST, second challenge. The claim: "a college program can run this" (§7, question 4).**
**Why I doubt it.** You priced it at 17 bullpens per condition under an assumption you invented — that a nap recovers half the available skill decrement. You have no basis for one half rather than one quarter, and at one quarter the study is unaffordable. **BIOMECHANIST: CONCEDED, and it is stated as a bracket in F-654 rather than a price.** The defensible version is narrower and still useful: **the command-side crossover is the only design in this topic whose cost is within an order of magnitude of a college season, and the velocity-side design is not.** That is a claim about *ranking*, not about affordability.
