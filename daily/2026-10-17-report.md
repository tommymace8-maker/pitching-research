## 1. ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY — does this work given the tissue?

**The mechanism for a pull-down is real and it is not mysterious: it is overspeed.** A run-up adds translational momentum to the whole system, the arm is carried into layback with more incoming energy, and ball speed rises. The tissue question is whether a repeated supra-mound output teaches the system to produce more output *from a standing start*. That is an open question everywhere in sport, and the honest anatomical position is that **overspeed work has a coherent mechanism and no demonstrated transfer in throwing.**

**Where I push back on the coach's enthusiasm.** The thing the run-up adds is linear momentum of the centre of mass. The thing the rubber permits is rotational impulse generated against a fixed back foot. **These are not the same force–time problem**, and the lower-limb demands differ in direction as well as magnitude. Training the first with high frequency does not obviously load the second.

**Where I push back on the biomechanist.** He will quote r = 0.164 (F-740) as if it settles the run-up question. It does not. **Centre-of-mass velocity is not a tissue quality — it is an outcome of one, and a between-athlete correlation on an outcome tells you nothing about an individual's adaptive ceiling.** Elite pitchers have already converged on near-maximal drift; that is what selection does. The absence of between-athlete spread is evidence about the *population*, not about the *lever*.

**The cost line, one sentence as the brief requires.** Static flat ground carries mound-level elbow and shoulder load at 82% of the velocity (F-739a) — the worst output-per-unit-load in the topic. Flat-ground work is not a light day, and a pull-down day is a max-intent throwing day in the workload ledger whatever the surface.

**Adaptation timeline, for honesty:** nothing in this literature ran longer than six weeks, and the only six-week randomised attempt did not move velocity in either arm (F-737).

## 2. BIOMECHANIST — attack the data

**Four checks, run on everything kept today.**

**Check 1 — what was the sample's actual velocity?** 🚨 **ZERO of the ten retrievable objects in this topic reports its sample's mean velocity.** Not Stone's 271 professional pitchers, not Slenker's 29 collegians, not Fleisig's 17 or his 25, not Leafblad's 60, not Cross's 19 professionals, not Zappa's 332 D1 arms, not the Linfield 20. **Ninth topic in this registry where that is true.** Every number below is therefore unplaceable against the 85 mph floor, and "collegiate" has meant 78 mph here before.

**Check 2 — control group, and did anyone manipulate it?**
- **Manipulated within athlete:** the distance and surface comparisons (every thrower threw every condition) — Slenker, Fleisig 2011, Fleisig 2016, Leafblad, Cross. **So the velocity ORDERING in F-739 is a real within-athlete effect.** What was manipulated is the *task*, not a trainable quality.
- **Randomised allocation with a velocity outcome: one object, a student abstract, n ≈ 10 per arm, no non-throwing control** (F-737).
- **Randomised allocation in a confirmed D1 pitching sample: one object, and it measured passive range of motion** (Luo 2021, F-738).
- **Manipulated pull-down dose with a mound-velocity outcome: zero. In any population, ever** (F-738).

**Check 3 — does the number measure what the sentence says?** Two catches.
- **"Long toss is dangerous past X feet"** rests on torque summed across throws whose technique was never recorded — and F-735 proves technique varies 60% vs 28% by style. That is the published objection at F-743, and it is correct on the face of it.
- **Leafblad's sleeper number: ≈91% of throwers had acceptable intra-thrower reliability for ball velocity, ≈79% for elbow torque.** The instrument is worse at the variable the whole workload literature is built on, and that was published by the same senior-author group that wrote the rebuttal defending it.

**Check 4 — is a high R² a geometric identity?** No R² above 0.1 appeared in this topic, which is itself the story. **r = 0.164 → R² ≈ 2.7%** (F-740).

**What I concede before the coach says it.** F-740 is CROSS_SECTIONAL and I will not let it be used as an instruction in either direction. A between-subject correlation in a range-restricted selected sample collapses whether or not the lever is real (Luera 2020: r = .17–.29, n = 149 pros). **Stride length and extension both died of this inference run the other way. I am not going to run it in reverse and pretend that is rigour.**

**The one number I would spend money on:** the within-athlete slope of forward COM velocity against ball speed. It has never been measured, and it is the only thing that would make the run-up a lever or kill it.

## 3. PITCHING-COACH — four pointed questions

**Q1. My guys pull down 5–6 mph above their mound number. Is that gap telling me anything about them?**
**Nobody knows, and that is a scandal, because the number is free.** The 5.7 mph figure (23 athletes, VeloU) replicated; the 6.1 mph / 57-pitcher figure failed its second pass and is UNVERIFIED (F-741). **No reliability estimate for the gap exists from any source.** If an individual's gap wanders ±2 mph between sessions, "his gap is big, he's leaking energy" is noise.
**→ So what I tell the pitcher:** *"Your pull-down and your mound number are two different throws — one of them you get a running start on, and the rules don't let you run."* **The drill is the measurement:** 10 pull-downs + 10 mound fastballs, same session, order counterbalanced, three sessions, six arms. **On video the failure looks like:** the trunk standing up at front foot contact, less forward flexion than on the mound, ball leaving on an arc. **How we know it's working:** we compute the within-athlete between-session SD of the gap. **With 10 of each, the smallest detectable change in one man's gap is ≈1.24 mph; to power a 1.0 mph change you'd need ≈31 throws of each per session, which is a day nobody should run.** (F-746)

**Q2. Jaeger's logic is that you free the arm up at distance and then compress it to 60 feet holding the arm speed. Is that wrong?**
**Unproven, and there is a specific reason to doubt the compression step.** The arm speed at distance is partly bought by running forward (F-739b, Fleisig's own attribution). You cannot bring the running start to the rubber. **The claim is not absurd — it is simply untested, in a literature that has never once run the trial** (F-738).
**→ What I tell the pitcher:** *"We'll keep the distance work because it's part of how you build throwing fitness. I'm not selling you a mile an hour for it."* **The failure on video:** his best "free" throws are arc throws and his delivery changes shape between the 150-ft throw and the 60-ft throw. **How we'd know:** we would not, from one block — F-737's design is the only one ever attempted and it was flat.

**Q3. Should he be at 150 feet on flat ground for three weeks before he gets on a mound in the fall?**
**Probably not, and this is the one place today changes a programme.** At ≈120 ft, long toss already loads the elbow like a mound pitch — two independent groups, two instruments (F-742). Leafblad's own conclusion is that **the mound can be introduced earlier, once he's comfortable at 120 ft**, because it adds no load he isn't already taking. And **the mound is the only surface ever shown to be worth ~2 mph** (F-464, randomised within athlete).
**→ What I tell the pitcher:** *"If you're comfortable at 120, we're not holding you off the mound to protect you — the mound isn't the escalation, the intent is."* **The failure on video:** a guy whose flat-ground delivery looks nothing like his mound delivery, running the ladder for three weeks and arriving on the mound with no mound reps. **How we'd know it's working:** his mound fastball mean, ≈16 fastballs per bucket to see 1.0 mph (F-289) — which a Friday starter supplies without special effort.

**Q4. Does a pull-down week cost me command on Friday?**
**Unknown, and nobody anywhere has looked.** Ten studies, zero accuracy outcomes (F-745). **The stated purpose of the pull-down phase is transfer into a precision task, and the precision half of its own rationale has never been measured.**
**→ What I tell the pitcher:** nothing, because I have nothing. **What I do:** log miss distance in inches with the sign of the miss (F-185's protocol, already in the vault) across the pull-down block and the week after. **Sample needed: ≈200 tracked pitches to see a 2-inch command change (F-186) — so one bullpen proves nothing, and a six-arm staff across a two-week block is the minimum unit.**

## 4. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY.** *"You called overspeed 'a coherent mechanism.' Name the measured adaptation. Not the plausible one — the measured one."*
**ANATOMY CONCEDES.** There isn't one in throwing. The nearest analogues are in cycling and sprinting, cross-sport, and this registry has already refused that kind of import twice. **Register overspeed-via-run-up as MECHANISM, not EMERGING.** **What would settle it:** the within-athlete COM-velocity→ball-speed slope, measured on one roster across a block. Free with markerless capture the program may already have.

**ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY → BIOMECHANIST.** *"You are about to let r = 0.164 do work it cannot do. In a sample selected for throwing hard, a near-zero between-athlete correlation is what you'd predict for a variable everyone has already maximised. You cannot read that as 'the run-up doesn't matter.'"*
**BIOMECHANIST CONCEDES, and had already stated it.** F-740's COACHING line carries the symmetric reading explicitly. **The agreed form of words: the pull-down radar number is not a mound number (that is settled by the task difference, F-739, not by the correlation), and the run-up's value as a trainable quality is UNMEASURED.**

**PITCHING-COACH → BIOMECHANIST.** *"You've got me measuring the reliability of a number instead of training anything. If the gap turns out reliable, what have I won?"*
**BIOMECHANIST, PARTLY CONCEDING.** A fair hit. If the gap is reliable you have won a free per-athlete descriptive and nothing more — reliability is necessary for the diagnosis, not sufficient. **The honest statement of what F-746 buys: it can KILL a practice the whole industry runs, and it cannot validate one.** That asymmetry is worth one afternoon and it is not worth a programme.

**PITCHING-COACH → ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY.** *"You told me flat ground is 'not a light day.' My trainers call it recovery. Which is it?"*
**ANATOMY DEFENDS.** Slenker: no significant difference in humeral internal rotation torque or elbow valgus load between any flat-ground distance and the mound, at 82% of the velocity (F-739a). **It is not a light day. It may still be a USEFUL day; those are different claims.** ⚠️ Snippet-only, n unverified (27 or 29), no sample velocity.

**BIOMECHANIST → PITCHING-COACH.** *"You want to put guys on the mound earlier on Leafblad's authority. That is an IMU study whose own reliability table says the torque channel is the weak one — and you're using the torque channel."*
**COACH CONCEDES THE INSTRUMENT, DEFENDS THE DECISION.** The 120-ft convergence comes from two groups with different instruments (F-742c, F-137), so the instrument objection does not sink it. **But the real warrant for "mound sooner" is not the torque number at all — it is F-464: the mound is worth ~2 mph and flat ground is not velocity work.** The torque finding only removes the reason to wait. **Restated that way it survives.**

## 5. DISPUTES

**NEW — #82: is the pull-down a velocity STIMULUS or only a velocity MEASUREMENT?** Anatomy: real overspeed mechanism, untested. Biomechanist: the added velocity comes from a translational component absent on the mound, so it trains a different task. Coach: either way the number is used as a test, which is the practice actually at stake. **Settled by:** a within-athlete COM-velocity→ball-speed slope, or a pull-down dose trial with a mound-velocity outcome (which has never existed).

**NEW — #83: does the pull-down→mound GAP carry per-athlete information, or is it measurement noise?** **Resolvable for free, this week** — F-746. The industry already acts on it.

**MOVED — #41 ("install the block, refuse the magnitude").** Yesterday's rule was: the magnitude is dispensable when the intervention is **free and reversible**. **Today supplies the contrasting case and the rule holds in the negative.** A pull-down block is NOT free — it is max-intent throwing load (F-739a, F-137, F-742) — **so the magnitude is NOT dispensable, and nothing may be installed on mechanism alone.** Two days, two directions, same rule. **That is the first time #41 has been applied to a case it REFUSES since 2026-10-04.**
