## 1. WHY THIS TOPIC IS STRUCTURALLY DIFFERENT — THE HEADLINE

This corpus has spent six weeks documenting one failure mode. F-571 (2026-10-03) found four randomised ROM trials in baseball players that **all measured ROM as the outcome**. F-582 (2026-10-04) promoted it from observation to prior: *applied baseball science measures the construct the intervention obviously moves, rather than a pitching output.*

**The plyometric literature does the exact opposite, and it is the first topic in the registry to do so.**

Controlled plyometric trials in throwers **measure ball velocity.** Carter 2007 measured mph in D1 baseball players. Cüre 2020 measured mph. Kim 2024 measured km/h. Deng 2023's meta-analysis pools *throwing velocity* as an outcome class. **The distal, sport-relevant, hard-to-fake outcome was measured, repeatedly, by independent groups.** That is the thing F-571 said nobody ever does.

**And the mechanism was never measured once.**

Not one plyometric trial with a throwing outcome reports **reactive strength index, drop-jump height, ground contact time, rate of force development, tendon stiffness, or any stretch-shortening-cycle variable** — in any population, at any level (searched this cycle, four angles). So:

> **F-006 says reactive strength is the only jump quality that survives into a professional pitching sample (drop jump → ball velocity R² = 0.30, n = 53 pros). The plyometric training literature is the literature that should convert that marker into a lever. It cannot, because it never measured the mediator.**

**This is the mirror image of F-571, and it is a worse position to be in than F-571 was**, because F-571's literature at least produced a clean manipulation check. Here we have a set of trials that moved the outcome and cannot say what they moved. We know plyometrics may buy ~2 mph. We do not know whether it did so through the SSC, through added high-intent throwing volume, through shoulder-rotator strength, or through nothing at all plus a significance filter. **Registered as F-721.**

## 2. THE EVIDENCE, TRIAL BY TRIAL — AND THE DETECTION FLOOR ON EACH

### 2.1 Carter et al. 2007 — the only controlled plyometric trial in collegiate baseball with a velocity outcome

**Carter AB, Kaminski TW, Douex AT Jr, Knight CA, Richards JG (2007), *J Strength Cond Res* 21(1):208–215.** "Effects of high volume upper extremity plyometric training on throwing velocity and functional strength ratios of the shoulder rotators in collegiate baseball players."

- **n = 24** NCAA Division I baseball players, assigned to plyometric or control after rank-ordering by concentric internal-rotation strength.
- **Intervention:** the "Ballistic Six" — six upper-extremity plyometric exercises, **2×/week**, during off-season training.
- **Control:** regular off-season training **without** plyometrics. **This is a real comparison arm, not a second active arm** — which already makes it better than four of the five baseball "velocity intervention" studies this corpus has audited.
- **Result: throwing velocity 83.15 → 85.15 mph (+2.00 mph), significant against control; control showed no significant change.**

**CHECK #1 — what was the sample's actual velocity? 83.15 mph at baseline.** This is the **closest population match in the entire velocity-intervention literature this corpus holds** — and it is still **below the 85 mph floor**. The group crossed 85 only at post-test. Read it as *directional for an 85+ arm, borderline rather than mismatched.*

**CHECK #2 — manipulated, with a control. YES. CAUSALITY: INTERVENTION.** One of very few in this registry.

**CHECK #3 — does the number measure what the sentence says?** Partly. "Throwing velocity" is not specified in any retrievable snippet as *mound fastball velocity* versus a crow-hop or flat-ground throw. Unknown. The study population is "collegiate baseball players," not "pitchers" — position players may be included. **Unknown and material.**

**CHECK #4 — geometric identity? No. Not applicable here.**

**THE DEFECT THAT MATTERS, AND IT IS ARITHMETIC (F-723).** Twelve per arm. The between-group difference in change scores detectable at 80% power, α = .05 two-tailed:

$$d_{\text{detectable}} = (z_{.975} + z_{.80})\sqrt{2/n} = 2.8016 \times \sqrt{2/12} = 1.144$$

Priced against a plausible SD of an 8-week velocity change:

| SD of change | Minimum detectable difference |
|---|---|
| 1.5 mph | **1.72 mph** |
| 1.75 mph | **2.00 mph** |
| 2.0 mph | **2.29 mph** |

**The study's detection floor is 1.7–2.3 mph. The study reported +2.0 mph.** The observed effect sits *inside* the floor bracket. That is the signature of the significance filter: at n = 12 per arm, an effect this size is the *smallest thing that could have been published*, so the published estimate is an upper bound on the truth, not an estimate of it. **The honest read of Carter 2007 is "the true effect is somewhere in [0, 2.0] mph and this design cannot resolve it further."**

⚠️ **AND A PARAPHRASE-CHAIN WARNING. Secondary sources disagree on the study's DURATION — most say 8 weeks, at least one says 6.** This corpus has already inherited one wrong claim from a third-party paraphrase of a source nobody read. The duration is the paper's single most important moderator (see §2.4). **Every number in this subsection is SNIPPET-ONLY and third-hand. `journals.lww.com` is NO-DNS. This is verification-queue item #1 out of today's cycle.**

### 2.2 Cüre, Sterlace & Griffiths 2020 — the one that looks like replication and is not

*Gaziantep University Journal of Sport Sciences.* "Implementation of plyometric exercises to improve throwing velocity of male youth baseball players."

- **n = 12 total. SIX per arm.** Convenience sample from two select-level teams, randomly split.
- 6-week upper-extremity plyometric program, quasi-experimental with a comparison group.
- **Experimental +2.44 mph (significant); comparison +0.59 mph (NS).**

**SAMPLE MISMATCH — YOUTH. Directional only, never a norm.** And then the arithmetic (F-725):

$$d_{\text{detectable}} = 2.8016 \times \sqrt{2/6} = 1.618 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{MDD} = 2.43\ \text{mph at SD} = 1.5$$

**The detection floor is 2.43 mph. The reported effect is 2.44 mph.** To one hundredth of a mile per hour, the study found the smallest effect it was capable of finding. **This is not corroboration of Carter. It is the same artifact at a third the sample size**, and it should move nobody's belief in either direction. Under F-439's n ≥ ~97 rule this is not even a usable null, let alone a usable positive.

### 2.3 Kim, Jaber & Yim 2024 — the fifth Type B in eight weeks

*Medical Science Monitor*, PMC11321950. "Comparison of the Effects of Compound Training, Plyometric Exercises, and Kettlebell Exercises on Strength, Power, Dynamic Balance, and Pitched Ball Velocity in 30 Male High School Baseball Pitchers Aged 16–19 Years."

- **n = 30, ten per arm, three arms, 4 weeks, 2×/week, July–August (in-season).**
- **NO NON-TRAINING CONTROL.** Three active arms only.
- **Compound training: ~128.4 → ~134.3 km/h = +5.9 km/h = +3.67 mph.** Plyometric and kettlebell arms: **+1.0 to 1.2 km/h ≈ +0.6 to 0.75 mph each.** Post hoc: compound > plyometric and > kettlebell.

**Four defects (F-724).**
1. **TYPE B — no no-training control.** The design this corpus corrected F-039, F-044, F-508, F-510 and F-596 for. **Fifth instance in eight weeks; Type B is a base rate, not an anomaly.**
2. **SAMPLE MISMATCH. Baseline 128.4 km/h = 79.8 mph.** Below the floor by five miles an hour.
3. **The magnitude is implausible. +3.67 mph in FOUR WEEKS, in-season, in 16–19-year-olds.** Compare: the entire controlled literature's best case is Carter's +2.0 in 6–8 weeks of off-season work.
4. **The plyometric arm's own result is below its own detection floor.** At n = 10/arm, $d = 2.8016\sqrt{2/10} = 1.253$, i.e. MDD ≈ 1.88 mph ≈ 3.0 km/h at SD 1.5. **The plyometric arm gained ~1.1 km/h — roughly a third of what this design could resolve.** So the paper's actual plyometric finding is *uninterpretable*, and the headline it is being used for in the field ("compound beats plyos") rests on comparing one arm that cleared the floor against two that could not have.

✅ **DO NOT QUOTE +5.9 km/h OR THE "COMPOUND BEATS PLYOS" RANKING IN ANY DIRECTION.** One Substack write-up of this paper claims the compound group improved "5–7×" more than the single-method groups. That ratio is two numbers below a detection floor divided into a third. It is noise with a multiplication sign in it.

### 2.5 Martínez-García et al. 2021 — the "large effect" that fits the corpus's inflation profile

*Int J Sports Sci Coach* 16(5), DOI 10.1177/17479541211002977. Strength training → throwing velocity in overhead throwers. **16 studies, 424 participants.** Randomised designs with a control group, ≥4 weeks supervised, sport-specific throwing velocity as the outcome.

**Pooled ES = 1.10 [0.64, 1.57], p < .00001.** Male 1.12; female 1.22. **Adults ES = 1.34; teenagers ES = 0.49 with a CI spanning zero.**

**424 / 16 = 26.5 participants per study ≈ 13 per arm.** That is the identical sample profile to Carter and to every trial in §2.2–2.3 — i.e. **a pooled estimate assembled entirely out of studies sitting on their own detection floors.** This corpus has twice acted on exactly this signature: **F-040 discarded a periodization meta-analysis at g = 3.445 as a small-study-bias artifact, and F-194 downgraded Differential Learning on single-lab and publication-bias grounds.** No Egger or trim-and-fill figure was retrievable. **And the same literature has produced a competing review reporting a "trivial" effect for resistance training on handball throwing performance (BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 2024, DOI 10.1186/s13102-024-00872-y).** ES 1.10 and "trivial" cannot both be right. **Treat 1.10 as inflated; EMERGING at best (F-729).**

⚠️ **No plyometric-vs-resistance-vs-combined modality subgroup was retrievable from either review.** The question a coach actually has — *is a plyometric minute better spent than a barbell minute?* — is not answerable from any retrievable source.

## 3. 🚨 THE METHOD RESULT OF THE CYCLE — YOU CANNOT RE-MULTIPLY A POOLED SMD BY YOUR OWN POPULATION'S SD (F-726)

This is the most portable thing the cycle produced and it applies to roughly every meta-analytic effect size in this registry.

A pooled standardised mean difference is $\text{SMD} = \Delta / SD_{\text{pooled}}$, where the denominator is **the between-subject SD of the samples that were pooled**. To get mph back out, you multiply by an SD. **Which one?**

- **Handball throwing velocity:** the samples run 45.9–51.3 mph (corpus F-396, F-398, Dispute #29). Between-player SD, order 2–3 mph on a ~48 mph mean.
- **Youth / unselected HS baseball:** 70–85 mph spread, between-player SD order **4–6 mph**.
- **An 85+ college staff — this corpus's population:** restriction of range bites hard. Luera 2020 (n = 149 pros) is the registry's anchor for what range restriction does; between-pitcher fastball SD in an all-hard-throwing group is order **2–3 mph**.

Now price **one** effect size across those denominators:

| SD used | ES 0.78 → | ES 1.05 → | ES 1.10 → |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5 mph (85+ restricted) | 1.95 mph | 2.63 mph | 2.75 mph |
| 5.0 mph (youth/mixed) | 3.90 mph | 5.25 mph | 5.50 mph |

**The same effect size yields answers 2.5× apart depending on whose SD you multiply by — and the SD you are entitled to use is the one from the pooled samples, not the one from your own roster.** So the conversion is not merely uncertain; **the standardised metric is not a transferable quantity across a population boundary at all.** What transfers, if anything, is the **raw mph change**.

**THE RULE, stated for the registry:** *when a finding crosses a population boundary, carry the RAW units and discard the standardised effect. An SMD is a within-literature comparison device, not a magnitude.*

⭐ **AND IT GENERATES THE SIXTH QUEUED STRUCTURAL AUDIT: how many of this registry's quoted SMDs were converted to mph, or read as if they transferred, across a population gap?** This is F-510's Type D defect (an exchange rate fitted across a competition-level gap) in standardised-effect clothing. It needs no egress — it is one pass over `FINDINGS.md`. **Added to the audit queue as item 6 and to Dispute #80.**

## 4. THE THREE AGENTS

### ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY — does this work given the tissue?

**The mechanism is the most plausible one in the whole velocity-training literature, and that is exactly why it needs policing.**

A pitch is a stretch-shortening-cycle event at nearly every joint, and the ones that matter are **fast** SSC: the lead-leg block is a **~50–80 ms braking event** (F-061, F-006 coaching note), and shoulder external-to-internal rotation reversal is faster still. Fast-SSC performance depends on (a) **tendon and aponeurosis stiffness** in series with the contracting muscle, (b) **pre-activation and stretch-reflex gain**, and (c) **rate of motor-unit recruitment** — not on the maximal force the muscle can eventually produce. Plyometric training is the only modality in common use whose *stimulus matches that time domain*. Heavy strength work loads the muscle for 300–1500 ms; a drop jump loads it for 150–250 ms; a Ballistic Six med-ball rebound throw for less.

**Three mechanistic cautions, and they all cut the same way.**

1. **The adaptation timeline for the series-elastic component is ~8–12 weeks, not 4.** This corpus's tendon work (F-628→F-645, `library/tendon-stiffness.md`) puts Achilles/patellar stiffness change at **8 weeks minimum** under a loading protocol designed for it. Deng's >7-week moderator is *independently consistent with the tissue timeline* — which is the single strongest piece of mechanistic corroboration in the topic, and it is corroboration of a *dose*, not of an *effect*.
2. **🚨 But the corpus's own tendon cycle established that NO TRIAL IN ANY POPULATION HAS EVER REPORTED A TENDON-STIFFNESS OUTCOME FOR THE ROTATOR CUFF OR LONG HEAD OF BICEPS (F-636), and that NOBODY HAS EVER RELATED TENDON OR MUSCLE STIFFNESS TO PITCH VELOCITY IN ANY POPULATION (F-635).** So the mechanism I just described has, at the shoulder, **never been measured in anyone.** It is borrowed from the lower limb of non-throwers. **MECHANISM grade, and it cannot be promoted.**
3. **Upper-extremity "plyometrics" may not be plyometric.** The Ballistic Six is med-ball and plyo-ball throwing. Whether a 1 kg med-ball rebound throw actually elicits a fast-SSC in the shoulder's series-elastic structures — or is simply a submaximal throw — **is unmeasured.** The label is anatomical folklore applied to a drill, and nobody has put EMG or ultrasound on it.

**Ceiling:** reactive-strength qualities are among the more trainable in the catalogue in *untrained* people and among the least trainable in athletes who already do ballistic work daily. **A pitcher throwing 85+ performs several hundred maximal-intent SSC events a week already.** The expected increment on top of that baseline is small, and the restriction-of-range logic that collapses correlations in hard-throwing samples applies to *adaptability* too (F-630/F-631 registered exactly this attenuation for tendon work). **An 85+ arm is the population where this should work least.**

### BIOMECHANIST — attack the data

**The data are better than this corpus is used to, and still cannot support a magnitude.**

- **Design quality: genuinely above the registry's average.** Carter has a real non-training control. Deng restricted inclusion to randomised designs with active controls. That is rarer in this field than it should be.
- **Sample sizes: uniformly at the detection floor.** 12, 6, 10 per arm; 13 per arm in the pooled 16-study set. **Every reported positive in this topic sits inside its own minimum detectable difference.** The two most quotable effects (+2.00 mph, +2.44 mph) are, to within rounding, *equal to* their studies' detection floors. A literature where every published effect equals its own detection floor is a literature whose effect sizes are determined by its sample sizes.
- **Between-subject design sold as a within-athlete lever?** No — and credit where due. These are pre-post change scores with a comparison arm. **This is a genuine within-athlete-change design, which is what this corpus has been asking for since F-634.** The problem is power, not identification.
- **The missing number is the same one that is missing everywhere: F-634.** No trial in this topic reports **between-subject SD alongside within-athlete change**. Without it, I cannot tell you whether +2.0 mph is a large or trivial within-athlete move for an 83 mph college arm, and I had to *assume* an SD-of-change bracket (1.5–2.0 mph) to compute every detection floor in §2. **That assumption is mine, not a published value, and it is stated rather than hidden.**
- **Check #1 is answerable here, which is unusual.** 83.15 mph (Carter), ~79.8 mph (Kim), unreported (Cüre, youth). **Not one trial is at or above 85.**
- **Check #4:** no geometric identities in this topic. Nothing to catch.
- **And the attribution conflict in §2.4 is the methodological event of the day.** A single open-access meta-analysis returned the same effect size for two different outcomes across two search passes. **If this corpus had run one search instead of two, it would have registered ES = 0.78 for throwing velocity today and been wrong, and a future cycle would have found it as Correction #16.** That near-miss is worth more than any number in the report.

### PITCHING COACH — can he feel it, can it survive a bullpen, is it worth a block?

**Yes, yes, and probably — and the honest sell is narrower than the industry's.**

What the pitcher experiences in Ballistic Six / plyo-ball work is **short, violent, rebound-driven throwing with no intent to locate**. It is feelable: the cue is *catch it and get rid of it*, and the athlete knows immediately whether he caught the rebound or absorbed it. **That is a rare property in this corpus** — most of what we register (open pelvis, extension, stride length) cannot be consciously felt and therefore cannot be coached directly.

It survives a bullpen because **it is not a mechanical change at all.** Nothing about the delivery is being altered. This is a *capacity* intervention, which is the category this program is explicitly built for ("mechanics already good — the work is refinement and capacity"). There is no transfer-lag problem, no re-learning window, no F-545-style transition period, and no risk that a cue installed in the weight room blows up in the seventh inning.

**The cost is real and it is not time — it is throw count.** A Ballistic Six block is **upper-extremity throwing volume that nobody is logging as throwing volume.** `library/non-fastball-workload.md` and F-493 exist because this program already knows that unaccounted throws are how arms get hurt. If a pitcher adds 2 sessions/week of med-ball and plyo-ball throws and the pitching coach's workload sheet does not show it, **the sheet is wrong by however many throws that is.** That is the one-line injury constraint, and it is a bookkeeping problem, not a biological one.

**What I will not say:** "plyos add 2 mph." The one trial that got +2.0 mph found exactly what it was powered to find, in a group averaging 83 mph, and the population it is being sold to averages 90.

## 5. THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS

### Q1 — "F-006 says drop jump predicts velocity at R² = 0.30 in pros. Carter says plyos add 2 mph. Isn't that the marker and the lever, finally matching up? Can I tell a kid that training reactive strength makes him throw harder?"

**NO, and the reason is the headline of this cycle.** You have two facts that *look* like they chain and do not touch:

- **F-006:** drop-jump variables → ball velocity, **R² = 0.30, n = 53 professionals** (Wong et al. 2023, *JSCR* 37(4):823–828, PMID 36026465). **CROSS-SECTIONAL. A marker.**
- **Carter 2007:** upper-extremity plyometric training → +2.0 mph, n = 24 D1, controlled. **INTERVENTION. A lever.**

**The chain requires a middle link that does not exist: did the plyometric training change reactive strength?** Carter did not measure RSI, drop jump, or ground contact time. Nor did Cüre, Kim, or any trial in Deng's 30. **And Carter's intervention was UPPER-extremity work, while F-006's marker is a LOWER-extremity drop jump.** They are not even in the same limb. Chaining them is inventing a mediator from two studies that share nothing but a direction.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Short, violent rebound work two days a week for eight weeks is one of the few things in the weight room with a real controlled study behind it in college baseball players. It's worth doing. I'm not promising you a number."*
> **The drill is:** Ballistic Six-pattern upper-extremity plyometrics, 2×/week, **8 weeks minimum — four will not do it**, in an off-season or early-pre-season block.
> **On video the failure looks like:** the athlete *absorbing* the med ball — a long, soft catch with the elbow collapsing and a visible pause before the return throw. If you can see a pause, the fast SSC is not happening and he is doing a strength exercise he thinks is a plyometric.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** not the radar gun (see Q3). **A contact mat and a drop-jump RSI, pre and post** — which is the measurement nobody in this literature has ever taken (§6).

### Q2 — "Driveline sells plyo balls, Tread sells med-ball work, everyone sells 'elasticity.' Is any of that the same thing as what Carter studied?"

**Partly, and the overlap is smaller than the marketing implies.** Driveline's own published guidance is explicit that **soft-covered PlyoCare balls are for submaximal throwing, restraint training and arm conditioning**, while **leather weighted balls** are the max-intent velocity tool — i.e. *their own* framing does not claim the plyo ball is the velocity driver. They also warn that throwing them at max effort without a progression "very possibly will not increase throwing velocity," and that on med-ball work, "when athletes start getting into 4, 5 or even 6 kg balls, movement quality and speed tend to suffer, negating many of the benefits," describing the overhead med-ball throw as **"a body positioning, explosiveness and energy transfer drill, not a strength exercise."**

**That is a vendor arguing against the heavier-is-better reading of its own product line, and it is consistent with the mechanism in §4: load the time domain, not the tissue.** Credit it.

**What nobody can tell you — including Driveline — is whether a plyo-ball session is a plyometric stimulus or just a throw.** See Dispute #79. **This matters practically**, because if it is "just a throw," then the right accounting is *throwing volume* and the right comparison is against long toss, which this corpus already holds.

### Q3 — "Fine. I run the 8-week block on my staff. Can I measure whether it worked?"

**On velocity, NO, and this is the arithmetic that should change what you do.**

Suppose you split a 12-pitcher staff 6/6, plyometrics vs normal training. From §2.2: your minimum detectable between-group difference is **~2.4 mph**. **You would fail to detect the entire effect that Carter reported**, and a null from your own staff would mean nothing (F-439).

Run it on all 12 with no control arm and you have a Type B design — and 8 weeks of off-season in a 19-year-old produces velocity gain *for reasons that have nothing to do with your intervention*, which is precisely why Carter's control arm is the valuable part of that paper.

**On the mediator, YES — and that is the recommendation.** Drop-jump RSI on a contact mat is cheap, repeatable, and has a far better signal-to-noise ratio than fastball velocity. Within-athlete:

$$\text{MDC}_{95} = 1.96 \times \sqrt{2} \times \text{CV}$$

| Assumed within-athlete CV of drop-jump RSI | MDC₉₅ for ONE athlete |
|---|---|
| 5% | **13.9%** |
| 6% | **16.6%** |
| 8% | **22.2%** |

**So: a single pitcher needs a >14–22% RSI change before you may call it real.** At a staff level (n = 12 paired pre/post), you detect a group mean change of roughly CV × 1.41 × 2.8 / √12 ≈ **4.5–7%** — comfortably inside what an 8-week block should produce if the mechanism is real.

⚠️ **AND THE CV IS NOT A PUBLISHED NUMBER FOR A PITCHER.** The 5–8% bracket is general athlete-population reliability, snippet-level, and this corpus does not hold a pitcher-specific figure. **That is the EIGHTEENTH entry in the F-264 / F-289 / F-295 / F-307 / F-320 / F-336 / F-348 / F-363 / F-374 / F-385 / F-396 / F-457 / F-471 / F-493 / F-504 / F-507 / F-600 pattern — a within-athlete variance that is already in the data of every program that owns a contact mat, gating an entire topic's detection arithmetic, published by nobody. It is four weeks of Tuesday jumps.**

### Q4 — "Give me the one-sentence version I can say to a 92 mph junior who asks whether plyos will make him throw harder."

## 6. 🚨 THE CHEAPEST UNRUN STUDY IN THE TOPIC — AND IT IS CHEAPER THAN MOST

**Three arms are not needed. One is.**

> **Take 12 pitchers at 85+. Measure drop-jump RSI on a contact mat and 20 mound fastballs on a radar gun. Run an 8-week Ballistic Six block, 2×/week. Re-measure both.**
>
> **Report the RSI change and the velocity change for each athlete, with the between-subject SD of both at baseline.**

That single report would, in one pass:
1. **Supply F-634's missing quantity** (between-subject SD and within-athlete change, reported together) for a pitching population, which this registry has said for seven days would "convert the question" of marker-vs-lever from argument into arithmetic.
2. **Supply the mediator that the entire plyometric throwing literature has never measured** (F-721), i.e. did the intervention change the thing it is theorised to change.
3. **Supply the eighteenth missing within-athlete variance** (the drop-jump RSI CV for a pitcher) as a by-product.
4. **Be the first plyometric measurement of any kind in a sample above 85 mph.**

**It needs a contact mat, a radar gun, and eight weeks.** It does not need a control group to answer (1), (3) or (4) — only (2)'s causal claim needs one, and the velocity arm is underpowered regardless, so **the honest version of this study does not pretend to test velocity at all.** That is why it is cheap: it stops trying to prove the thing it cannot afford to prove.

## 8. CROSS-EXAMINATION

### Challenge 1 — BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST
**"You called the >7-week moderator 'mechanistic corroboration' because it matches the 8-week tendon timeline. I say that's a coincidence you are dressing up as convergence."**
Reasoning: duration is collinear with total volume, with total throw count, and with season phase. A longer block is a bigger dose of *everything*. And your own citation (F-636) establishes that **no shoulder tendon's stiffness has ever been measured in any training trial**, so you have no timeline for the tissue Carter actually loaded — you borrowed the Achilles'.
**What would settle it:** a dose-matched duration comparison — same total volume delivered over 4 weeks vs 8 — or any ultrasound/shear-wave measurement of a shoulder tendon across a plyometric block.
**ANATOMIST — PARTIALLY CONCEDED.** The upper-extremity application is borrowed and is conceded as such; the finding is downgraded to **MECHANISM** and the word "corroboration" is withdrawn in favour of "consistent with." **Defended in one respect:** the lower-limb 8-week figure is not arbitrary and the direction of the moderator was predicted by the mechanism rather than fitted to it. **Logged in F-728 as a stated limit, and the dose-matched comparison is added to the gap list.**

### Challenge 2 — COACH → BIOMECHANIST
**"You have now told me every positive in this topic equals its own detection floor and therefore proves nothing. By that standard nothing in this literature can ever be acted on. At some point 'underpowered' becomes an excuse to never do anything."**
Reasoning: this is Dispute #41's sixth form. Carter has a real control arm, a near-population sample, and a plausible mechanism, and it is the best-designed velocity intervention in this corpus. If that is not actionable, the standard is unmeetable.
**BIOMECHANIST — CONCEDED ON ACTION, DEFENDED ON MAGNITUDE, and this is the resolution the cycle ships.** The floor argument does not say *do not do it*; it says *do not quote a number*. The two separate cleanly here because **the intervention is nearly free and carries no mechanical risk** — so the decision does not require the magnitude. **Install the block; refuse the mph figure.** This is not a dodge, because it is falsifiable in the other direction: if the intervention were expensive, or required changing the delivery, the same evidence would NOT license it. Compare 2026-10-04, where Dispute #41 *stopped* a prescription (F-587) because the prescription there was a day-by-day throwing map with real cost.

### Challenge 3 — ANATOMIST → COACH
**"You are about to add two sessions a week of maximal-intent upper-extremity throwing and call it a weight-room intervention. Carter's title says 'high volume.' You do not know what that volume was, and you are recommending it to pitchers who already throw at maximal intent three times a week."**
Reasoning: the Ballistic Six is *throwing*. The retrievable sources give the frequency (2×/week) and the exercise list and **not one of them gives a set, rep or throw count.** "High volume" is in the paper's own title and this corpus cannot read the paper.
**COACH — CONCEDED, and it changes the recommendation.** The prescription is amended to require that **the block's throws be logged on the same workload sheet as bullpen and game throws**, and that the block sit in the off-season or early pre-season, never on top of in-season game volume. **The unknown throw count is registered as a limit on F-722 and as a verification item:** the one number this corpus most needs out of Carter 2007 is not the p-value, it is the throw count.

### Challenge 4 — BIOMECHANIST → COACH (the one that is not conceded)
**"Your Q3 prescription is to measure RSI instead of velocity. But you have no evidence that an RSI change in a pitcher means anything about his pitching. You are substituting a measurable surrogate for an unmeasurable outcome and calling it progress — which is the exact error F-590 caught VBT making with CMJ height."**
Reasoning: F-590 established that the **exchange rate from jump performance to mph is unknown in both directions**, with CMJ's interval at [−0.280, +0.404]. Drop-jump RSI has a *cross-sectional* R² = 0.30 in pros (F-006) and **no intervention evidence whatsoever.** So "measure the RSI" risks installing a readout that will say "it worked" when nothing happened to the pitcher's actual job.
**COACH — DEFENDED, and the defence is narrow and should be read as narrow.** The claim is **not** "RSI going up means velocity will go up." The claim is **"RSI is the manipulation check, not the outcome."** Its job is to tell you whether the block changed the quality it was designed to change — which is a question about the *intervention*, not about the *pitcher's performance*. If RSI does not move, the block did nothing and should be abandoned regardless of what the radar says. If RSI moves and velocity does not, **that is a finding this literature has never produced and is worth more than either number alone** — and it is the measurement that would finally price F-006's marker.
**NOT RESOLVED. → Dispute #79 and Dispute #81.** The biomechanist maintains that a surrogate with no intervention evidence will in practice be read as an outcome by every coach who sees it, whatever the stated intent.

## 12. WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK

**Install an 8-week upper-extremity plyometric block, 2×/week, and measure the jump — not the fastball.**

1. **Block:** Ballistic Six-pattern upper-extremity plyometrics, **2×/week, 8 weeks minimum** (4 weeks is below the duration moderator and below the tissue timeline). Off-season or early pre-season.
2. **Log the throws.** Every med-ball and plyo-ball rep goes on the same workload sheet as bullpens and games. Carter's own title says "high volume" and this corpus cannot read the number — so assume it is more than it looks.
3. **Measure:** drop-jump RSI on a contact mat, pre and post, **3 trials per athlete per session on at least 2 separate days at each end** (the second day is what makes the comparison readable).
4. **Read it:** an individual needs **>14–22% RSI change** (CV-dependent) before you call it real. A 12-pitcher staff mean needs **~4.5–7%**.
5. **Do NOT read velocity as the test.** A 6/6 split detects 2.4 mph at best and would miss the entire reported effect.
6. **Free by-product worth more than the block:** log each athlete's day-to-day RSI across 4 weeks and you will have produced the **drop-jump RSI within-athlete CV for a pitcher**, which does not exist anywhere in print.
