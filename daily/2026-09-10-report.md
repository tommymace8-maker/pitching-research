## §3 — THE RESEARCH

### §3a — What framing actually operates on: the shadow band. **PURE GEOMETRY. No source.**

A pitch is a strike if any part of the ball overlaps the zone, so the *ball centre* may sit up to
half a plate plus half a ball from the middle:

```
half-width (ball centre) = 17.0/2 + 2.9/2 = 9.95 in   ->  effective width 19.90 in
```

Statcast's Shadow Zone is defined as one ball width inside the edge to one ball width outside —
a band **5.8 in** wide in ball-centre coordinates, straddling the boundary. Areas, across the
range of rulebook zone heights:

| Zone height | Effective strike area | Shadow annulus | Shadow ÷ zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17.0 in | 396 in² | 462 in² | **1.17** |
| 19.5 in | 446 in² | 491 in² | **1.10** |
| 22.0 in | 496 in² | 520 in² | **1.05** |

> **F-305. THE SHADOW ANNULUS IS LARGER IN AREA THAN THE STRIKE ZONE IT BORDERS — by 5–17%,
> across every plausible zone height.** This is not a small correction band. It is a region bigger
> than the target, in which the call is a decision rather than a fact, and in which someone other
> than the pitcher has a vote.

*Caveat, stated because it matters: area is not probability. Pitch density is not uniform, so the
share of actual pitches in the shadow is not 50%. That empirical share is bracketed at 12–20% of
all pitches throughout §3d and the conclusions are shown to survive the whole bracket.*

### §3b — Framing in inches: the conversion rate. **DERIVATION + one shape assumption.**

Called-strike probability runs from near-1 at the inner edge of the shadow to near-0 at the outer
edge, across the 5.8 in band. Taking the transition as roughly linear across that band:

| Assumed transition | Gradient |
|---|---|
| 0.10 → 0.90 across 5.8 in | **13.8 pp per inch** |
| 0.15 → 0.85 across 5.8 in | **12.1 pp per inch** |
| 0.05 → 0.95 across 5.8 in | **15.5 pp per inch** |

> **F-306. THE CONVERSION RATE IS ~12–16 PERCENTAGE POINTS OF SHADOW-STRIKE RATE PER INCH OF
> LOCATION. Divide any framing gap in pp by ~14 to read it in inches.**

**This form is deliberate.** It does not depend on §2c's unverified 18.7 pp. Whatever the true
best-to-worst framing gap turns out to be, the conversion is fixed by geometry and one shape
assumption. Applying it to the unverified figure *illustratively only*: 18.7 pp ÷ 14 ≈ **1.2–1.6
inches**.

**And that is the finding that reframes the topic.** F-183: the best command in the major leagues
misses the target by **~7 inches**; the average major leaguer by **~11–13**. So the entire
best-to-worst spread in major-league catcher framing is worth **roughly one-fifth of a single
average pitcher's miss**.

**Framing is a large number in RUNS because it is applied to thousands of pitches. It is a tiny
number in INCHES.** Both halves matter, and they point opposite ways:
- *For the catcher*, an inch applied 1,600 times a year is a real job.
- *For the pitcher*, the entire framing industry is fighting over a fifth of his own error bar.
  **There is no version of this where a pitcher's development plan should contain a framing term.**

### §3c — A consistency check on §2c's numbers that costs nothing. **DERIVATION.**

Statcast's stated methodology converts at **0.125 runs per strike** (this is a documented
conversion, not an estimate). Take the summary-level 2025 endpoints — Bailey **+25 runs**, Quero
**−13 runs** — and ask what denominator makes them consistent with an 18.7 pp gap:

```
+25 runs / 0.125 = +200 strikes ;  -13 / 0.125 = -104 strikes  ->  gap 304 strikes
304 strikes / 0.187 = 1,626 shadow-zone chances per full-time catcher-season
```

| If chances/season = | implied gap |
|---|---|
| 1,200 | 25.3 pp |
| **1,600** | **19.0 pp** |
| 2,000 | 15.2 pp |
| 3,000 | 10.1 pp |

**Three independently-reported summary-level numbers are mutually consistent only near ~1,600
chances per catcher-season.** That is a *coherence* result, not a measurement — three fabrications
would not usually cohere, so this raises the odds the numbers are real, and it does not make them
verified. **It also supplies the only anchor available for §3d's denominator, and it is used as a

### §3d — **THE MAIN RESULT: called-strike rate cannot measure a college pitcher's command.**
**PURE POWER ARITHMETIC. No source.**

Shadow pitches are, by construction, near coin flips (p ≈ 0.5) — that is what makes them the
shadow. Two-proportion test, 80% power, α = .05 two-sided:

| Detect a gap of | Shadow takes **per group** | Total |
|---|---|---|
| 2 pp | 9,811 | 19,622 |
| 3 pp | 4,360 | 8,721 |
| **5 pp** | **1,570** | **3,140** |
| 7 pp | 801 | 1,602 |
| 10 pp | 392 | 785 |

Now the supply side. A college starter throws roughly 1,200–1,800 pitches in a season. At a
shadow-take share of 12–20%:

| | 12% | 15% | 20% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,200 pitches | 144 | 180 | 240 |
| 1,500 pitches | 180 | **225** | 300 |
| 1,800 pitches | 216 | 270 | 360 |

> **F-307. A COLLEGE STARTER GENERATES ROUGHLY 150–360 FRAMABLE TAKEN PITCHES IN A FULL SEASON.
> Detecting a 5-percentage-point improvement in his called-strike rate requires ~3,140 of them —
> about FOURTEEN SEASONS. Detecting 7 pp — a gap larger than the best framer in baseball is worth
> — requires about SEVEN.**

**The conclusion is not sensitive to anything.** Take the most generous cell in the table (360/
season) and the most forgiving effect (7 pp): still 4.5 seasons. Take the shadow share to 25% and
the pitch count to 2,000: still over three seasons for an effect nobody will ever produce.

**So: any called-strike-based command number computed on your own staff — a spreadsheet column, a
TrackMan report, an in-house "strike-stealing" metric — is noise wearing a decimal point.** Not
approximately. Not "needs a bigger sample." **The sample does not exist inside a college career.**

### §3e — And the noise has a bias inside it: the catcher-assignment artifact. **DERIVATION.**

Worse, and this is the part that should change behaviour. A pitcher's measured called-strike rate
is jointly produced by his location, the catcher's receiving, the umpire, the batter and the count.
BP's CSAA handles this with a mixed model controlling all five (F-182) — **and that model is
identifiable in MLB because catcher assignment rotates**: many catchers catch many pitchers.

**A college staff has two catchers.** Often one catches 70–90% of the innings, and personal-catcher
pairings are common. The measured difference between two pitchers arising **from the catcher
alone** is:

```
artifact = (s_i - s_j) x delta_catcher
```

where *s* is each pitcher's share caught by catcher A and Δ is the framing gap between the two
catchers.

| Δ between your catchers | share diff 0.2 | 0.5 | 0.8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 pp | 0.60 pp | 1.50 pp | 2.40 pp |
| **5 pp** | 1.00 pp | **2.50 pp** | 4.00 pp |
| 8 pp | 1.60 pp | 4.00 pp | **6.40 pp** |

**Now the comparison that matters.** F-182 records BP's 2016 CSAA leaders: Zach Davies **+3.5%**,
Josh Tomlin +2.8%, Kyle Hendricks +2.5%, Zack Greinke +2.1%. **That is the top of Major League
Baseball.** A 2.5 pp artifact on a college staff is **seventy percent of the best command season in
the major leagues, manufactured entirely by who was squatting.**

> **F-308. ON A TWO-CATCHER COLLEGE STAFF, THE CATCHER-ASSIGNMENT ARTIFACT IN A CALLED-STRIKE
> COMMAND METRIC IS THE SAME ORDER OF MAGNITUDE AS THE ENTIRE REAL SIGNAL — AND IT IS BIAS, NOT
> NOISE. IT DOES NOT SHRINK WITH MORE INNINGS. MORE DATA MAKES THE WRONG RANKING MORE CONFIDENT.**

This is the second member of a family the corpus opened with **F-287** (the lineup-slot composition
bias in times-through-order splits). Both are systematic, both are larger than the effect they
contaminate, and both defeat the single instinct every coach has about bad numbers — *wait for a
bigger sample*. **Here, waiting is the error.**

The run cost is trivial and that is the point: 0.3–1.5 runs a season. **The damage is not runs. It
is that the artifact corrupts the RANKING you allocate development time against.** You do not lose
a run; you spend a winter on the wrong pitcher.

**And the standard fix is unavailable.** Co-estimating catcher and pitcher effects requires
variation in the pairing. With two catchers and near-constant assignment shares, the pitcher and
catcher terms are near-collinear — the model does not fail loudly, it returns wide, unstable
pitcher estimates that look like numbers. **Deliberately randomising catcher assignment across a
staff would identify it, and no program will trade a season of battery chemistry for a cleaner
spreadsheet column. Correctly so.**

### §3f — The challenge-success selection effect. **PURE BASE-RATE REASONING. No source.**

§2b's empirical pattern needs no data to explain. Let *T* be the borderline pitches that are truly
strikes by the ABS zone. A good framer converts a high fraction of *T* into called strikes; a bad
framer converts a low fraction. The **residual pool** — true strikes called balls, i.e. the only
challengeable-and-winnable calls — is therefore **small for the good framer and large for the bad
one**. Worse, the good framer's residual is adversely selected: what he failed to convert is what
the umpire was most confident about, so even his residual overturns at a lower rate.

Both terms push the same way.

> **F-309. CHALLENGE SUCCESS RATE IS MECHANICALLY ANTI-CORRELATED WITH FRAMING SKILL. A CATCHER
> WHO LEADS THE LEAGUE IN CHALLENGE SUCCESS IS DISPLAYING A LARGER SUPPLY OF UMPIRE MISTAKES
> AGAINST HIM, NOT BETTER JUDGEMENT. DO NOT EVALUATE, RECRUIT OR PLAY A CATCHER ON IT.**

**This generalises past catchers and is the reason it earns a place in a pitching corpus.**
*Any* success-rate metric computed over a pool whose composition the actor influences is
contaminated. The pitcher version is immediate: a pitcher with poor command generates more
genuinely-missed calls, so **his** challenge success rate is inflated too. §2d's summary-level
figure that pitchers succeed on only **41%** of challenges — worst of the three parties, against
catchers ~56–59% — is a lead, not a number, but it is at least consistent with the pitcher having
the worst *view* of the three. The right conclusion does not depend on the figure: **the pitcher is
the party furthest from the pitch, with the strongest motivated reasoning about it, and the least
information. He should almost never be the one who spends the challenge.** (Links to F-283.)

### §3g — An arithmetic self-check on F-010, the corpus's own most population-matched finding.
**DERIVATION on two already-verified numbers.**

F-010 registers Fernandez, Bullock, Zappa, Oliver & Nicholson (2026), *Sports Biomechanics*,
PMID 42253247 — 322 NCAA D1 pitchers, ACC (n=122) and SEC (n=200), in-game markerless capture,
2022–2024, Super Learner ensemble. **R² = 0.29, RMSE = 2.70 mph, height 81.2% of variable
importance, mass 2.47%.** Today's sweep re-surfaced it and corroborated those figures. The
citation was verified into the corpus previously; today added the ensemble composition (elastic
net, random forest, SVM, gradient boosting, MARS; 10-fold CV with internal-external validation by
conference).

**Nobody has run the obvious check.** RMSE and R² are not independent:

```
SD = RMSE / sqrt(1 - R^2) = 2.70 / sqrt(0.71) = 3.20 mph
```

So the sample's fastball velocity SD is **3.20 mph** — and a model that simply predicts the mean
for everyone has RMSE **3.20 mph**.

> **F-311. THE ENTIRE SUPER LEARNER ENSEMBLE — FIVE ALGORITHMS, FULL IN-GAME BIOMECHANICS, 322
> ACC AND SEC PITCHERS — IMPROVES ON GUESSING THE TEAM AVERAGE BY 0.50 MPH (95% CI ≈ 0.21–0.76
> MPH). AND 81.2% OF ITS VARIABLE IMPORTANCE IS A TAPE MEASURE.**

Two consequences.

**(i) The reported RMSE and R² are mutually consistent with a realistic population**, which is a
point in the paper's favour — SD ≈ 3.2 mph is exactly what a D1 fastball distribution should look
like. This is a genuine internal-validity check that passed.

**(ii) The biomechanical variables — the entire point of the study — are carrying almost nothing.**
Importance shares are not variance shares, so this is directional rather than exact; but however
you apportion R² = 0.29 with height at 81.2% and mass at 2.47%, **all kinematics combined are left
with a low-single-digit share of velocity variance.** The next-ranked features were maximum trunk
rotation velocity, **stride length as a percentage of height**, trunk flexion and lateral trunk
lean at release, and shoulder external rotation at MER.

**Note what is sitting in that list. Stride length — normalised to height — appears again, in the
largest in-band sample that exists, carrying a trivial share of importance, in a cross-sectional
model.** F-043/F-044/F-045 record what happened the last time this program treated stride length as
a lever, and F-049 (open pelvis at foot contact) is flagged in INDEX as sitting in the same
position right now. **This is a fourth cross-sectional appearance and it is not evidence for a
lever. Nobody manipulated any of these variables. The study did not attempt to.** The authors'
own stated conclusion — arguing *against* universal mechanics models — is the correct reading.

**The honest athlete-facing version:** *"The biggest study ever run on pitchers exactly like you
could predict your velocity half a mile an hour better than guessing, and most of what it knew, it
knew from your height."*

### §3h — Where this leaves the population constraint

Restriction of range cuts in the usual direction here and one unusual one. The 3.20 mph SD is a
**D1** SD, so it is already restricted relative to published mixed-level samples (Luera 2020,
r = .17–.29 in n=149 pros). **But §3d's detection arithmetic runs the other way and gets WORSE at
higher levels of selection**, because elite command populations are more tightly clustered: the
gaps you are trying to detect shrink while the shadow-take supply does not grow. **The 85+
population is the hardest population in which to measure command by called strikes, not the
easiest.**

## §4 — TRANSLATE: the coach's four questions

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "My pitching coordinator has a called-strike-rate column on the staff
report. Is it telling me anything?"**

**No, and the reason is not sample size — it is two separate failures stacked.** (1) The variance
alone requires ~3,140 shadow takes to see 5 pp and your starter supplies ~225 a year (F-307). (2)
Underneath that noise sits a systematic catcher artifact of 1–6 pp that does **not** average out,
because your staff's catcher assignment is not random (F-308). Reading it as a ranking is worse
than reading nothing, because nothing does not have a decimal point.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *nothing.* This one is for the staff, not the athlete. **What
> I tell the coordinator is:** *"Delete the column, or rename it 'catcher + umpire + noise.'"*
> **The drill is:** replace it with a declared-target miss-distance log (§5). **On video the failure
> looks like:** a coach saying "he's stealing more strikes this year" about a 4-point move in a
> 225-pitch denominator. **Here's how we know it's working:** you stop making winter development
> decisions that reverse themselves when the catcher graduates.

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "Then what CAN I measure inside one season?"**

**The input, in inches, against a declared target.** Same power arithmetic, different variable —
and it is not close:

| Route | Pitches needed |
|---|---|
| Miss distance, 2 in gain, SD 5 in | **~196** |
| Miss distance, 1.5 in gain, SD 5 in | ~349 |
| Miss distance, 1 in gain, SD 5 in | ~785 |

This is the corpus's input-vs-outcome asymmetry (F-257, F-289, F-295) landing on command, and it

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We're not counting strikes. We're measuring how far you miss

> against. **Here's how we know it's working:** ~200 tracked pitches, pre versus post, mean miss in

**COACH → ANATOMIST: "Is there any tissue-level or physiological reason a pitcher's command
should respond to training at all, or am I about to measure something that doesn't move?"**

**Honest answer: the mechanism is discouraging and the corpus already says so.** F-171 is
trigonometry: ~30 cm of plate location per 1° of release angle, and within-pitcher release-angle SD
is ±1.09–1.1°, which multiplies out to ~13 inches of scatter from release angle alone —
essentially the entire observed miss. **The variable that dominates command is an angular quantity
at release with no feedback channel a pitcher can use inside 150 ms.** F-197 records that **no
bullpen-to-game command transfer study exists in baseball** — the largest hole in the applied
command literature, and it is load-bearing for every recommendation in this file.

**So the anatomist's position is: measure the input because it is the only thing you can measure,
and hold your expectation for the trainability of the underlying quantity at low.** That is not an
argument against §5. It is an argument for §5 being cheap, which it is.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The best command in the big leagues misses by about seven
> inches. Average misses by a foot. If you think you're hitting the glove, you're not, and neither
> is anybody else"* (F-183's athlete-facing anchor). **Why that line:** it converts a demoralising
> measurement into a calibration, before you start showing him his own numbers.

**COACH → BOTH: "ABS hits the SEC tournament and goes NCAA-wide in 2027. What actually changes for
my pitcher?"**

**Three things, in descending order of confidence.**

1. **The shadow band stops being negotiable.** Under a challenged call there is no framing term —
   the call is geometry. **This does not change what you train; it removes the last reason anyone
   had to argue that a pitcher's called-strike rate reflects the pitcher.** The measurement problem
   in §3d/§3e is being solved for you, slowly, by a rules change. **DERIVATION-SAFE.**
2. **Do not let your pitcher spend the challenge.** He is the furthest party from the pitch with
   the strongest motivated reasoning about it (§3f). The catcher has the best view and, per the
   summary-level figures, the best success rate. **The mechanism is safe; the percentages are
   not.** ⚠️ **Snippet-level. Do not repeat 41% / 56% / 59% to the team.**
3. **Any framing-dependent investment now has a depreciation schedule.** Recruiting a catcher for

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"When the zone goes automatic, the only thing left is where
> the ball actually goes. That's what we've been measuring anyway."* **Here's how we know it's
> working:** it is not a training intervention, it is a planning fact — the check is that you did
> not spend a recruiting slot or a winter on a skill with an expiry date.

## §5 — WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK: THE DECLARED-TARGET MISS LOG

**Cost: a notebook and a camera you already own. No purchase. Runs alongside normal bullpens.**

F-182 is explicit that **declared-target-before-the-pitch is the only approach that SOLVES rather
than infers the intent problem** — glove tagging is deprecated precisely because *the glove moves
and framing motion contaminates the frame*. That is the same contamination §3e quantifies, arriving
from the other direction.

**The protocol.**
1. Before each pitch in a charted bullpen, the pitcher **declares the target out loud** — a
   9-zone call plus in/out of zone. Not the glove. The glove may be there; it is not the record.
2. Catcher sets up and **holds** until release. A drifting or late glove voids the pitch for
   charting purposes (it does not void the pitch).
3. Log actual location from any tracking unit, or from a fixed camera at 240 fps behind the plate
   with a taped reference (F-204: 240 fps is the practical standard for anything at or distal to
   the shoulder; for plate location a fixed camera and a scale suffice).
4. Record **signed miss in inches, horizontal and vertical**, then the scalar radial miss.
5. **Never record whether it was called a strike.** That is the corrupted variable.

**The measurable check, with the sample size to detect it:**
- **~200 charted pitches pre and post** detects a **2-inch** improvement in mean radial miss at 80%
  power (miss SD ≈ 5 in). At ~50 charted pitches a week that is **four weeks each side**.
- Want 1 inch? **~785 pitches.** Budget for it or do not claim it.
- **Anchor expectations to F-183 before you show him a number:** elite MLB ≈ 7 in, average MLB
  ≈ 11–13 in. A college arm logging 12–14 in is normal, not broken.
- **Report the miss SD to the program** — nobody has published a college one (F-314), and yours
  would immediately sharpen this table for everyone, including you.

**What would make me wrong:** F-197. There is no evidence anywhere that bullpen command transfers
to games. **This protocol measures a bullpen quantity and I am asserting it is worth measuring
because it is measurable, not because transfer is established.** That is a real limitation and the
coach conceded it below.

## §6 — CROSS-EXAMINATION

### §6.1 — BIOMECHANIST → COACH: "Your §5 measures the wrong bullpen." **CONCEDED IN PART.**

*The challenge:* §5 has the catcher **hold** a static target until release. **That is not what
happens in a game** — game targets are set late, moved, and the pitcher throws to a moving
reference under a different arousal state. So §5 buys internal validity by destroying ecological
validity, and F-197 says nobody has ever shown the two are connected. **A 2-inch improvement in the
held-target bullpen may be a 0-inch improvement in May.**

*The coach's answer — CONCEDED, and the protocol is NOT changed.* The objection is correct and it
is the same one F-182 already records against declared-target methods ("the cost is ecological
validity: a bullpen is not a game"). **But the alternative is not a better measurement, it is no
measurement**, because the game-realistic version is the called-strike route, which needs 21,000
pitches (F-310). **§5 stands as the best available measurement of an input, explicitly not as
evidence of game transfer, and the report must not be read as claiming otherwise.**

*What would settle it:* the study F-197 says does not exist — held-target bullpen miss distance
against in-game miss distance from an intent model, same athletes, same season. **It needs no new
instrumentation for a program that owns TrackMan and runs §5.** This is now the second cycle
running in which the cycle's only executable recommendation rests on F-197 (cf. F-282, 2026-09-07).
**Escalated in INDEX.**

### §6.2 — ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST: "Your 107× is two different tests wearing one result."
**DEFENDED, with the ratio withdrawn as a quotable.**

*The challenge:* F-310 compares a **continuous-outcome t-test on inches** against a **binomial test
on a percentage**, and the 107× is dominated by the arbitrary choice of what counts as an
"equivalent" effect — 2 inches versus 5 pp. Choose 1 inch and 10 pp and the ratio collapses. **This
is exactly the objection that produced Dispute #22 against F-295 last cycle, and it was upheld
there.**

*The biomechanist's answer — DEFENDED, narrowly.* Conceded that **the ratio's exact value is an
artefact of the pairing and must not be quoted**, precisely as Dispute #22 ruled for F-295. But
F-306 supplies something F-295 did not have: **a conversion rate between the two units.** At ~14
pp per inch, 5 pp **is** ~0.36 inches, so the comparison can be made in one unit. Detecting a
0.36-inch mean shift at SD 5 in needs ~6,000 pitches by the input route — still **3.5× fewer** than
the 21,000 the outcome route needs, and that residual gap is real, arising because a binomial at
p = 0.5 discards nearly all the information in a continuous location.

> **Settled position: SAY "the input route needs several-fold fewer pitches, and the outcome route
> needs more than a career." DO NOT SAY 107×.** The 107× is retained in F-310 as a worked example
> **with this correction attached**, and Dispute #22 is extended to cover it.

*What would settle it:* one program's own charted bullpen data, giving the real miss SD and the
real shadow-take share. Both are in-house quantities.

### §6.3 — COACH → ANATOMIST: "F-309 proves too much." **DEFENDED.**

*The challenge:* if success rate over a self-influenced pool is always contaminated, that
indicts half the corpus's outcome metrics, including things the coach uses daily.

*The anatomist's answer — DEFENDED.* It does indict them, and that is the finding rather than a
bug in it. **The specific condition is narrow: the actor influences the COMPOSITION of the pool
over which his success rate is computed.** Framing does that to the challenge pool; command does it
to a pitcher's challenge pool. It does **not** apply to a rate computed over a pool the actor
cannot shape — a hitter's success against a pitch he did not select, say. **The rule is: before
reading a success rate, ask who chose the denominator.**

### §6.4 — BIOMECHANIST → COACH: "You are treating §2d as a bigger catch than it is."
**PARTIALLY CONCEDED.**

*The challenge:* the two-query disagreement in §2d has an innocent explanation — **two different
article dates**. Kelly's record moving 21-4 → 24-28 is exactly what a season does. Calling this a
summariser failure risks the F-303 error in reverse: **a defensive rule firing on a true signal.**

*The coach's answer — CONCEDED for Kelly, DEFENDED for the aggregates.* The individual record is
withdrawn as evidence; a player's record is *supposed* to move. **What is not withdrawn is that
league-wide challenge success rates for catchers and batters moved 3 points in OPPOSITE directions
across two queries in one cycle.** Those are aggregates over thousands of challenges and they
cannot drift that way in weeks. **F-312 is registered on the aggregates only, and the Kelly line is
explicitly demoted to illustration.**

*This is F-303's lesson being applied one cycle later, in the direction F-303 asked for.* The
retraction cycle established that over-skepticism suppresses true findings invisibly. **Here the
skeptical claim was narrowed on the spot rather than published at full width.**
