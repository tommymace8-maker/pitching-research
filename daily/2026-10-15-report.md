## 1. THE RESEARCH

### 1.1 The number everybody quotes, and what it actually says

**Spiering BA, Mujika I, Sharp MA, Foulis SA (2021). "Maintaining Physical Performance: The Minimal Dose of Exercise Needed to Preserve Endurance and Strength Over Time." *J Strength Cond Res* 35(5):1449–1458. DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964. — ✅ READ IN FULL (10 pp., PDF, text extracted).**

The circulating version, in every in-season blog and every strength-coach podcast: *"You can hold your strength on one session a week and one set per exercise for 32 weeks, as long as you keep the load heavy."* That is a fair paraphrase of the abstract. Four things in the body of the paper destroy its application to a pitcher.

**(a) The samples were untrained general-population adults, and the authors say so in a stand-alone warning.** Verbatim: *"Importantly, except where specifically indicated, the studies referenced in this section used relatively untrained participants of various ages from the general population. Therefore, caution is warranted when attempting to extrapolate these findings to special populations (i.e., athletes and military personnel)."* And in the abstract: *"Insufficient data exists to make specific recommendations for athletes or military personnel."* And in the conclusion: *"although insufficient studies using trained/athletic populations met the inclusion criteria of this paper..."* Three separate disclaimers. **SAMPLE MISMATCH — directional only.** → F-708

**(b) Power and rate of force development are explicitly outside the review's scope.** Verbatim caveat 2: *"This review paper will not focus on maintaining other adaptations to training (e.g., muscle power, agility, flexibility), as relatively few studies have investigated the minimal dose of exercise necessary to maintain these variables."* The number has been imported into a ballistic sport by people who stopped reading at the abstract. Pitching velocity is an RFD problem, not a 1RM problem, and **the review says nothing about RFD.** → F-709

**(c) The "1 set" half of the recommendation was never isolated.** Verbatim: *"To our knowledge, no research has isolated the independent effects of reduced exercise volume (number of sets) on the maintenance of strength and muscle size."* The two studies behind it cut frequency **and** volume simultaneously — Bickel et al. (2011, *MSSE* 43:1177–1187: 3 sessions→1, 3 sets→1, 32 weeks, intensity held at 8–12RM) and Tavares et al. (2–3 sessions→2, 3–4 sets→2, 8 weeks). The 32-week figure traces to **one** study. Notably Bickel's 1RM *further improved* on the reduced dose, and muscle size held in the 20–35 group but **not** the 60–75 group. This is Check #3: the sentence says "1 set is enough" and the data say "1 set bundled with a frequency cut was enough." → F-710

**(d) The headline conclusion — "intensity is the key variable" — rests, on the strength side, on one 1967 isometric study.** Verbatim: *"To our knowledge, only one study has investigated the effects of reduced intensity on the maintenance of muscle strength"* → Morehouse CA (1967), *Res Q* 38:449–456, isometric training at 100% MVC for 9 weeks then 8 weeks reduced. The endurance side of the same conclusion has Hickson's well-designed series behind it; the strength side has a single Lyndon-Johnson-era isometric report. The conclusion may well be right. Its evidentiary base is one paper. → F-711

**(e) And which strength you measure changes the answer fourfold.** Same review: maintaining **1RM** needs 1 session/week; maintaining **maximal isometric strength (MIS)** holds on *"as little as 1 session of training every 4 weeks... for up to 12 weeks."* The authors resolve the discrepancy by choosing 1RM on the grounds that it tracked sprint speed in the one athlete study. **"Strength was maintained" is not a fact about the athlete. It is a fact about the test.** → F-713

### 1.2 The only in-season maintenance trial ever run on athletes

**Rønnestad BR, Nymark BS, Raastad T (2011). "Effects of In-Season Strength Maintenance Training Frequency in Professional Soccer Players." *J Strength Cond Res* 25(10):2653–2660.** ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY** — no reachable copy; figures from search snippets of the abstract plus Spiering's in-text description, which was read at source.

Design: 10-week preseason at 2 sessions/week, then in-season either **1 session/week (n = 7)** or **1 session every second week (n = 7)**, 12 weeks.

| Outcome | 1×/week | 1× per 2 weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Half-squat 1RM | maintained | **significantly reduced** |
| 40 m sprint | maintained | **significantly slower** |
| Jump height | no significant change | no significant change |

This single study, **n = 7 per arm**, is the entire athlete-specific evidence base for the industry's in-season prescription. Spiering's own words: *"Because an exercise frequency of 1 session per week was sufficient to maintain functional performance (in this case, sprint speed), whereas exercise frequency of less than 1 session per week was insufficient... we recommend a minimum dose of 1 session per week of training to maintain strength."* → F-712

Three things follow. **One:** by Dispute #63's rule, a positive at n = 7 is a result reported at its own detection floor, so the *direction* is informative and the *magnitude* is not. **Two:** the ballistic outcome — jump height — was flat in **both** arms, including the arm that demonstrably lost squat strength and sprint speed. A jump null at n = 7 is evidence of nothing in either direction (F-004 / F-438→F-449), so the one measurement in the one athlete trial that speaks to explosive output is uninterpretable. **Three:** zero throwing outcomes. 40 m sprint and half-squat 1RM are proximal tests this corpus cannot convert to mph in an 85+ arm — the **fourth confirmed instance** of the candidate sixth structural sweep logged 2026-10-12/13. → F-714

### 1.3 What complete cessation costs, and the "power goes first" claim

**Bosquet L, Berryman N, Dupuy O, et al. (2013). "Effect of training cessation on muscular performance: A meta-analysis." *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 23:e140–149. PMID 23347054.** ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY.** 103 of 284 screened studies. Submaximal strength **SMD −0.62 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.45)**; maximal force **SMD −0.46 (−0.54 to −0.37)**. Dose–response with cessation duration. Effects larger in >65s and in the inactive than in recreational athletes. **The maximal-power SMD was truncated in every snippet retrieved and is NOT in this corpus.** That is the one number the topic needs and it was not obtained. Note the design: this is **cessation**, not reduced training — it bounds what an off week costs, not what a maintenance dose buys.

**"Power is lost before strength."** Mechanistically supported, but not by the literature it is usually attributed to. **Ruggiero L, Gruber M (2024/2025), "Neuromuscular mechanisms for the fast decline in rate of force development with muscle disuse," *J Physiol*** (snippet-only) finds that **unloading** produces a greater decline in explosive than in maximal strength, with the gap narrowing as unloading lengthens — mechanisms given as higher recruitment thresholds, lower firing rates, slower twitch kinetics, impaired excitation–contraction coupling, and **decreased tendon stiffness** (which hooks directly to F-630s/`library/tendon-stiffness.md`). But the dose there is **bed rest and limb immobilisation**, not a pitcher who lifts once a week and throws every day. Grade: **MECHANISM**, not ESTABLISHED, for a training context. The specific **"7–10 days"** figure circulating in VBT material is **UNSOURCED** — not found in any retrievable primary text. The most pitching-relevant observation is secondary and old: Mujika & Padilla report college swimmers holding **muscular strength** across 4 weeks of cessation while **swim power declined**. The gym number held and the sport output did not. → F-719

### 1.4 The in-season literature that does have a throwing outcome — and why it is unusable

There is exactly one body of in-season resistance-training work with a **ball-throwing velocity** endpoint, and it is handball, largely from one research group (Hermassi and colleagues). **Hermassi et al. (2019), "In-Season Weightlifting Training Exercise in Healthy Male Handball Players," *IJERPH* 16(22):4520** ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY:** n = 20 randomised to EG/CG (split not retrieved), 8 weeks, 3–4 sets of explosive weightlifting at 75–90% 1RM. Reported: **upper-limb muscle volume +~20–22% (d = 7.00, η²p = 0.920)**; medicine-ball throw between-group difference **>40% (d = 6.44)**.

A 22% gain in limb muscle volume in 8 weeks is outside physiological possibility; the hypertrophy literature's 8-week ceiling is a small single-digit percentage of CSA. A *d* of 7.00 means seven pooled standard deviations. **This is the same signature F-040 used to discard the g = 3.445 periodization meta-analysis and F-194 used to downgrade Differential Learning: a single lab, small samples, effect sizes that cannot be real.** DO NOT CONVERT ANY OF IT TO MPH. → F-717

### 1.5 The gap, stated plainly

**No in-season maintenance-dose trial has ever been run in baseball, at any level.** Searched in both directions. And across the entire reduced-training literature — Spiering's tables, Bosquet's 103 studies, the taper reviews — **every outcome is 1RM, maximal isometric force, muscle size, sprint time, jump height or VO2max. Not one throwing outcome.** The industry prescription Tommy will hear this spring is a general-population 1RM-maintenance figure, re-exported into a ballistic sport by analogy, with one n = 7 soccer study in between. → F-714

## 3. THE THREE AGENTS

### 3.1 ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY — does the dose make sense given the tissue?

**The mechanism splits the question in two, and the literature only answers one half.**

*What 1RM maintenance needs.* Maximal strength is carried by contractile protein and by the slow, robust end of neural drive. Both are cheap to hold: a single weekly exposure at high relative load re-supplies the mechanotransductive signal, and myofibrillar protein has a long half-life. Bickel's result — 1RM *improving* on one weekly set for 32 weeks — is not surprising once you see that the subjects were untrained, so they were still on the steep part of their own dose–response.

*What RFD needs, and why it is a different question.* Ball velocity depends on the first 100–150 ms of force production, which is governed by motor-unit **discharge rate** and **doublet firing**, by synchronisation, and by series-elastic stiffness in the tendon — none of which is proxied by a 1RM. Ruggiero & Gruber's unloading mechanisms are precisely these: firing-rate reduction, higher recruitment thresholds, slower twitch kinetics, falling tendon stiffness. **High-frequency discharge is the most rapidly reversible neural property there is.** The physiological prediction is therefore specific and testable: *a once-weekly heavy session will hold the 1RM and will not hold the RFD, because RFD is maintained by frequent exposure to high-velocity intent, not by occasional exposure to high load.*

**But** — and this is why the anatomy agent does not get to declare victory — a pitcher is not an unloaded limb. He throws a baseball at maximal intent several times a week. **Throwing is itself a high-RFD stimulus, and it never stops in-season.** The disuse models have no analogue for that. So the mechanism predicts a decline that the sport's own practice may already prevent, and nobody has measured which way it comes out. The honest physiological statement is: **the tissue argument says the gym's job in-season is to supply load, not speed, because the mound already supplies speed.** That is a prediction, and it is the opposite of what most in-season baseball programs do (they cut load and keep "velocity work").

### 3.2 BIOMECHANIST — attacking the data

1. **The population.** Spiering's samples are untrained adults aged 20–35 and 60–75. Rønnestad's are professional soccer players. Bosquet's moderators are "inactive vs recreational athletes." **No sample in this entire topic has a reported throwing velocity, because no sample throws.** Check #1 is not merely unrunnable here — it is categorically inapplicable, and that is a stronger objection than "the collegiate sample threw 78."
2. **The design.** Spiering 2021 is a **brief narrative review**, not a systematic review and not a meta-analysis. Its own Practical Applications section says the recommendations *"should be treated with the utmost caution because of the limited number of studies available, limitations in interpreting the existing studies, and the primary population studied (i.e., mostly the general population)."* The review was manipulated-variable throughout (frequency, volume and intensity were all genuinely reduced by investigators), so the **CAUSALITY grade is INTERVENTION** — which is rare and good — but the *population* transfer is pure analogy.
3. **The power.** Rønnestad: n = 7 vs 7. At that size the minimum detectable between-group effect at 80% power is roughly d ≈ 1.6. Both of the study's positive findings therefore sit at or above its own detection floor, and its one null (jump height) is uninformative. Dispute #63, fifth application.
4. **The internal inconsistency in Gdovin.** This is the sharpest data objection of the cycle and it needs no egress. **F-094 (Slowik 2019, n = 64 professional): within an individual pitcher, ball velocity predicts elbow varus torque at R² = 0.957, p < .001.** Gdovin reports, in the same 12 athletes, on the same pitches, over the same 8 weeks: a velocity change significant at **p < 0.001** and **no change whatsoever** in peak elbow varus torque, peak arm velocity or arm slot (all p > .05). If the circulating magnitude is right, that is a **−7.5%** velocity change with a near-deterministic correlate sitting still. **Both tests share the same n = 12, so underpowering cannot explain the asymmetry** — a study with enough power to find the velocity effect at p < 0.001 should have had comparable power on a variable with R² = 0.957 against it. And F-202 says the IMU sleeve metric is at its **most** valid exactly in this mode (relative, within-athlete, longitudinal), which makes the torque null harder to dismiss, not easier. The parsimonious reading is that **the velocity contrast is a context or measurement artifact** — pre and post collected under different intent instructions, bullpen structures, gun placements or (per F-704) feedback conditions — not a 6.3 mph detraining effect. The honest counter: 8 weeks of sleeve wear invites placement drift, which F-202 warns degrades longitudinal tracking; and a baseline of **84.3 mph** is below this program's floor, so the sample is off-population regardless. → F-716
5. **The effect sizes that cannot be real.** d = 7.00 and η²p = 0.920 for 8 weeks of in-season lifting (§1.4). Not a finding. A signature.

### 3.3 PITCHING COACH — can he do it, and is it worth a block?

Three of the four defensible conclusions here are **scheduling and paperwork**, not coaching. That is becoming this corpus's most common output shape and it is worth saying out loud: this is now the **sixth** topic whose entire actionable content is a scheduling or logging decision.

**What survives into practice:**
- **Hold the load, cut the sets.** This is the one instruction with converging support (Spiering's conclusion, Morehouse's single study, the taper literature's "reduce volume 30–70%, maintain or increase intensity"), and it is the opposite of what the in-season blogs say. It costs nothing to switch to and it buys back time and fatigue.
- **One heavy session a week is a floor, not a target.** Every-other-week demonstrably failed in the one athlete trial. Whether 1 is as good as 3 for a thrower is unknown.
- **A flat velocity trajectory is a decline.** F-082: league velocity *rises* +0.6 mph across a season, with the biggest step April→May. If a Friday starter sits at his February number in May, he has lost ground relative to the population. Most coaches score flat as a win.

**What does not survive:** any claim about how much mph the in-season program is worth. There is no such number, in any population, and the one magnitude circulating (−6.3 mph) is from a design the corpus already corrected and is internally inconsistent with its own kinetics.

## 4. THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS, ANSWERED AND TRANSLATED

### Q1 (to the biomechanist): "If I cut my guys to one lift a week in April, am I going to lose velocity?"

**Answer:** Nobody knows, and the honest version of that is specific. The best-supported finding in the topic (Spiering 2021, read at source) says a once-weekly heavy session holds **1RM** in untrained adults for up to 32 weeks and holds **squat 1RM and sprint speed** in n = 7 professional soccer players for 12 weeks. It says *nothing* about power or RFD — explicitly, by the authors' own scope statement — and nothing about throwing, because no study in the literature has a throwing outcome. The one circulating magnitude for the velocity cost of removing the weight room (−6.3 mph) comes from an uncontrolled pre-post whose own kinetic data contradict it (F-716).

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The lifting is going to get shorter in-season, not lighter. Same bar weight, fewer sets."*
> **The drill is:** rebuild each in-season session as 1–2 sets per main lift at the fall's relative load, instead of 3–4 sets at a reduced load.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing — this one does not show on video, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed. It shows in the log as the spring's mean %1RM drifting down while set counts stay up.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** we don't, on velocity, and no program this size can know (see Q4). We verify the **input**: mean %1RM per main lift per week, February through May, against the fall baseline. Flat or rising = the program is doing the thing the evidence supports. Falling = you are running reduced-intensity training, which has one 1967 isometric study behind it.

### Q2 (to anatomy–physiology): "Which comes off first if something's going to come off — the squat number or the fastball?"

**Answer:** Mechanistically, the fastball, and for a reason that matters: velocity is an RFD expression and RFD is carried by discharge rate, synchronisation and tendon stiffness — all fast to degrade and none of them visible in a 1RM. Ruggiero & Gruber's unloading work finds explosive strength declining faster than maximal strength, and Mujika & Padilla's swimmers held gym strength across 4 weeks of cessation while **swim power fell**. **But** the unloading models have no analogue for an athlete who throws at maximal intent three days a week, and that throwing is itself the RFD stimulus. So the prediction is conditional: *the squat number is the last thing you'll lose and the worst thing to monitor.* The "7–10 days" number in the VBT literature is unsourced — do not repeat it.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The bar is for force. The mound is for speed. Don't try to make the gym fast in-season — you've already got three fast days a week."*
> **The drill is:** keep one heavy compound session weekly at fall load; stop adding in-season "power work" (jumps, med-ball circuits, light explosive doubles) on top of a full throwing week — it is a volume cost paid for a stimulus the mound already supplies.
> **On video the failure looks like:** late-outing lower-half collapse — the lead leg yielding at foot strike — rather than anything in the arm.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** it is not testable with a jump. F-004 is a regraded **UNDERPOWERED NULL** and jump height did not move in either Rønnestad arm — the whole channel is dead for this purpose. The only honest check is the velocity trajectory in Q4.

### Q3 (to both): "A guy in my room says his arm feels better when he stops lifting in May. Is he wrong?"

**Answer:** Not necessarily, and this corpus cannot adjudicate it on velocity. What the evidence rules out is the *inference*: Spiering's review notes both bounds — **insufficient** in-season strength training costs strength and performance (ref 49, Rønnestad), and **too much** supplementary in-season strength training *"can overstress the athlete and ultimately impair strength (as well as other indicators of athletic performance)"* (ref 30, Kraemer et al. 2004, *JSCR* 18:121–128, Big Ten soccer, starters vs non-starters). There is a dose that is too high and one that is too low, and no study in any throwing population locates either. "My arm feels better" is a real signal about total load. It is not a reason to drop the one weekly heavy session; it is a reason to drop the accessory volume stacked around it.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"You're probably right that you're doing too much. The thing to cut is the forty minutes of extra stuff, not the squat."*
> **The drill is:** audit total in-season sets per week per athlete; cut accessory volume first, keep the heavy compound, keep the arm-care dose.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing on video — this one shows up as velocity variance between outings rising before the mean moves (F-127's hierarchy: command drift and breaking-ball finish degrade before velocity does).
> **Here's how we know it's working:** it is a within-athlete question, so it is one of the few the corpus can price. See Q4.

### Q4 (to the biomechanist): "Can I actually measure whether my spring program held his velocity?"

**Answer:** Yes on **one arm**, no on **the program**. The group question is behind the detection wall: a mean-velocity endpoint across a staff needs roughly **~106 pitchers** at the corpus's working figures (F-681, F-289) and Ole Miss does not have 106 pitchers. The individual question is affordable because the athlete is his own control, which is the same route F-689 and F-706 used to breach the wall on the last two cycles.

**Priced off F-289 at the corpus working within-outing SD of 1.0 mph, two-sample, α = .05, 80% power:**

| Contrast to detect | Fastballs **per bucket** |
|---|---|
| 2.0 mph | 4 |
| 1.5 mph | 7 |
| **1.0 mph** | **16** |
| 0.5 mph | 63 |

**The protocol.** Take **innings 1–2 fastballs only** (so the within-outing fade, F-384's −0.2 mph/inning, falls equally on both buckets), same radar unit, same mound where possible, and — per **F-704** — the **same feedback condition**, written down and never changed. Bucket A = February/March outings, Bucket B = May outings. A Friday starter supplies ~16 first-two-inning fastballs in roughly 1–2 outings, so a **1.0 mph** contrast costs about two starts per bucket; **0.5 mph** costs about four starts per bucket, which is a whole season's worth and is the realistic ceiling for one arm.

**And here is the part that is easy to get backwards: the null is not zero.** F-082 says MLB starter velocity rises **+0.6 mph** across a season (90.1 in Mar/Apr → 90.7 by Aug), with the largest single step April→May. So for a healthy arm on a working program, the *expected* May-minus-March contrast is roughly **+0.3 to +0.6 mph**. **A flat trajectory is not a success — it is a relative decline of about the size you set out to detect.** Score it against the rising baseline, not against zero.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We're tracking your first-two-inning fastballs all spring. You should be a little faster in May than in March. Same as everybody."*
> **The drill is:** no drill — a standing query against the TrackMan log, filtered to innings 1–2 fastballs, bucketed by month.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing. This is a log, not a tape, and that is the point.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** ≥16 fastballs per bucket for a 1.0 mph read, scored against a +0.3 to +0.6 mph expectation. **PREREQUISITE, AND IT IS FREE AND NOW THREE DAYS OVERDUE:** the 1.0 mph SD is a **corpus assumption**, never published for an 85+ arm, and it is now load-bearing in F-289, F-659, F-684, F-689, F-706 and this test. It is in your own radar log. Computing it is one afternoon and it re-prices six findings.

→ F-718

## 5. CROSS-EXAMINATION

### 5.1 Biomechanist → Anatomy–physiology
**The claim challenged:** *"RFD will decay on a once-weekly dose while the 1RM holds."*
**Why I doubt it:** every mechanism cited comes from **bed rest and limb immobilisation**. The effect sizes in disuse models are driven by a dose no in-season pitcher experiences — he throws at maximal intent several times a week. You have imported a disuse magnitude into a reduced-training context, which is the same move Spiering's own authors refused to make across a smaller gap (general population → athlete). Also: Spiering explicitly excluded power because *"relatively few studies have investigated"* it — you are predicting into an acknowledged void and calling it mechanism.
**What would settle it:** an in-season trial with **both** a 1RM and an RFD/IMTP-slope endpoint measured on the same athletes at the same time points, under two lifting frequencies. Nobody has run it in any population.
**Anatomy–physiology — PARTIALLY CONCEDED.** The disuse-to-reduced-training extrapolation is real and I withdraw the magnitude claim entirely; I keep the *direction* and I downgrade the grade from EMERGING to **MECHANISM**. I also accept the sharper form of the objection, which strengthens my practical conclusion rather than weakening it: *if throwing is the RFD stimulus, then the gym's in-season job is load, and the programs stacking in-season "power work" onto a full throwing week are paying volume for a stimulus they already have.* That is now a prediction on the record (F-709, F-719) and it is falsifiable.

### 5.2 Anatomy–physiology → Pitching coach
**The claim challenged:** *"Hold the load, cut the sets — it costs nothing."*
**Why I doubt it:** it is not free in the tissue. Heavy relative load at reduced total volume concentrates mechanical stress into fewer, harder exposures. Spiering's own ref 30 (Kraemer 2004) is cited as the *upper* bound — too much in-season strength work impairs performance — and a 1-set-at-90% session is a higher per-rep stress environment than 3 sets at 75%, landing on an athlete whose connective tissue is already absorbing a full throwing week. "Cut volume, hold intensity" may be right for the 1RM and wrong for the elbow.
**What would settle it:** an in-season comparison of matched weekly tonnage at high vs moderate relative load with a **throwing-arm** load marker (sleeve stress per pitch, or ROM/strength on the day after) as co-primary. Not run.
**Pitching coach — DEFENDED, with the scope narrowed.** The recommendation stands because the alternative on offer is worse: every industry prescription in §2 cuts intensity, which is the one variable with any supporting evidence at all. But I accept the constraint and I will state it in the output: **hold the load on the lower-body and trunk compounds; this says nothing about overhead or arm-specific work**, where the corpus's own guidance (F-036's cuff-strength loss, the arm-care literature) governs and the in-season dose question is separate. One line on injury and then move on, per the brief: this is a load-distribution caveat, not an injury thesis.

### 5.3 Pitching coach → Biomechanist
**The claim challenged:** *"Gdovin's −6.3 mph is probably a measurement artifact, because elbow varus torque didn't move."*
**Why I doubt it:** you are using F-094's R² = 0.957 as though it were an identity, and F-094 is a **correlation within-athlete across pitches in a single session** — a tight coupling between how hard this pitch was thrown and what the elbow absorbed on *that* pitch. That single paragraph decides whether this is a detraining result or a protocol change.
