## 1. THE ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS

**The question put to the anatomist: given the tissue, is there a reason eccentric-emphasis loading should raise pitch velocity in an arm already at 85+?**

### 1.1 The one mechanism that is genuinely distinctive — and it is architectural, not neural

Eccentric loading is the only resistance modality with a reasonably replicated, *direction-specific* architectural signature: **fascicle lengthening** (sarcomeres added in series), as against the **pennation-angle increase** that characterises conventional concentric-dominant loading.

- **Banks et al. (2024), *Eur J Sport Sci*, DOI 10.1002/ejsc.12215** (correction PMID 40367320). n = 31 healthy young adults (24 ± 3 y), **randomised** to 10 weeks traditional RT (TRT) / flywheel RT (FWRT) / habitual-activity control, **volume-matched**. FWRT increased **fascicle length and DISTAL vastus lateralis CSA**, plus CMJ height and broad-jump distance (p ≤ 0.048). TRT instead increased **pennation angle and PROXIMAL VL CSA**. Both raised maximal isometric strength and fat-free mass *similarly*. ⚠️ **Magnitudes not retrievable this cycle.**
- **Dose context for fascicle length.** 8 weeks of eccentric training **at long muscle length** raised fascicle length ≈ **8.5%**, with **no change** in the short-muscle-length group, and **no change in passive mechanical properties** — the authors attribute it to sarcomere lengthening and/or sarcomerogenesis in series (*J Appl Physiol* 2025, DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00859.2024). A separate VL study reports ≈ **5.2% at 11 weeks**. A 5-week hamstring study reports longer fascicles and *smaller* pennation angles with eccentric work versus *shorter* fascicles with concentric work, with no change in thickness.

**The anatomist's reading:** this is a real, modality-specific adaptation on a 5–11 week clock, and the muscle-length condition matters more than the loading mode — the 8-week trial's own control arm did eccentric work at short length and got nothing. **So the lever, if one exists, is "load eccentrically at long muscle length," not "buy a flywheel."**

### 1.2 Why the mechanism does not obviously reach a pitch — the time-domain objection

A longer fascicle raises the velocity at which a muscle can still produce force and shifts the force–length operating range. For that to show up in a pitch, the pitch has to contain an eccentric event the trained tissue serves. It contains two, and **this corpus has already priced both:**

1. **The lead-leg block.** A **~50–80 ms** braking event (F-645), served by series-elastic lower-limb tendons, and worth **~4–6% of between-pitcher velocity variance once body mass is controlled** (F-061: lead-leg Z GRF r = 0.44 raw → **0.23** bodyweight-controlled).
2. **The shoulder's deceleration.** **−201 ± 70 J** of negative work (F-064), **whose tissue attribution is an untested premise** (F-636 — flagged 2026-10-09) and for which **zero** trials in any population report a rotator-cuff stiffness outcome.

**The objection:** flywheel eccentric braking occupies **hundreds of milliseconds**; the pitching eccentric events are **50–80 ms at the leg and sub-100 ms at the arm**. This is the same time-domain mismatch that the corpus has used against heavy-implement work, and it cuts here too. Registered as **F-773 (MECHANISM)**.

### 1.3 And the anatomist's own concession

The strongest *pro* case is not velocity at all. It is that the one documented eccentric capacity gap in the throwing arm — the deceleration phase — is a **tissue-capacity** argument, not a performance argument. That is injury, which this program treats as a one-line supporting constraint. **One line: eccentric cuff work has a plausible tissue-capacity rationale, no trial relates it to pitch velocity, and a 2025 review's inclusion of it is a recommendation, not a result.** Move on.

## 2. THE BIOMECHANIST PASS — AND THE STRUCTURAL RESULT OF THE DAY

### 2.1 ⭐⭐ THE MODALITY IS NAMED FOR A MECHANISM ITS OWN LITERATURE NEVER VERIFIED

**Spudić & Nosaka (2026), "Do Flywheel Exercises Provide Eccentric-Overload Training?", *Sports Medicine – Open*, DOI 10.1186/s40798-025-00974-y, PMID 41528377.**

The stated conclusion: **flywheel exercises do not necessarily produce eccentric overload.** Whether overload occurs depends on execution tempo, technique, joint angle, age and training history; the overload window, where it exists, is **brief**. And the methodological point that matters most: **the metrics the literature uses to indicate eccentric overload are peak POWER and peak VELOCITY, not peak FORCE** — i.e. the field has been inferring a *force* condition from *velocity-derived* quantities.

⚠️ **One search pass reported "only 17 of 79 studies provided sufficient data to prove eccentric overload existed." A second, targeted pass on that exact string DID NOT REPRODUCE IT. The 17/79 figure is UNVERIFIED and must not be quoted.** The qualitative conclusion reproduced across two independent passes and is corroborated by Banks et al. 2024's own text ("flywheel devices do not necessarily provide eccentric overload").

**Why this is the day's structural result.** This is the **third arrival** of the F-571 / F-721 structure, and the worst-positioned of the three:

### 2.2 The defining metric of the modality is one its own field says not to use

An editorial on flywheel science (*Front Sports Act Living*, PMC10332935) states that concentric and eccentric peak power can each inform training, **but the eccentric:concentric ratio should NOT be used because of its low reliability.** That ratio is precisely the quantity that operationalises "eccentric overload." **Twentieth entry in this corpus's standing reliability gap** (after F-755, F-703, …): a modality sold on a metric with no usable reliability estimate. Registered as **F-763**.

### 2.3 The inertia dial trades the two things a pitcher needs against each other

Across loading studies of the flywheel squat (n = 20 physically active men, five moments of inertia, PMID 37867740; and the inertia–velocity/inertia–power relationships paper, DOI 10.1080/02640414.2020.1860472, PMID 33337956):

- As inertia rises, **peak concentric velocity, peak eccentric velocity, mean concentric velocity and mean eccentric velocity all fall**, while the **peak eccentric : peak concentric power ratio rises.**
- **0.025 kg·m²** → highest **concentric** peak power. **0.075 kg·m²** → more likely to produce higher **eccentric** peak power.
- The inertia–velocity papers recommend prescribing by **velocity**, because the subject-specific relationships fit best for peak concentric velocity.

**So you cannot have overload and limb speed in the same set.** Pitching is a velocity sport with a 145 g implement. Registered as **F-764**.

### 2.4 ⭐ THE ONLY POOLED ESTIMATE FOR THROWING VELOCITY IS NEGATIVE — AND IT IS ONE STUDY IN A META-ANALYSIS'S CLOTHES

**Petruzela, Papla & Stastny (2023), "Conditioning Strategies for Improving Handball Throwing Velocity: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses", *J Hum Kinet* 87:189–200.**

- **13 studies, 174 players.** Composition as reported: **five** resistance-training studies, **one** core training, **one** repeated-shuffle-sprint + small-sided games, **one eccentric-overload training.**
- **Eccentric-overload training: d = −0.15.** Resistance training rated most effective, **d > 0.7**. Core training **d = 0.35**. Small-sided games **d = 1.95 to −2.03**.
- ⚠️ **No confidence interval retrievable.** The review itself notes the small number of elite-player studies limits its conclusions.

**The biomechanist's attack, and it is the whole point:** *"one was on eccentric overload training."* **The pooled eccentric-overload estimate is a single study.** "A meta-analysis finds eccentric overload is negative for throwing velocity" is, on inspection, **Sabido 2017's n = 18 null, restated with a decimal point.** A d of −0.15 at n = 11 vs 7 is indistinguishable from zero and from anything up to a moderate effect in either direction. Registered as **F-765**.

### 2.5 The null itself — and it passed its manipulation check

**Sabido et al. (2017), *Eur J Sport Sci* 17(5):530–538, DOI 10.1080/17461391.2017.1282046.** n = 18 team-handball players, **EOT n = 11 vs control n = 7**. Both groups kept their habitual strength training; EOT **added one session per week for 7 weeks**, 4 × 8 of bilateral half-squat and unilateral lunge.

- **EOT improved power output in BOTH the eccentric and concentric phases** of the trained exercises, and improved functional jumping.
- **"No group showed changes in handball throwing velocity."**

**This is the cleanest object in the topic, and it is a MANIPULATED_NULL whose manipulation check PASSED.** The trained quality moved; the throw did not. That is the exact shape of **F-004** (the jump-height transfer null) arriving in a second modality — and it is *better* evidence than F-721's plyometric trials, which moved ball velocity without ever checking whether they moved an SSC quality. **With the caveat that 11 vs 7 has a detection floor so high that this null excludes almost nothing** (see F-774). Registered as **F-766**.

### 2.6 The one positive trial has no control group, is not randomised, is youth, and its flywheel arm failed its own strength check

**Sports (2026) 14(5):172, DOI 10.3390/sports14050172 — "Effects of Eccentric-Overload vs. Free-Weight High Load Resistance Training on Throwing Velocity in Elite Young Male Handball Players."**

- n = **27** elite **young male** handball players; **free-weight n = 14**, flywheel n = 13. **2 ×/week for 8 weeks**, on top of regular handball training.
- **Free-weight arm:** throwing velocity ↑ **p < 0.05**, and **all** strength measures ↑.
- **Flywheel arm:** throwing velocity ↑ **p = 0.008**, but **upper-body strength gains NOT significant.**
- **No between-group difference at baseline, none at post.** Authors' conclusion: neither method is clearly superior for throwing velocity.
- **Allocation: quasi-experimental, stratified by team and playing position — NOT randomised.**
- ⚠️ **km/h tables not retrievable this cycle. No baseline throwing velocity obtained.**

**Four independent problems, each sufficient on its own.** (1) **No non-training control arm** — 8 weeks of a youth handball season is uncontrolled, and the corpus has killed findings for exactly this (F-416). (2) **Not randomised.** (3) **YOUTH handball with a 425 g ball — SAMPLE MISMATCH, directional only** under this program's 85 mph floor, and Dispute #85 already established that a 425 g handball result cannot be borrowed for a 145 g baseball. (4) **The flywheel arm raised throwing velocity while failing to raise upper-body strength** — which is either a transfer route the strength tests miss, or (far more likely) evidence that neither arm's gain is attributable to the training. Registered as **F-767**; the fourth problem becomes **Dispute #87**.

### 2.7 Against matched conventional loading, the modality has no chronic advantage

- **AEL vs constant-load resistance training, systematic review + meta-analysis (PMC13388742, 2026):** chronic **maximal strength concentric SMD = 0.12, p = 0.41**; **hypertrophy SMD = −0.05, p = 0.87.** Both null. ⚠️ RFD and power outcomes not retrievable.
- **Hu et al. (2024), *Life* 14(7):908 — flywheel vs traditional, 16 articles / 341 subjects:** **maximal strength SMD = 0.24, 95% CI [−0.26, 0.74]** — NS. **Power SMD = 0.47, 95% CI [0.10, 0.84]** — small, favours flywheel, CI excludes zero.

**The honest summary of the controlled literature: one small power advantage, nothing for strength, nothing for hypertrophy.** Registered as **F-768**.

### 2.8 And the number the vendors quote is a pre-post number — the F-416 error, in a second modality

- **Petré et al. (2018), *Sports Med Open*, "Effects of Flywheel Training on Strength-Related Variables: a Meta-analysis":** 20 experimental studies; ES **1.33** maximum strength, **1.19** power, **0.59** muscle CSA, **1.01** horizontal movement, **0.85** vertical. **These are PRE-POST effect sizes, not flywheel-versus-comparator.**

## 4. THE COACH'S PASS — FOUR POINTED QUESTIONS, ANSWERED, THEN TRANSLATED

### Q1 (to the biomechanist): "I have a flywheel in the building. Tread and VeloU talk about eccentrics. Do I put my 85+ arms on it for velocity?"

**Answer: no, and not because it is dangerous — because the modality cannot currently demonstrate that it does the thing it is named for.** The chain of failures: the device may not produce eccentric overload at all (F-762); the metric that would tell you whether it did is unreliable and its own field says not to use it (F-763); turning the inertia up to get overload turns limb speed down (F-764); against matched conventional loading the controlled advantage is one small power effect and nothing else (F-768); the headline effect sizes are pre-post artefacts (F-769); the only throwing-velocity pooled estimate is negative and is one n = 18 study (F-765, F-766); and **no trial has ever been run on a pitcher** (F-771).

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing — this is a decision I make, not a cue he needs. If he asks, the honest sentence is *"the machine is fine, the claim on the box is unverified, and nobody has tested it on a pitcher."*
> **The drill is:** keep the flywheel for what the controlled data supports — a **general-strength and lower-limb power tool**, interchangeable with a barbell, chosen for **joint comfort, accommodating resistance and unilateral loading**, not for a velocity mechanism.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a pitcher who has traded his heavy lower-body block for flywheel sets and now has the same vertical force output with **less** peak-velocity training exposure — the F-764 trade, taken without being named.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** you do not. **There is no measurable check for a velocity effect of this modality at one-roster sample sizes** — F-774 prices it below.

### Q2 (to the biomechanist): "Sabido's guys got stronger eccentrically and threw the same. Is that a real null or just a small study?"

**Answer: it is the most informative object in the topic and it still excludes very little.** What makes it informative is that **the manipulation check passed** — eccentric and concentric power output both rose in the trained exercises, jumping improved, and throwing velocity did not move. That is a *transfer* failure, not a *training* failure, and it is the same shape as F-004. What makes it weak is 11 versus 7.

**The arithmetic (F-774, derived in-cycle, source-independent).** Two-group independent-samples comparison, α = .05 two-tailed, power = .80: n per group = 2(1.96 + 0.8416)²/d² = **15.696/d²**.

| true effect | n per group | total |
|---|---|---|
| d = 0.47 (the one defensible controlled effect — *power*, Hu 2024) | **72** | **144** |
| d = 0.24 (flywheel vs traditional, max strength) | **273** | **546** |
| d = 0.15 (the magnitude of the reported throwing-velocity effect) | **698** | **1,396** |

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing changes in his program on the strength of this.
> **The drill is:** none. This answers a question about *evidence*, not about training.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a staff reading d = −0.15 as "eccentrics hurt velocity." They do not. It means nobody has looked hard enough to see anything.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** **we cannot, on one roster.** 144 athletes for the *power* effect; **1,396 for the throwing effect.** Ole Miss has ~20 arms. **This is a multi-program study or it is nothing** — the same verdict F-758 reached for the med-ball block, and the second consecutive cycle to reach it.

### Q3 (to the anatomist): "Fascicle length is the one thing only eccentrics do. Is that worth a block?"

**Answer: it is the best mechanistic card in the deck and it is still two full steps from a pitch.** Step one is missing: **no study relates fascicle length to pitch velocity, in any population** (F-772). Step two is a confound inside the mechanism itself: the 8-week trial that produced ≈ 8.5% fascicle lengthening got **nothing** from its short-muscle-length eccentric arm. **The active ingredient may be muscle length at loading, not the loading mode** — which would make the flywheel irrelevant to its own best mechanism, and would make a Bulgarian split squat at depth or a deficit RDL the cheaper route.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We load you at long positions, under control, because that is where the tissue changes. The machine doesn't matter; the position and the control do."*
> **The drill is:** **loaded eccentrics at end-range, lower body** — deficit RDL, Bulgarian split squat to full depth, slow eccentric step-down — 3–4 sets of 5–6, **4–6 s lowering**, in the general-strength slot he already has, **replacing nothing on the throwing sheet**.
> **On video the failure looks like:** range cut short under load — the exact condition that produced **zero** architectural change in the trial's control arm. If his knee doesn't pass his toe and his hip doesn't reach depth, he is in the arm that got nothing.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** **not by velocity.** The honest check is the **training log** (did end-range loading actually happen, at depth, for 6+ weeks) plus **end-range strength** at the loaded position. **A velocity check is not available** at n = 20.

### Q4 (to both): "Then what IS the defensible use of eccentric work for a pitcher this week?"

**Answer — and all three agents agree on this one.** The defensible use is not velocity. It is that **the pitching delivery contains two eccentric events this corpus has already located and priced — the ~50–80 ms lead-leg block (F-645, ~4–6% of between-pitcher velocity variance after mass control, F-061) and the −201 ± 70 J shoulder deceleration (F-064, tissue attribution UNTESTED, F-636)** — and that **tissue capacity for the second is the one place eccentric loading has a mechanical home that no other modality covers** (F-773). That is a capacity argument, which this program's mission treats as a supporting constraint, not a velocity lever.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We do controlled eccentric work so your arm can absorb what it already absorbs 100 times a night. It is not going to add a mile per hour."*
> **The drill is:** **eccentric-emphasis external/internal rotation**, light, 3 × 8 with a **3–4 s lowering phase**, twice a week, in the arm-care slot that already exists. **No new slot, no new volume, nothing removed from throwing.**
> **On video the failure looks like:** speed on the lowering phase — the moment he drops the band or dumbbell instead of resisting it, the eccentric stimulus is gone and it is just another set.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** **capacity markers, never velocity.** Post-outing ER/IR strength retention on the device he already owns (ArmCare or equivalent), and the **non-throwing shoulder as his own control** (the free measurement F-622 identified, still unexecuted). ⚠️ **No trial relates any of this to velocity, and the 2025 review that recommends it is a recommendation, not a result.**

## 5. CROSS-EXAMINATION

### 5.1 BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY

**The claim challenged:** "fascicle lengthening is the distinctive eccentric adaptation, and it is a plausible velocity route."
**Why I doubt it:** every fascicle number you have is **vastus lateralis or hamstring, in untrained or recreationally active young adults, with a jump outcome.** The limb is wrong for the arm, the population is wrong for an 85+ staff, and **Banks 2024's own result undercuts you**: TRT and FWRT produced *equal* maximal strength and fat-free mass gains, differing only in *where* the CSA went. An architectural redistribution with equal strength is not obviously a performance lever; it may be a wash reported as a difference.
**What would settle it:** fascicle length measured against pitch velocity in a single 85+ sample — a between-subject correlation would do as a first pass, and it does not exist (F-772).
**ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY'S RESPONSE — CONCEDED, with one retention.** Conceded: the limb is wrong, the population is wrong, and "equal strength, different architecture" is not a performance claim. **Retained:** the CMJ and broad-jump gains in Banks' flywheel arm reached p ≤ 0.048 where the traditional arm's did not, and jump output is the one lower-limb quality that survived into a professional pitching sample (F-006, drop jump R² = 0.30, n = 53 pros). **That is a two-link chain with a missing middle, and I am not claiming more than that.**

### 5.2 ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY → PITCHING-COACH

**The claim challenged:** "eccentric cuff work for deceleration capacity is defensible this week."
**Why I doubt it:** you are prescribing against **F-636**, which this corpus flagged ten days ago — the **−201 ± 70 J** of negative work at the shoulder is **not established to be absorbed by the tissue you are loading**. Zero trials in any population report a rotator-cuff stiffness or capacity outcome. You are loading a structure on an energy figure whose owner is unknown.
**What would settle it:** any trial reporting a rotator-cuff capacity outcome (stiffness, eccentric peak torque retention) against a throwing exposure.
**COACH'S RESPONSE — DEFENDED, narrowly, and the defence is about cost not mechanism.** The prescription is **3 × 8 light with a slow lowering, inside an existing arm-care slot**. Its cost is approximately zero and it replaces nothing. **I am not claiming the mechanism; I am claiming the dose is below the threshold at which being wrong matters.** If the anatomist's objection is that I should not *tell the pitcher a mechanism I cannot support* — **conceded, and the script in Q4 was rewritten to say "absorb what it already absorbs," not "protect your UCL."**

### 5.3 PITCHING-COACH → BIOMECHANIST

**The claim challenged:** "d = −0.15 for eccentric overload on throwing velocity."
**Why I doubt your use of it:** you presented a **meta-analytic-looking number that is one study of 18 handball players**, and you presented it in a brief whose whole job is to catch exactly that move. If I quote "−0.15" to a strength coach, I am doing to him what the med-ball literature did to this program.
**What would settle it:** the confidence interval, which is not retrievable this cycle.
**BIOMECHANIST'S RESPONSE — CONCEDED, and the concession is registered in the finding.** F-765's CLAIM field states that the pooled estimate **is a single study** and that the number must not be quoted as a pooled result. **The correct sentence is: "the only controlled test of eccentric-overload training on throwing velocity is an n = 18 null whose manipulation check passed."** That is weaker than "−0.15" and more useful.

### 5.4 BIOMECHANIST → PITCHING-COACH (the carried-forward one)

**The claim challenged:** the coach's Q1 verdict, "keep the flywheel as a general-strength tool."
**Why I doubt it:** on your own reasoning in Q2, you cannot detect a difference between the flywheel and a barbell at n = 20 either. **"Interchangeable with a barbell" is a claim you have no more power to support than the velocity claim you just rejected.**
**COACH'S RESPONSE — CONCEDED AND RESTATED.** Correct.
