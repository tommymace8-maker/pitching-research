## §1 — THE ANCHOR, READ IN FULL: the meta-analysis everyone cites does not measure what its title says

**Grgic J., Varovic D. (2022). "Ergogenic Effects of Caffeine on Ballistic (Throwing) Performance: A Meta-Analytical Review." *Nutrients* 14(19):4155. PMCID PMC9572449, PMID 36235804. doi:10.3390/nu14194155.** ✅ **READ IN FULL from the PMC object store.**

Headline result, verbatim from §3.4:

> "In the main meta-analysis, there was a significant ergogenic effect of caffeine on throwing performance (SMD: 0.19; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.33; p = 0.007; I² = 62%)... In the subgroup analysis for studies that evaluated throwing velocity, there was a significant ergogenic effect of caffeine (SMD: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.10, 0.37; p = 0.0006; I² = 28%)."

That sentence — *caffeine improves throwing velocity, SMD 0.24* — is the one in circulation. **Then read Table 1.** Ten studies, 151 participants (111 M / 40 F), median n = 13 per study:

| Study | Participants | Dose | Throwing test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bellar 2012 | 9 college shot putters | 100 mg gum | Shot put |
| Correia Rocha 2021 | 10 male handball | 5 mg/kg | **Medicine ball (2 kg)** |
| Filip-Stachnik 2021 | 12 resistance-trained M | 9 or 12 mg/kg | **Bench press throw, 30% 1RM** |
| Giráldez-Costas 2022 | 13 shot putters | 3 mg/kg | Backward throw, standing shot put, shot put, ballistic push-up |
| Grgic & Mikulic 2017 | 17 resistance-trained M | 6 mg/kg | **Medicine ball (9 kg)** |
| Martin 2015 | 12 resistance-trained M | 75 mg gel | **Medicine ball (5 kg)** |
| **Muñoz 2020** | **31 professional handball** | **3 mg/kg** | **7-m and 9-m handball throw** |
| Robles-González 2022 | 15 resistance-trained F | 3 mg/kg | **Bench press throw (bare 17 kg bar)** |
| Sabol 2020 | 20 resistance-trained M | 2, 4, 6 mg/kg | **Medicine ball (9 kg)** |
| Wilk 2020 | 12 resistance-trained M | 3 or 6 mg/kg | **Bench press throw, 30% 1RM** |

**ONE study out of ten threw a ball overhead.** Six of ten used resistance-trained non-throwers. The "throwing velocity" subgroup that produced SMD = 0.24 is, in substance, **bar velocity in a bench press throw and release velocity of a 2–9 kg medicine ball.**

**BIOMECHANIST:** This is check #3 from the standing brief — *does the number measure what the sentence says it measures?* — and the answer is no. A 9 kg medicine ball is **62× the mass of a baseball** (9000 g vs 145 g) and leaves the hand at a small fraction of the velocity. A bench press throw is bilateral, horizontal, closed-chain-ish and trunk-supported. A pitch is a unilateral open kinetic chain terminating in the fastest human joint rotation measured. These sit at **opposite ends of the force–velocity curve.** There is no warrant for carrying a pooled effect across that gap, and the paper never claims there is — the *field* does.

To the authors' credit, they never say "baseball." The failure is downstream. But two further things belong on the record:
1. **No publication-bias assessment was performed at all** — no Egger's test, no funnel plot, no trim-and-fill. Defensible at k = 10, but it means SMD 0.19 is uncorrected.
2. **Every subgroup that failed became significant after removing one study.** Throwing distance: p = 0.22 → p = 0.007. Doses > 3 mg/kg: p = 0.19 → p < 0.0001. The outlier (Correia Rocha 2021) was identified by **visual inspection of the forest plot**, post hoc. That is a researcher-degrees-of-freedom flag, and the conclusions lean on the rescued versions.

Risk of bias: all ten studies rated **"some concerns"** overall (no allocation concealment, no pre-registration).

## §2 — THE ONE ON-TASK STUDY, READ IN FULL: one of four throw tests

**Muñoz A. et al. (2020). "Effects of CYP1A2 and ADORA2A Genotypes on the Ergogenic Response to Caffeine in Professional Handball Players." *Genes (Basel)* 11(8):933. PMCID PMC7464361, PMID 32823594.** ✅ **READ IN FULL.** (Note: the meta-analysis lists it only as "Muñoz et al. 2020"; it is in *Genes*, not *Nutrients*.)

n = 31 professional handball (16 M, 15 F). **3 mg/kg, 60 min pre.** Crossover, placebo-controlled. Radar: Pocket Radar Ball Coach, test-retest CV **3.5%**. Max of three throws, 60 s apart.

**Four throw tests were run. One reached significance** (§3, verbatim):

| Test | Placebo | Caffeine | p | ES |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Throw 9 m, no keeper** | **82.55 ± 7.64** | **84.90 ± 7.32 km/h** | **0.008 ✅** | **0.31** |
| Throw 9 m + keeper | 83.72 ± 7.40 | 85.59 ± 7.10 | 0.093 | 0.26 |
| Throw 7 m, no keeper | 82.69 ± 9.25 | 84.03 ± 8.91 | 0.065 | 0.15 |
| Throw 7 m + keeper | 81.97 ± 7.72 | 82.62 ± 7.38 | 0.492 | 0.09 |

The abstract reports this as *"caffeine increased... ball throwing velocity from 9 m."* True as written. But the study ran **four throw tests plus CMJ, sprint, agility and handgrip — eight performance outcomes — with no multiplicity correction reported.** Three of the four throws, including both contested ones, did not clear 0.05. **This corpus has dismantled a headline on exactly this ground eight days ago** (F-379, the r = 0.583). The same discipline applies here.

**Magnitude and population.** The significant gain is 2.35 km/h on 82.55 = **+2.85%**. Note the absolute level: **82.55 km/h = 51.3 mph.** ⚠️ **SAMPLE MISMATCH on two axes at once** — 51 mph, and a ~425–475 g handball rather than a 145 g baseball. Naively transferring +2.85% to a 90 mph fastball gives **+2.6 mph**, which is the number the supplement industry would quote and which nothing in this literature supports. **That percentage transfer is the error to refuse.**

**⚠️ AND THE HABITUATION CLAUSE IS THE WHOLE BALLGAME.** The sample's habitual caffeine intake was **60 ± 25 mg/day** — under one cup of coffee. The authors' own conclusion is scoped to it: *"in players with low habituation to caffeine"*, and they explicitly warn that *"there is a progressive tolerance to the ergogenic benefit of caffeine with chronic ingestion."* **A 2026 college pitcher on energy drinks is not this sample.** See §5.

## §3 — THE CLEANEST RESULT OF THE CYCLE: a null with its positive controls firing

**Life (2026) 16(6):954. "Dose-Dependent Effects of Acute Caffeine Ingestion on Physical and Cognitive Performance in Trained Female Handball Players: A Randomized Crossover Study." PMCID PMC13302302. doi:10.3390/life16060954.** ✅ **READ IN FULL.**

n = 20 trained female handball. Randomised, **double-blind**, placebo-controlled, three-way crossover (placebo / 3 mg/kg / 6 mg/kg), 72 h washout. Throw: standing 7-m maximal throw, IHF size-2 ball, three trials, **mean** of three, Stalker Solo 2 radar.

**Throwing velocity (§Results, verbatim): "No significant main effect of supplementation condition was detected for throwing velocity (F = 0.04, p = 0.961, η²p = 0.004)."**

| Condition | Throwing velocity |
|---|---|
| Placebo | 73.79 ± 6.05 km/h |
| Caffeine 3 mg/kg | 73.95 ± 6.49 km/h |
| Caffeine 6 mg/kg | 73.71 ± 6.60 km/h |

Mean differences with CIs: **3 mg/kg vs placebo +0.16 km/h [−1.82, +2.15]**; 6 mg/kg vs placebo −0.08 km/h [−1.96, +1.80]. **±2 km/h = ±1.24 mph, centred on zero.**

**This is not an underpowered null, and that is what makes it the most important study in the cycle.** In the *same* athletes, in the *same* session, on the *same* doses, the positive controls fired hard:
- **Yo-Yo IRT-1: +227 m at 3 mg/kg [+88.6, +365.5] and +280 m at 6 mg/kg [+110.0, +450.0]** — roughly +23.5% and +29.0%, η²p = 0.415.
- **Flanker reaction time: −136.6 ms at 6 mg/kg [−209.0, −64.1]**, ~18.5%, η²p = 0.486.

**The dose was demonstrably bioactive in these exact athletes on that exact day. It moved endurance enormously and reaction time substantially, and moved the throw by η²p = 0.004.** A null flanked by two large confirmed effects in the same design is worth far more than a bare null.

A **second independent handball null** exists: Yildirim U.C. (2023), *Int. J. Disabil. Sports Health Sci.* 6:107–115 — titled, in terms, *"Acute Low and Moderate Doses of Caffeine Improve Aerobic Endurance but **Not** Throwing Velocity in Trained Female Handball Players."* ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY — title and abstract conclusion only, not read at source.**

**Tally across the three overhead-throwing studies: roughly one positive subtest out of six or seven.** The meta-analytic "ergogenic effect on throwing velocity" does not survive restriction to actual overhead ball throwing.

## §4 — ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY: the mechanism predicts exactly this pattern, and predicts something testable

**ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY.** Caffeine's dominant *in vivo* ergogenic mechanism at 3–6 mg/kg is **adenosine receptor antagonism in the central nervous system**, which lowers perceived effort and raises voluntary activation and arousal. The direct peripheral effects (calcium handling at the sarcoplasmic reticulum, catecholamine release) require concentrations well above those achieved by an oral sporting dose.

The meta-analysis proposes the RFD/motor-unit-recruitment route (§4: *"it seems likely that caffeine ingestion increases motor unit recruitment, resulting in an increase in RFD"*). **I accept the mechanism and reject its application here**, because of a ceiling argument this corpus already uses elsewhere:

> **In a maximal single-effort ballistic task performed by an expert, voluntary activation is already essentially complete.** There is no recruitment reserve for an adenosine antagonist to unlock. A drug that works by making a hard effort *feel* easier has nothing to purchase when the limiting factor is intersegmental coordination and technique, not willingness to try hard.

This predicts the observed pattern precisely: **large effects where effort perception is limiting (Yo-Yo, +29%), moderate where vigilance is limiting (Flanker, −18.5%), nil where technique is limiting (the throw, η²p = 0.004).** The *Life* authors reach the same conclusion independently: *"caffeine's potential to increase central nervous system stimulation, alertness, or general activation levels may not directly translate to the fine motor control, muscle activation sequence, and technical calibration processes that determine throw speed... throwing technique is often optimized near the individual performance ceiling."*

**⚠️ AND HERE IS THE PREDICTION THAT MATTERS, AND IT CONNECTS DIRECTLY TO YESTERDAY'S CYCLE.**

If caffeine is an effort-perception and fatigue-resistance drug rather than a peak-output drug, then its expected effect on a pitcher is **not on the best fastball of the night — it is on the ones thrown in the sixth and seventh innings.** That is precisely the within-outing retention quantity F-384 and F-385 opened yesterday.

**Nobody has tested this.** Every caffeine-and-throwing study in existence measures **fresh, maximal, single throws** — which the mechanism says is the one condition where caffeine should do least. The literature has systematically measured caffeine in the place its own mechanism predicts it will fail. **This is a new gap and it is the most promising unexplored claim the cycle produced.**

## §5 — HABITUATION: the variable that decides whether any of this applies to your roster

Three converging facts:
1. Muñoz's sample habitually consumed **60 ± 25 mg/day** and the authors scope their conclusion to low habituation, warning explicitly of *"progressive tolerance to the ergogenic benefit of caffeine with chronic ingestion, together with a progressive increase in the prevalence of side effects."*
2. A typical 2026 US college athlete's intake is far higher — a single 16 oz energy drink is 160–300 mg, and multiple daily servings are ordinary. ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY / general knowledge; this corpus holds no measured intake distribution for college pitchers, and neither, as far as the sweep could establish, does anyone else.**
3. **Withdrawal is a performance decrement in its own right.** A habituated consumer who abstains on a start day is not at baseline — he is in withdrawal, with headache and reduced alertness. Nothing in this literature models that, because every study recruited low consumers and mandated 48 h abstinence, which *manufactures* withdrawal in a habituated subject and would bias a habituated athlete's placebo trial downward.

**Practical consequence:** the honest prior for a habituated 85+ arm is **smaller than the already-near-zero effect measured in unhabituated handball players**, and the *placebo* condition in any self-run test is contaminated by withdrawal. This is the single most actionable thing in the cycle and it is free to measure. See §8.

## §6 — THE COUNTER-RISK: the dose that might help output is the dose flagged for dexterity

**⚠️ SNIPPET-ONLY THROUGHOUT THIS SECTION — the source review is on sciencedirect.com, which is refused by the proxy. This is a LEAD, not a finding.**

A review ("Caffeine: benefits and drawbacks for technical performance", ScienceDirect S0266435623000426) is summarised as reporting that caffeine's effect on dexterity is dose-dependent, with **~2.5 mg/kg capable of inducing fine hand tremor and ~5 mg/kg associated with worsened dexterity**, partially mitigated by tolerance — i.e. caffeine "may increase speed at the expense of dexterity." The primary sources behind this appear to be **Jacobson et al. (1991, 1992), *Perceptual and Motor Skills*** — old, small, and in non-athletes. **Not verified at source. Do not quote the numbers.**

**PITCHING-COACH:** If this holds even directionally it inverts the whole recommendation, because **command is at least half of what an 85+ arm is being paid for.** The mission is performance, not just velocity. A 3–6 mg/kg dose that buys ~0 mph and costs any fine-motor precision is a straightforwardly bad trade for a pitcher — and *worse for a pitcher than for any athlete in the existing literature*, because no study in this topic used a population whose sport is a precision task.

**BIOMECHANIST:** And note what is missing: **not one study in this entire topic measured throwing ACCURACY.** Muñoz measured velocity into an open goal. The *Life* study measured velocity into a goal. The meta-analysis's ten studies measured distance and velocity. **The outcome variable that matters most to a pitcher has never been collected in a caffeine-and-throwing study.** That is the topic's largest hole and it is a cheap one to fill.

## §7 — THE OTHER TWO ARMS: two documented absences

**CREATINE.** Searched specifically for a randomised trial with an overhead-throwing-velocity outcome. **None found, in baseball or any throwing sport.** What exists:
- Ferrauti et al. (2003), *Eur J Sport Sci* — review reporting that **only ~5 articles** had investigated creatine under conditions designed for ballgames; 18 PubMed hits total, one of them softball. ⚠️ snippet-only.
- A creatine loading study in trained female softball players (PMID 14767409): 20 g/day for one week improved **mean strength and endurance of repeated contractions but not maximal static strength or dynamic peak torque.** ⚠️ snippet-only; **no throwing outcome.**
- In soccer players (PMC6520963 meta-analysis), creatine did **not** improve phosphagen-system tests (strength, single jump, single sprint, agility). ⚠️ snippet-only.

**The verdict is an absence, and it should be stated as one.** Creatine's mechanism — raising intramuscular phosphocreatine — is well established and its effect on *repeated* high-intensity work and on training-mediated lean mass gain is among the best-supported in sports nutrition. Since **body mass is the strongest correlate of velocity in this entire corpus (F-001, F-002)**, the plausible route by which creatine helps a pitcher is **indirect and slow: more training volume tolerated → more lean mass → velocity.** That is a months-long mechanism, not a game-day one, and **it has never been tested end-to-end with a velocity outcome in a thrower.** Anyone selling creatine as a throwing-velocity aid is extrapolating across a gap nobody has measured.

**HYDRATION.** ⚠️ **The search returned almost nothing but content farms** — "Baseball Egg", baseballtips.com, getvari.app, an ArmCare blog post. **No primary study of hydration status and pitching performance was found, for any population.**

And the sweep caught a **live laundering event**. The search summariser asserted, with no citation attached: *"dehydrated players show decreased fine motor control, reducing throwing accuracy and pitching command by measurable margins."* **There is no such measurement.** That sentence is a content-farm paraphrase being handed back as fact — the third instance of this vector after F-274 and F-275, and the first where the fabricated specificity ("by measurable margins") attached to *command*, this corpus's most load-bearing outcome.

What is real, and is a **SAMPLE MISMATCH — directional only**: progressive dehydration degrades basketball skill performance, with significance reached around **2% body-mass loss** in a randomised six-trial design in 17 male basketball players aged 17–28 (Baker et al. 2007, PMID 17596779, ⚠️ snippet-only). A separate elite-basketball study found three-point shooting success **not** significantly reduced at 2.1% body-mass loss, with the authors noting kinematics were robust enough to maintain scoring (⚠️ snippet-only). **The two do not agree**, and neither involved throwing, an overhead athlete, or a pitcher.

**Honest position: 2% is a reasonable operational guardrail imported from other sports, and nothing more. It is not a pitching finding.**

**NCAA REGULATORY.** ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY — the primary PDF was retrieved from `ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com` but could not be parsed (§0).** Reported: caffeine is a banned stimulant **above a urine concentration of 15 µg/mL**, described as requiring roughly **500 mg** to approach; creatine appears among permissible institutionally-provided supplements, alongside the standing NCAA warning that no supplement is NCAA-approved and that supplements are poorly regulated and can cause positive tests. **Verify against the 2026-27 manual before acting on any of it** — `https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/ssi/substance/SSI_DrugTestingManual.pdf`. A compliance decision should not rest on a search snippet.

## §8 — TRANSLATION: the coach's four questions

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "The meta-analysis says caffeine improves throwing velocity. Why am I not allowed to use that?"**
Because nine of its ten studies threw a medicine ball, a shot, or a barbell, and the tenth is the handball study whose four throw tests produced one significant result. Restricted to overhead ball throwing, the record is ~1 positive subtest in 6–7 across three studies, and the best-designed of them — double-blind, three-arm, with two large positive controls firing in the same session — puts the 3 mg/kg effect at **+0.16 km/h [−1.82, +2.15]**, i.e. **+0.1 mph with a ±1.2 mph interval.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "Caffeine is not a velocity drug. The best study on it moved a throw by a tenth of a mile an hour. What it *does* do is keep you sharp and let you repeat hard efforts — so it's a late-innings and a long-day tool, not a radar-gun tool."
> **The drill is:** nothing. There is no drill. The intervention is a dosing and timing decision.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing — and that is the point. Any pitcher who tells you he threw harder on a pre-workout has run an unblinded n-of-1 with no placebo. **Caffeine is nearly impossible to blind**, which is exactly why unblinded self-reports about it are worthless.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** you can't, on velocity, in under the number of paired sessions in §9. Don't try.

**COACH → ANATOMY: "Then when would it actually help one of my guys?"**
Where effort perception or vigilance is the binding constraint, not technique. Three real situations: **(a)** a starter in the sixth and seventh — the retention question, which is *untested* but is what the mechanism predicts; **(b)** a reliever warming up at 11 pm after a rain delay or in the second game of a doubleheader; **(c)** a long travel day into an evening start. The Yo-Yo result (+23–29%) and the Flanker result (−137 ms) are the effects with real magnitudes behind them, and neither is a velocity effect.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "If you use it, use it for alertness on a day you're flat — not to add a tick."
> **How we know it's working:** subjective alertness and, if you want a number, simple reaction time. **Not the radar gun.**

**COACH → BOTH: "Could it make command worse?"**
Possibly, and **nobody has measured it.** Not one study in this topic collected a throwing-accuracy outcome. The dexterity/tremor literature (§6, snippet-only, old, non-athlete) suggests a dose-dependent fine-motor cost starting around the doses that might help ballistic output. **For a pitcher this is the live risk and the evidence base is empty.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "If you use caffeine, keep it off command days until we've looked at it. Bullpens are for learning where the ball goes."
> **On video the failure looks like:** jittery, quick tempo, rushing the delivery, misses arm-side and up.
> **How we know:** §9's command arithmetic — and per F-374 use **release-point SD**, not strike rate, because it is continuous, uncontaminated by the catcher (F-309), and needs far fewer pitches.

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "What about creatine and water?"**
Creatine: no throwing-velocity trial exists anywhere. Its plausible route to your pitchers is slow and indirect — training tolerance → lean mass → velocity via F-001/F-002 — and that chain has never been tested end to end. It is a reasonable *training-phase* supplement on general grounds and it is **not** a velocity intervention. Water: there is no pitching study at all; 2% body-mass loss is a guardrail borrowed from basketball, where two studies disagree. Treat both as hygiene, not as levers.

## §9 — DETECTION: what it would cost to settle this on your own mound

**Velocity.** Paired crossover, caffeine vs placebo, k paired sessions, comparing session mean fastball velocity. With within-outing pitch-to-pitch SD σ_p = 1.0 mph (F-289's bracket) over n = 30 fastballs, σ_p/√n = 0.18 mph — **negligible. The binding term is σ_s, the session-to-session SD of an athlete's mean velocity, which this corpus does not hold** (the F-385 problem again, and the **eleventh** entry in the "already in every radar log, published by nobody" pattern).

Paired sessions needed for 80% power, α = .05 two-sided, δ = the true effect:

| σ_s | to detect **1.0 mph** | to detect **0.5 mph** |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 mph | **2 pairs** | 8 pairs |
| 0.5 mph | **5 pairs** | 18 pairs |
| 0.8 mph | **11 pairs** | 42 pairs |

**Read the table against §3's confidence interval.** The best evidence bounds the true effect near zero with a ±1.24 mph interval. So the realistic target is the **0.5 mph column — 8 to 42 paired sessions per pitcher.** That is a season of bullpens to chase an effect the literature says is probably zero. **The velocity test is not worth running.** The table's value is that it prices the question and closes it.

**Command.** Per F-374, release-point SD is the right outcome. The corpus holds no published release-point SD split by any condition, so this cannot be priced exactly today — but it is the measurement to build toward, and it is the one where the evidence base is genuinely empty rather than genuinely null.

**Blinding.** Any self-run version needs capsules or decaf-vs-caf, randomised, with the pitcher and the person holding the radar both blind. **An unblinded caffeine trial measures expectancy, not caffeine.**

## §11 — CROSS-EXAMINATION

**① BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY.** *"Your ceiling argument is unfalsifiable as stated. 'Voluntary activation is already complete in experts' explains the null after the fact, but you'd have used the same mechanism to explain a positive result — you said yourself that caffeine raises activation. What would falsify you?"*
**CONCEDED IN PART, AND DEFENDED.** The argument is falsifiable and here is the test: **the ceiling claim predicts that caffeine's effect on throwing velocity should be LARGER in less-skilled or fatigued throwers and should shrink toward zero as expertise and freshness rise.** Three observations already point that way — the effect appeared in the *unopposed* 9 m throw and not the opposed ones, it appeared in low-caffeine-habituation athletes, and it vanished entirely in the cleanest double-blind design. A direct falsification would be a positive caffeine effect on peak velocity in fresh, expert, high-velocity throwers. **That study does not exist, so the claim stands as a mechanism, not as a finding, and it is labelled MECHANISM in the registry.**

**② PITCHING-COACH → BIOMECHANIST.** *"You're leaning on a 20-person null in female handball players throwing 46 mph to tell a 92 mph right-hander that caffeine does nothing. That's the same population error you flag in everyone else. Why is your null exempt?"*
**CONCEDED — AND THIS IS THE CYCLE'S SHARPEST SELF-CORRECTION.** It is not exempt. The *Life* sample throws 73.79 km/h = **45.9 mph** with a size-2 ball. That is *further* from an 85+ arm than Muñoz's 51 mph men. **The null carries a SAMPLE MISMATCH exactly as severe as the positive result does, and the registry records it that way.** What survives the objection is narrower and I will state only that: **(a)** the meta-analytic case for "caffeine improves throwing velocity" is built on non-overhead tasks and that is a *design* criticism immune to population; **(b)** the *Life* study's value is its internal positive controls — the demonstration that a bioactive dose can move endurance 29% and a throw 0.2% *in the same people on the same day* is a statement about **task type**, which generalises better than a magnitude does; and **(c)** no claim of a *zero* effect at 90 mph is made or supported. **The correct summary is "unmeasured at 85+, and near-zero wherever it has been measured" — not "zero."**

**③ ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY → PITCHING-COACH.** *"You want to recommend caffeine for late-inning retention on the strength of a mechanism and no data. That is precisely the move — a mechanism promoted to a prescription — that cost this corpus stride length and extension."*
**CONCEDED, FULLY, AND THE RECOMMENDATION IS WITHDRAWN.** §4's retention prediction is **a hypothesis and a proposed study, not advice.** It is registered as MECHANISM with CONFIDENCE low, and §12 hands it forward as a question. **Nothing in §8 tells a pitcher to take caffeine to hold velocity in the seventh.** The only thing §8 recommends is measuring habitual intake, which is a measurement, not an intervention.

**④ BIOMECHANIST → PITCHING-COACH.** *"Your one weekly action is a caffeine-intake log. That's an audit, not a training intervention. Is this cycle shipping a recommendation or admitting it has none?"*
**DEFENDED.** It is shipping the correct recommendation, which in this topic is a small one. The evidence says the velocity lever does not exist; the honest deliverable is therefore not a protocol but the one measurement that decides whether the topic is live for a given athlete — and per §5 it also determines whether a pitcher's *placebo* state is contaminated by withdrawal, which is a genuine, unmodelled, and plausibly **negative** performance effect on start day. **A cycle that manufactured a training block here would be padding.** The corpus's standing instruction is to say so rather than pad.
