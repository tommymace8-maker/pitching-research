## 1. The question has three answers hiding in it

A coach asking "does a slider-heavy outing cost more?" is asking three unrelated questions:

1. **More arm load per pitch?** → Answerable, answered, and the folklore has the sign backwards. §2.
2. **More performance cost later — velocity, command, next outing?** → **Never measured, in any population.** §6.
3. **More injury risk?** → Out of scope, and inconclusive anyway.

**The literature answers only (1). The coach is asking (2).**

## 2. 🔵 ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY — the fastball is the expensive pitch

Five independent samples, one direction (F-483):

- **Escamilla 2017 (professional):** varus torque 8–9% greater in FB and SL than CH; deceleration-phase elbow flexor torque 9–14% greater in FB than CB and CH.
- **Fleisig 2006 (21 collegiate):** loads greatest in FB, least in CH; **"few kinetic differences" between FB and CB**; slider inconclusive on n.
- **Makhni 2018 (n = 37, 8 pitches per type, randomised order):** FB > CB and CH.
- **Okoroha 2018 (youth ⚠️):** FB 47.3 > CB 45.0 > CH 44.2 N·m.
- **Hodakowski 2025 (professional, AJSM):** FB greater **cumulative** varus torque **and** loading rate than **all** other types; no CH-vs-breaking-ball difference; **and no relationship between spin rate and varus torque, within or across type.**

**ESTABLISHED for direction. WEAK for every magnitude — all five are snippet-only, and not one reports its sample's velocity.**

> **The pitch that loads the elbow most is the one nobody restricts.**

## 3. ⭐ THE RESULT — a breaking ball is not a slower fastball at the elbow

### 3a. The exponent, source-verified and MANIPULATED (F-485)

PMC12820011, read in full. **24 PROFESSIONAL pitchers**, 480 Hz motion capture, fastballs, **effort manipulated within pitcher**, mixed-effects model:

| | 75% effort | 100% effort |
|---|---|---|
| Ball velocity | 35.6 ± 1.0 m/s = **79.6 mph** | 39.7 ± 0.7 m/s = **88.8 mph** |
| Peak elbow varus torque | 73.1 ± 3.0 N·m | 92.1 ± 3.5 N·m |

✅ **88.8 mph at max effort — this clears the floor. An on-population INTERVENTION, which this corpus almost never gets.**

`ln(92.1/73.1) / ln(39.7/35.6)` = **2.12.** Within a pitcher, **torque ≈ velocity².** At the margin, **+1 mph off 89 costs ≈ +2.4% elbow torque.**

**Second, independently useful result from the same paper: "take something off" does not work as instructed.** A professional told to throw **75%** delivered **90 ± 4%** of his velocity and **80 ± 8%** of his torque. A high-schooler told **50%** delivered **86 ± 7%** of velocity and **76 ± 11%** of torque. **If you are running a flat-ground day or a rehab progression by RPE, the athlete is working harder than either of you thinks. Use the gun.**

### 3b. Apply it across pitch types (F-486)

Streepy 2024 poster (n = 41 pro, ⚠️ **snippet — F-484**): **FB 93.7, CB 91.0, CH 84.1 N·m.**

| | speed ratio to FB | **predicted** at v^2.12 | **observed** |
|---|---|---|---|
| CB, 16 mph off 94 | 0.830 | 67.3% | — |
| CB, 12 mph off 94 | 0.872 | 74.9% | — |
| CB, 12 mph off 88 | 0.864 | 73.3% | — |
| **Curveball** | — | **67–75%** | **97.1%** |
| **Changeup** | — | **~81%** | **89.8%** |

> ### The curveball carries **22–30 percentage points more elbow torque than its ball speed predicts.** At the elbow it looks like a **97%-effort fastball delivered 12–15 mph slower.**

**Survives the whole velocity bracket. Falsified if a professional sample ever shows a CB–FB gap under ~5 mph, or if the AJSM full text reports a different ratio.**

## 4. ✅ The arsenal shift is priced — and F-303 SURVIVES (F-487)

```
mean per-pitch peak varus torque, shifting s of pitches FB → CB:
  s = 0.10 → −0.27 N·m  (−0.29%)
  s = 0.15 → −0.41 N·m  (−0.43%)
  s = 0.20 → −0.54 N·m  (−0.58%)
```

> **A 15-point shift toward curveballs moves mean per-pitch elbow torque by under half a percent, and the sign is NEGATIVE.** On cumulative torque and loading rate the shift is mildly *protective* (the fastball leads on both).

**The two results are not in tension:** the breaking ball is **expensive relative to its own ball speed** and **nearly free relative to the fastball it displaces.** F-303's anatomist caveat is **closed in F-303's favour** — at the elbow, on peak torque, per pitch, on a poster magnitude.

## 5. The measurement war — and one paper that should not be cited

**Trunk sensors see nothing (F-488).** Agresta 2022, n = 10 D1, four workload metrics: trunk **p = 0.34 / 0.57 / 0.73 / 0.34**. Forearm: **p < 0.001 / 0.05 / 0.008 / < 0.001**. Authors: *"trunk-based workload estimates may not appropriately reflect external mechanical load at the elbow or the shoulder."* ⚠️ Underpowered null at n = 10 under the corpus's own F-439 rule; the defensible reading is *the trunk is the wrong place to look*, corroborated by the positive forearm contrast in the same data.

**The forearm sees something, and it points the other way (F-490).** Peak resultant acceleration: FB 1288.7 ± 184.1, **CB 1450.9 ± 243.5 (+12.6%, d = 0.75)**, CH 1241.3 ± 190.6 (−3.7%, n.s.). **Inverted from the kinetics ordering.** Not a contradiction — **peak forearm acceleration is kinematic and dominated by pronation/supination and deceleration; varus torque is kinetic and peaks at max external rotation.** A curveball reorganises the forearm without reorganising the elbow's peak valgus demand. **Upshot: "workload" is not one number, and a program adopting one wearable metric has silently chosen which pitch type looks expensive.**

**🚨 The entire wearable slider literature is five pitches from one pitcher (F-491).** Agresta Table 1, read at source: only player 3 threw sliders (**5**); only players 8 and 9 threw cutters (**6** total). The "+43% forearm load on sliders" figure has **n = 1 athlete**. ⚠️ **And note the shape: the slider is ~20% of all MLB pitches and it is the pitch type with the LEAST data in every study read this cycle** — Fleisig called it inconclusive, Agresta had one thrower, the Streepy poster omits it entirely. **The pitch the field argues about hardest is the one it has measured least.**

**🚨 Qin et al. 2025 fails four checks at once (F-489).** The newest, most findable, open-access paper in this topic:
- **n = 4 pitchers**, 320 pitches analysed as 320 independent observations (H up to 254.68) — **pseudoreplication**.
- **75.5 ± 2.9 mph** fastball, described as "national-level." ⚠️ **HARD SAMPLE MISMATCH.**
- **UNIT MISLABEL, confirmed arithmetically at source:** `mph = 78.816 + 7.001·MaxPL`. At the mean Max PL of 4.217 that gives **108.34**, matching the tabled **108.33 km/h** mean, not the 67.31 mph one. **The slope is 4.35 mph/AU, not 7.0.** **Second published unit mislabel caught at source in four days** (after F-465). **The check that caught both takes thirty seconds: evaluate the equation at the sample mean and see which unit it reproduces.**
- **The R² is pitch type in disguise:** across the four type-means alone R² = 0.21, essentially the whole reported 0.192. The within-type slope — the only one that could be a lever — is never reported.
- **The discussion contradicts Table 1:** it concludes "variable-speed pitches generate greater peak external training loads" while its own table shows the **slider LOWEST of all four** (3.78; median 3.65).

**And a bonus instrumentation warning (F-492):** Agresta's Rapsodo table, indoor D1 cage — four-seam spin **1339.9 ± 956.9 rpm**, spin efficiency **58.7 ± 41.8%**. Published without comment. **Before running this corpus's own spin screens, establish your unit's own indoor dispersion on a known-stable arm.**

## 7. 🟡 PITCHING-COACH — four questions, and the translation

**Q1 to the anatomist: "If the fastball is the most expensive pitch, why does every arm injury story blame the slider?"**
*A:* Because the slider is correlated with the *pitcher*, not the *pitch*. Guys who throw a lot of sliders are late-inning arms throwing max-effort in leverage. And because peak varus torque is set at **maximum external rotation — before the hand has done anything pitch-specific.** The arm gets to the same place in cocking either way; the velocity difference is manufactured later. **So there is no mechanical reason to expect peak torque to track ball speed across pitch types at all.** That is the cleanest statement of today's result and it needs no arithmetic.

**Q2 to the biomechanist: "Your forearm study says curveballs load 13% more. Your torque studies say fastballs load more. Which do I believe?"**
*A:* Both, because they measure different things at different instants (§5). **Believe the torque literature for the elbow ligament and believe the forearm literature for the forearm musculature and the grip.** They are different tissues with different recovery timelines. ⚠️ And note the corpus already has a forearm/grip strand — F-425 (spin is friction-limited), F-432 (pinch strength falls 22–24% by pitch 100). **If the curveball is the forearm-expensive pitch, the prediction is that a curveball-heavy outing degrades SPIN faster than a fastball-heavy one. That is F-433's untested prediction with a new independent variable attached, and it is free.**

**Q3: "Should I cap breaking balls?"**
*A:* **No — not on arm-load grounds, and the number says so.** F-487: a 15-point shift is −0.43% of one pitch's elbow torque. **There is nothing to cap.** ⚠️ What you should do instead is stop treating a breaking ball as a rest pitch (§3b).

**Q4: "How would I ever know?"**
*A:* **THE SENTENCE:** *"Your breaking ball is not a rest pitch. It costs your elbow about what a fastball costs, at 12 mph less on the gun — but it doesn't cost extra either, so mix freely."*
**THE DRILL:** none. This is a planning finding with nothing a pitcher can feel.
**ON VIDEO THE FAILURE LOOKS LIKE:** a pitcher visibly dialling down for the breaking ball — slower tempo, shorter stride, a guided arm. **He is not saving his elbow; he is giving away spin and shape for nothing.**
**HOW YOU KNOW IT WORKED, WITH THE SAMPLE SIZE:** log **breaking-ball share** and **fastball velocity by pitch number**, every outing. Does the fastball fade faster in high-breaking-ball outings? **With fade ≈ 0.2 mph/inning (F-384, borrowed from an 81 mph sample) and session SD bracketed 0.3/0.5/0.8 mph (F-396, still unmeasured), the single-pitcher version needs dozens of starts — more than a season, and it is unaffordable.** Pooled across a staff (~12 arms × ~14 outings ≈ 170 outings) a **~0.15 mph/inning slope difference is reachable in one season.** **This is a staff question, not an individual one.** ⚠️ Do not run the bullpen version — F-197.

## 9. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**🔴 BIOMECHANIST → COACH: "F-486 is F-473 wearing physical units."** → **Dispute #43. PARTIALLY CONCEDED.** The coach concedes the structure and defends on two grounds: nothing is standardised (no SD import, so the assumption is about functional form and is directly testable), and the finding reports a *prediction that failed* rather than a magnitude. **The anatomist then refuses both framings and supplies a better one:** torque and ball speed are generated at *different instants*, so a velocity exponent was never the right null. **The coach accepts the reframe and F-486's usable content becomes the anatomist's sentence.** Settled by ~30 pitches in one lab session: fastballs at 100/85/70% plus curveballs at 100%, same athlete.

**🔴 BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST: "The slider's forearm acceleration is clipped."** → **Dispute #44. OPEN.** The anatomist answers that the small SD is a one-pitcher artefact (F-491), not a sensor artefact, and the biomechanist **narrows** the objection to a reporting omission: the paper never states whether any channel saturated, and the curveball's mean+2SD sits at the rail. **Unresolvable from published data.**

**🟡 COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "You have given me three 'workload' numbers that disagree. Which one goes on the whiteboard?"** → **CONCEDED BY THE BIOMECHANIST: none of them.** Trunk PlayerLoad is blind (F-488); forearm acceleration is real but is not torque (F-490); motus-class torque is the right construct but is rated for casual use. **The honest answer is that pitch count remains the best available whole-staff currency, and today's contribution is not a replacement for it — it is the demonstration that the leading candidate replacements disagree with each other about which pitch is expensive.**

**🔵 ANATOMIST → COACH: "Your recommendation is a negative. What did today actually buy?"** → **DEFENDED.** Three things: a coach can stop treating a mix change as a hidden arm-load decision (F-487, a number, not a reassurance); a coach can stop letting pitchers treat the breaking ball as a breather (F-486); and a coach running rehab or flat-ground by RPE learns his athletes are at 80% torque when he thinks they are at 75% effort (F-485). ⚠️ **The anatomist's charge is partly upheld: none of the three is a training block. This was a topic where the right answer was "stop worrying about this," and the cycle should say so rather than manufacture a prescription.**
