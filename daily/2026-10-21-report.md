## 2. The research

### 2.1 The terminal gap, documented rather than inferred — F-788

Zero weightlifting, Olympic-lift, derivative, complex or contrast trials with a throwing outcome, in
any population, at any level.

The contrast/complex half is the **stronger** gap claim, because the field's own review states it:
Petruzela, Papla & Stastny (2023), *J Hum Kinet* 87:189–200 — already in this registry at F-765 for
its composition — reports it **could not find studies with training focusing on intensity contrast
(PAPE), complex and ballistic methods**, and recommends they be tested in elite handball players.

That distinction matters. **Correction #13 falsified F-038's gap claim by re-searching.** A gap claim
sourced to somebody else's published search is harder to overturn the same way.

One partial exception: an older Spanish-language handball report of static-dynamic contrast training
beating maximal-strength-plus-peak-power for throwing velocity at **p = 0.033**, with design, n and
baseline velocity all unretrievable. **UNVERIFIED. Not imported as evidence.**

### 2.2 What the pooled evidence says, and what it is an outcome for — F-789, F-790

**ESTABLISHED (as a report of the review); SNIPPET-ONLY.** Morris SJ, Oliver JL, Pedley JS, Haff GG,
Lloyd RS (2022), *Sports Medicine* 52(7):1533–1554, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01627-2, PMID 35025093,
PMC9213388. 16 studies, 427 participants, Hedges' *g*, inverse-variance random effects.

| Comparison | Outcome | *g* | 95% CI | k | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WL vs traditional RT | load lifted | 1.35 | 0.20 – 2.51 | 4 | 0.02 |
| WL vs traditional RT | CMJ height | 0.95 | 0.04 – 1.87 | 9 | < 0.01 |
| WL vs traditional RT | linear sprint | 1.04 | −0.03 – 2.39 | 4 | 0.13 **NS** |
| WL vs traditional RT | change of direction | 1.21 | −1.41 – 3.83 | 2 | 0.36 **NS** |
| WL vs plyometrics | CMJ height | 0.31 | — | 6 | 0.09 **NS** |
| WL vs plyometrics | strength | 0.69 | — | 4 | 0.20 **NS** |

**Zero throwing outcomes.** **Population: no throwers; SAMPLE MISMATCH, directional only.**
**CAUSALITY: pooled INTERVENTION** — a better design class than most objects in this registry, which
is why the precision problem matters.

**F-790 — the interval arithmetic, derived in-cycle and source-independent.** Widths: **5.24** *g*
units (k = 2), **2.42** and **2.31** (k = 4), **1.83** (k = 9). **Width falls monotonically as k
rises, and the two largest point estimates sit on the two widest intervals.** That ordering is the
small-study-bias signature, not a dose-response.

⭐ **The load-bearing number: the only transfer outcome that reaches significance, CMJ height, has a
lower bound of *g* = 0.04.** The data are compatible with weightlifting being indistinguishable from
ordinary lifting for jumping. And *g* = 1.21 [−1.41, 3.83] cannot distinguish "substantially worse"
from "three times better than anything in sports science."

**Portable rule: at k ≤ 4 a pooled interval in a training meta-analysis is typically wider than the
effect it reports.** Fifth registry application of the implausible-magnitude / small-study screen
(F-040, F-194, F-717, F-765) — **and it has lowered the usable estimate five for five.**

### 2.3 ⭐⭐ The structural result — the chain's second step was never measured — F-791

**MARKER vs LEVER, stated plainly: nobody has manipulated weightlifting exposure and measured a
pitch. Everything below is a chain of associations.**

The NSCA position statement (Comfort P et al. 2023, *JSCR* 37(6):1163–1190, **not opened**) grounds
weightlifting's transfer claim in **biomechanical similarity to jumping and sprinting** — the hip,
knee and ankle extension shared with the pulls and the jerk drive.

For a pitcher that is a two-step chain:

1. **weightlifting → jump / sprint.** Best estimate *g* = 0.95 on CMJ height; lower bound 0.04.
2. **jump / sprint → ball velocity.** **This is the step that does not exist.**
   - CMJ **height** vs fastball velocity: **r = 0.07, n = 33 NCAA D1** (F-004, King 2025, PMID 39446825).
   - CMJ **impulse** vs velocity: r = 0.71 (F-003) — but **J = m·√(2gh)** (F-005), so the correlating
     term is body mass; F-001 restated.
   - Sprint: **zero sled or resisted-sprint trials with a throwing outcome in baseball** (F-776);
     "sprint momentum predicts ball speed" is **p = m·v** with the mass partial unreported (F-783).

Granting step 1 in full and multiplying through an r = 0.07 step 2 leaves essentially nothing at the
ball.

⚠️ **This kills the transfer ARGUMENT, not the exercise.** Honest position, identical to the one
Dispute #88 reached about sprinting one cycle ago: **keep the work for athleticism and loading
tolerance; drop the velocity claim.**

⭐ **FOURTH ARRIVAL IN NINE DAYS of the candidate sixth structural sweep flagged 2026-10-13** —
*which registered magnitudes are expressed only in a proximal test never linked to ball velocity in
an 85+ arm?* — after tendon stiffness, the rotational med-ball throw and sprint momentum.
**Recommend promoting it from candidate to standing sweep on the same grounds F-779 was promoted.**

### 2.4 ⭐⭐ The pre-post artefact, quantified by a review that prints both numbers — F-792

**ESTABLISHED for the split; SNIPPET-ONLY.** Fredriksen & van den Tillaar (2024), *Sports Medicine –
Open*, DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00785-7, PMC11550301. 17 studies, four categories, no pooled effect
sizes. **Already registered at F-782 for its taxonomy — this result is new:**

> **All resistance-band and forearm-loading studies raised throwing velocity, by roughly 3.7% to
> 26% — and only HALF found a significantly higher increase than the control group.**

**100% pre-post positive → 50% controlled positive.**

F-779's sweep had four registry instances (F-416 PAPE 0.33 → zero; F-754 med-ball +6.4% →
between-group NS; F-769 flywheel 1.19–1.33 → controlled 0.24–0.47; F-037 Escamilla's three within-arm
tests → between-arm null), all one-directional. **This is the first instance that quantifies the
survival rate, and it hands the sweep a prior: the expected shrinkage on an uncontrolled training
magnitude is roughly a coin flip between "some of it" and "none of it."** That is now the default
assumption for any uncontrolled number entering this registry.

⚠️ Note the population direction too: the review attributes the band effect to **junior and less
experienced** athletes. The effect lives in the least selected sub-population.

### 2.5 Complex and contrast training — F-793, F-794

**F-793. Cormier P, Freitas TT, Rubio-Arias JÁ, Alcaraz PE (2020),** *JSCR* 34(5):1461–1479,
DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003493. 27 articles. **SNIPPET-ONLY, and two retrieved versions disagreed
on the sprint SMD by 0.25, which is itself a reason not to quote it.**

- **1RM: described explicitly as improvements FROM BASELINE — +23.4% (complex), +23.7% (contrast).**
- Vertical jump: complex SMD 0.85 [0.45, 1.25]; contrast 0.50 [0.19, 0.81].
- Sprint: complex −0.94 [−1.33, −0.54] in one version, **−0.69 [−1.02, −0.36] in another**;
  contrast −0.51 [−0.90, −0.11].
- Change of direction: complex −0.83 [−1.08, −0.59], and the abstract states **no control group for
  contrast.**
- Conclusion: complex "potentially slightly greater." **Zero throwing outcomes.**

**The "complex beats contrast" conclusion compares two sets of within-arm pre-post effect sizes drawn
from different studies.** It is Dispute #80's defect in the wild. A +23% 1RM gain in team-sport
athletes is a training-age effect a third arm doing neither would also have got. **CAUSALITY: pooled,
but the pooled quantity is PRE-POST.**

**F-794. Xu et al. (2026),** *JSCR* 40(8), DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000005451, PMID 42223132, online
28 May 2026. **n = 45 national-level male track and field athletes, 24 weeks, three arms, 3-D force
plate — the best design in the topic.**

- **Jump height with arm swing: complex +30.9%, contrast +17.1%, control +5.2%.**
- Push-off time without arm swing: contrast −6.1%.
- Eccentric strength reported as 43.1% of jump-height variance. **Zero throwing outcomes.**

**PLAUSIBILITY FAILURE (derived).** A national-level male athlete's arm-swing CMJ is of the order of
50–60 cm. **+30.9% is +15 to +19 cm in 24 weeks in an already-selected athlete.** Outside what the
training literature supports in this population.

⭐ **And the control arm moved +5.2%. In an elite population a control group should not move; when it
does, suspect the test first** — and "jump height with arm swing" is the most coachable, most
learnable, least reliable jump variant in common use. **If the test drifted 5.2% for free, the
between-arm contrast (25.7 percentage points) inherits that uncertainty rather than escaping it.**

**EVIDENCE: the DESIGN is the best in the topic; the MAGNITUDE is UNUSABLE. Do not quote +30.9%, and
do not convert any of it to mph.** Fourth registry instance of the implausible-magnitude screen, and
the first where the control arm supplies the mechanism.

### 2.6 The derivative question is a loading question — F-795

**SNIPPET-ONLY.** Suchomel TJ, McKeever SM, Comfort P (2020), *JSCR* — **n = 27 resistance-trained
men, 10 weeks, CATCH / PULL / OL (≈ 9 per arm).** The force- and velocity-overload group produced the
greatest improvements in each examined characteristic.

**But a Comfort et al. load-matched 8-week comparison found no differences in strength, squat jump or
CMJ between catching and pulling derivatives at the same relative loads.** A 2022 follow-up (*JSSM*
21:504–516) has pulling positive, catching trivial.

**The pulling-over-catching inference is confounded with LOAD.** At ≈ 9 per arm the 2020 trial can
only detect very large effects — **the same power objection F-460 made against F-359's null, here
applied against a POSITIVE**, which should worry a coach more. **No throwing outcome in any of the
three.**

⭐ Fourth time this registry has decomposed a modality claim into a dose claim (after F-764's inertia
dial and F-711's relative load). **Programming answer: pick the derivative your athletes can perform
well at the load you want, and stop arguing about catch versus pull.**

### 2.7 🚨 The trial of Olympic lifting in throwers that never measured a throw — F-796

**SNIPPET-ONLY.** Pálinkás et al. (2025), *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 10(2):187,
DOI 10.3390/jfmk10020187, PMC12194710. **n = 27 after attrition from 13–14 per group: control 8,
heavy-load squat 7, Olympic weightlifting 12. Young female handball players, described by the authors
as less skilled.**

- Both intervention groups improved 5RM back squat and CMJ height; the control group did not.
- Theoretical maximal force ≈ unchanged (+2% each); FV imbalance −14% in the weightlifting arm only.
- **The force–velocity profile did not change significantly in either group.**
- **NO THROWING OUTCOME OF ANY KIND.**

⭐ **AUTHORS' OWN LIMITATION, AND IT IS A FAILED MANIPULATION CHECK IN PRINT:** the Olympic
weightlifting exercises were intended to be performed with maximal velocity, but **the realization of
this during the training sessions was limited by the players' technical proficiency.**

**POPULATION: SAMPLE MISMATCH twice over** (sex, and the authors' own "less skilled" description),
plus Dispute #85's unresolved 425 g → 145 g question. **Directional only, and arguably not even that.**

**Dispute #27's fourteenth venue, and its sharpest form yet: it is no longer only accuracy that goes
unmeasured but the sport's own primary skill.** Somebody randomised handball players to Olympic
lifting, tested them, published it, and never asked them to throw a ball. **This is F-782's
exercise-selection thesis in a single object.**

### 2.8 "Triple extension" / 3X — the cue, and the citation that refutes it — F-797

**VERDICT: MARKETING. CAUSALITY: NONE — nobody has manipulated drive-leg extension magnitude, order
or timing against any outcome, in any population.**

`triple extension` returned **0 hits** in 786 findings before this cycle. The claims, from
`topvelocity.net` / `.org` (vendor pages, **not opened**, and that is the weaker reason not to quote
them):

1. Triple extension = drive-leg ankle, knee and hip extension, "as your body drives itself off the
   ground when jumping."
2. A prescribed order — knee, then ankle, then hip, just before front-foot strike.
3. Drive-leg force becomes hip-to-shoulder separation, credited with most of a pitcher's velocity.
4. "The hang clean's second pull produces over four times the power output of a squat."

**The cited support for (3) is a 2024 force-plate study of 19 D1 pitchers, reported as CMJ concentric
impulse vs fastball velocity r = 0.71.**

⭐ **That study is already registered here as F-003** (Sakurai M, Qiao M, Szymanski DJ, Crotin RL
2024, *JSCR* 38(7):1288–1294, PMID 38900174) — **and F-003's own claim line reads that pelvis and
trunk pitching kinematics do NOT correlate with any CMJ variable.** That is the mechanism the cue
exists to serve. **The vendor's citation refutes the vendor's mechanism.** And the correlation that
does hold is mass (F-005; height alone r = 0.07, F-004).

⭐ **Third instance of the absolute-mass-weighted-output confound sold as a lever**, after the
rotational med-ball throw (F-751, Dispute #84) and sprint momentum (F-783). **In all three the
decisive test is the same partial correlation controlling for body mass — one regression, three
disputes.**

stronger argument against Olympic lifting on a mid-season college staff than anything in the transfer
literature, because it does not require transfer to be false.**

## 3. Translation — the coach's questions

### Q1 (to the biomechanist). "You've told me weightlifting beats ordinary lifting on jump height at *g* = 0.95. Why shouldn't I run it?"

**Answer.** Because *g* = 0.95's lower bound is **0.04**, and because the outcome is a jump. Against
**plyometrics** — cheaper, less technical, less coaching-intensive — weightlifting's jump effect is
***g* = 0.31, p = 0.09, k = 6** (F-789). And the second step of the chain, jump height to fastball,
is **r = 0.07 at n = 33 D1** (F-004). You are being offered a modality that wins a comparison you
don't care about against an option you weren't going to pick anyway.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is** *"the lift is in the program because it makes you a better
> athlete, not because it adds a mile per hour — nobody has ever measured that."*
> **The drill is** the lift you already run, unchanged.
> **On video the failure looks like** nothing — this is not a mechanics failure, it is a claim failure,
> which is exactly why it survives.
> **And here's how we know it's working:** we don't, and we can't. **53 pitchers per arm to detect
> 1 mph** (F-799). Anything you think you see on 14 arms is F-082's rising baseline.

### Q2 (to the anatomist). "Triple extension is a real movement. Why is the cue folklore?"

**Answer.** The movement is real; the **cue** has never been manipulated. Nobody has altered
drive-leg extension magnitude, order or timing and measured anything (F-797). And the vendor's own
evidence cuts the wrong way: the 2024 D1 force-plate study it cites (this registry's F-003) reports
**pelvis and trunk kinematics correlating with no CMJ variable** — which is the hip-shoulder
separation mechanism the cue is sold on. The part that correlates (impulse, r = 0.71) is **mass**
(F-005).

> **So what I tell the pitcher is** *"the number in that study that tracks velocity is how much of you
> there is, not how you jump."*
> **The drill is** no drill — this is a cue to stop giving, not a cue to install. A prescribed
> knee-then-ankle-then-hip sequence is an attentional load on a movement that happens in under 150 ms.
> **On video the failure looks like** a pitcher consciously finishing his back leg — late, slow and
> drifting off line — because he has been told to extend.
> **And here's how we know it's working:** you don't test a retired cue. You test whether removing it
> costs anything, and per F-186's pricing that is **~200 tracked pitches to see a 2-inch command
> change**, not one bullpen.

### Q3 (to both). "Fine — what do I actually do this week?"

**Answer: run the bar-velocity manipulation check (F-799).** It is the only thing in this entire topic
that is free, fast and able to come back negative.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is** *"we're not changing the lift. We're putting a sensor on the bar
> for a week, because this exercise is prescribed for speed and I want to know whether you're actually
> hitting speed. If you're not, we change the load, the exercise, or both — and that's a coaching
> problem, not a you problem."*
> **The drill is** three sessions, every working set logged, mean concentric velocity on the programmed
> derivative, nothing else altered. One transducer or a bar-velocity app. **Zero additional athlete
> time.**
> **On video the failure looks like** a pull that looks heavy and finishes slow — a grind through the
> second pull rather than a snap. **The number tells you before your eye does.**
> **And here's how we know it's working:** the check has a result either way. In band → the stimulus is
> real, and the only open question is transfer, which your roster cannot answer, so §2.3's position
> stands. Below band → **you found a program defect in one week for free, and nothing about transfer
> had to be true.** ⚠️ **Do NOT pair it with a velocity test and read the mph.**

**Sample size for the check itself:** 8–14 arms × 3 sessions × 3 sets ≈ 72–126 logged reps. That is
enough to characterise each athlete's own velocity band and his between-session spread. It is **not**
enough to compare athletes, and it is not a trial.

### Q4 (to the biomechanist). "Complex training showed +30.9% in a 24-week randomised trial. That's the design you keep asking for."

**Answer.** It is, and that is the problem. The design is right and the number is impossible:
**+30.9% of an arm-swing CMJ in national-level athletes is +15 to +19 cm.** The tell is the control
arm at **+5.2%** — an elite control group should not move, so the first hypothesis is the test, and
arm-swing jump height is the most learnable jump variant in use (F-794). **Do not quote it.** The
honest summary of complex/contrast training for a pitcher is shorter: **the only review of it reports
changes from baseline (F-793), it has no control group for one arm on one outcome, and handball's own
conditioning meta-analysis says nobody has ever tested it on a throw (F-788).**

### Q5 (to the anatomist). "Is there any version of this I should be looking for?"

**Answer — and this is the one place the anatomist's objection survives.** Every lower-body output
measure in this literature is **bilateral and vertical**. Pitching is **horizontal, rotational and
single-leg-braking** — the lead-leg block is a ~50–80 ms event (F-645). **A lower-body test that is
horizontal, rotational and unilateral, validated against ball velocity in an 85+ sample, does not
exist and nobody has proposed one.** That is the real hole, and it is Dispute #90's unresolved half.
