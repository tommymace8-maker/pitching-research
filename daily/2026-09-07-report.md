## 3. THE RESEARCH — the identity, and what survives being attacked

### 3.1 The BIOMECHANIST opens: this is computable with no data

Treat the plate appearance as a ball/strike race. $u(b,s)$ = probability of walking before striking out, with $B=4-b$ balls and $S=3-s$ strikes still needed:

$$u(b,s)=\sum_{j=0}^{S-1}\binom{B-1+j}{j}(1-q)^{B}q^{j}$$

Leverage — the plate-appearance value swing between a ball and a strike — is
$L(b,s)=[u(b+1,s)-u(b,s+1)](W-K)$.

**In the ratio $\lambda = L(b,s)/L(0\text{-}0)$, the walk and strikeout run values cancel exactly.** The whole relative structure follows from **one parameter, $q$**. (F-278)

| | 3-2 | 3-1 | 2-1 | 2-0 | 3-0 | 2-2 | 1-0 | 1-1 | 0-0 | 1-2 | 0-1 | 0-2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\lambda$ (q=.62) | **4.74** | 2.94 | 2.23 | 2.08 | 1.82 | 1.80 | 1.58 | 1.27 | **1.00** | 0.68 | 0.65 | **0.26** |

### 3.2 The three attacks I ran on it, and the one that landed

**(a) $q$ is not constant.** Pitchers fill up behind (~.78 at 3-0) and nibble ahead (~.48 at 0-2). Re-solved with a count-specific $q$: order changes, **no count moves more than 2 places**, 3-2/0-2 ratio 18.2× → 11.7×. *Survives.*

**(b) Balls in play.** Swept BIP rate .10–.24 and BIP value −0.10 to +0.10. **3-2 and 3-1 are top two in every specification; 0-2 is last in every specification.** But the middle reshuffles freely and the 3-2/0-2 ratio spans **4.9×–18.2×**. *Survives at the extremes; the middle does not.*

**(c) Aggregation — this one landed, on me.** The first pass computed pitch shares from the ball/strike-only lattice and produced the headline *"3-2 is 5.8% of pitches and 20.2% of leverage."* **That was an artifact.** A tree with no balls in play cannot end a plate appearance early: it returned **4.92 pitches/PA** against a real ~3.9 and systematically over-weighted deep counts. Re-derived with BIP (3.47 pitches/PA — now slightly *short*, so the two runs bracket the truth):

| Count | $\lambda$ | % pitches | % of total leverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0-0** | 1.00 | **28.8%** | **23.7%** |
| 3-2 | 4.74 | 3.1% | 11.9% |
| 1-0 | 1.58 | 9.1% | 11.8% |
| … | | | |
| **0-2** | 0.26 | **9.7%** | **2.1%** |

Groups: 2-strike **27.8% / 27.8%**; 3-ball **5.8% / 17.8%**; **pitcher-ahead (0-1, 0-2, 1-2) 33.8% / 15.2%**.

**The headline reversed.** (F-279) The corpus's own worked example of a reasoning error — a per-pitch truth read as an aggregate truth — happened inside this program, in the first hour of building the model, and is recorded on purpose.

### 3.3 The three readings

- **F-279 — 0-0 is the highest-*total*-leverage count in baseball.** Not because a first pitch matters much, but because there are so many of them.
- **F-280 — 0-2 is the lowest-leverage count in baseball.** Both outcomes are cheap: a strike ends it in your favour, a ball lands you in 1-2, which is still good. **The entire waste-pitch argument is a fight over about a fifth of a run per season.**
- **F-281 — "get ahead" pays at the transition, not in the destination.** Ahead counts are a third of the pitches and a seventh of the leverage. The orthodoxy survives intact — it survives *because* $\lambda(0\text{-}0)$ is high — but the habit of treating ahead-counts as the high-stakes moments does not.

## 5. WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK — the coach's translation

### The coach's four questions

**Q1 — "You've told me 3-2 is worth 4.7 first pitches. Can I do anything with that?"**
*Biomechanist:* Only via one channel, and it isn't a cue. You cannot make him better at 3-2 by telling him 3-2 matters. You can give him **more 3-2 repetitions**, which is a practice-design decision, not a mechanics one.
*Coach's translation:* **"What I tell the pitcher is** *nothing about leverage — he doesn't need the number.* **The drill is** a manufactured full-count block: catcher calls 3-2 before every pitch, 25 in a row, and the pitcher knows a ball is a walk. **On video the failure looks like** the delivery he uses on those 25 not matching his 1-1 delivery — a longer, more careful stride and a visibly slower arm are the tells. **Here's how we know it's working:** strike rate across the block, ~450 pitches per half to see 9 points (F-282)."

**Q2 — "Then why not just build the whole bullpen around 3-2?"**
*Biomechanist:* Because §4 says breadth beats targeting eight to one. A general command gain is worth ~10 runs; the best count-specific gain caps out near 1.2. **The full-count block is the right answer to "which count deserves dedicated reps," and the wrong answer to "what should most of the bullpen be."**
*Anatomist:* And note the load cost. Manufactured full counts are, by construction, maximum-intent competitive pitches with no easy ones mixed in — 25 in a block is not 25 ordinary bullpen pitches. Fold it into existing volume; do not add it on top. *(One line, per the brief; the mission is performance.)*

**Q3 — "So what do I actually stop doing?"**
*Coach:* **Stop spending meeting time on 0-2.** (F-280) Whatever he throws there, the season consequence is a fifth of a run. It is 10% of his pitches and 2% of his leverage. The staff argument about wasting versus burying is real and small.
**"What I tell the pitcher is** 'the first pitch is the one that matters — once you're ahead the next one is nearly free, so quit nibbling like it's a crisis.' **On video the failure looks like** three straight 0-2 pitches a foot off the plate with full effort. **How we know it's working:** first-pitch strike % over ~200 tracked pitches, not 0-2 chase rate."

**Q4 — "The measurement rule again, because I keep getting burned by this."**
*Biomechanist:* **F-282, and it is the most usable thing in the cycle.** A D1 starter throws ~43 full counts a season. Detecting a +10-point 3-2 gain in *game* data takes **≈17 seasons**. **The count must be manufactured to be measured.** Check strike rate in the manufactured block. **Never run value** (F-270, F-273), and never in-game 3-2 strike rate — he will not throw enough of those in his career.

### The one thing to use this week
**A 25-pitch manufactured full-count block, folded into an existing bullpen, three times a week.** Log strike rate only. Do not tell him the leverage number. Do not evaluate it before 450 pitches have accumulated on each side of the change — that is roughly six weeks. If his strike rate in the block is already at or above his overall strike rate, **he does not have a full-count problem and the block should be dropped.**

### And, conditionally: the ABS card (F-283)
If the conference runs a challenge system — **not verified this cycle** — the rule fits on an index card: **never burn a challenge on an 0-2 pitch.** The same challenge on 3-2 is worth roughly an order of magnitude more, because $L$ varies 5–18× across counts while overturn probability varies far less. The reported near-absence of pitcher challenges in MLB is a **lead worth checking, not a finding.**

## 6. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**BIOMECHANIST → COACH.** *The claim I doubt:* "manufactured full counts train the full count." **Why:** the manufactured count is not the real count. There is no runner, no crowd, no consequence, and — critically — **the hitter knows it is coming too.** You may be training a bullpen skill that does not transfer, and this corpus has a specific hole exactly here: **F-197, no bullpen-to-game command transfer study exists in baseball.** *What would settle it:* nothing available. **CONCEDED by the coach**, and it goes to Dispute #20. His position: it is the only executable design, the alternative is 17 seasons, and an untransferred practice gain is still better information than no information.

**ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST.** *The claim I doubt:* that $q$ can be treated as a property of the count. **Why:** $q$ is a *choice*, and it is a choice made against a hitter who is choosing too. A pitcher throws 78% strikes at 3-0 because he decides to. Your "one parameter" is an equilibrium outcome of a two-player game, and you have modelled it as a coin. *What would settle it:* solving the tree as a game rather than a Markov chain, with a hitter swing-decision model. **DEFENDED, partially.** The objection is correct and does not reach the conclusion: the count-varying-$q$ run in §3.2(a) is exactly this objection priced in numerically, and it moved no count more than two places. **But the anatomist's version is stronger than the version tested** — a genuine game solution could produce a $q$ vector far outside the one I assumed. **Logged in Dispute #20 as the open half.**

**COACH → BIOMECHANIST.** *The claim I doubt:* the leverage table as a table. **Why:** you built a model at 9am and by 10am it had already reversed its own headline (F-279), and the 3-2/0-2 ratio spans 4.9× to 18.2× depending on assumptions you cannot check. **A quantity that moves by a factor of four across defensible specifications is not a table row. It is a direction.** *What would settle it:* the actual count-frequency distribution and run-expectancy values — one Statcast query, currently impossible (F-277). **CONCEDED IN FULL.** The library file and F-278 both now carry the instruction in bold: **say the extremes, never the middle cells.** This is the F-269 discipline turned on the author's own work the day he produced it.

**BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST.** *The claim I doubt:* "a 25-pitch full-count block is not 25 ordinary bullpen pitches." **Why:** you asserted an intensity difference with no number. The corpus's own effort-dosing entry (F-211) says asking for 25% less effort buys only 13–19% less stress — the intensity band in bullpen work is *narrower* than coaches assume, which cuts against your claim that a competitive block is categorically different. **DEFENDED, weakly, and downgraded to one line in §5-Q2.** The anatomist's mechanism is plausible; the magnitude is unmeasured, and per the brief's own rule injury is a supporting constraint. It does not change the recommendation.
