## 2. THE BIOMECHANIST'S PASS — THE HEAT–VELOCITY CHANNEL IS CALENDAR-CONFOUNDED AT BOTH ENDS

**This is the structural result of the cycle and it kills the topic's entire public empirical basis.**

Two bodies of evidence are quoted for "temperature changes velocity." They point in opposite directions and **both are confounded by the calendar, in the direction of the claimed effect.**

**(a) The cold end.** A FanGraphs PITCHf/x study (2008–2015) reports starts at **≤40 °F averaging ~0.95 mph below the same pitchers' other games**, shrinking to ~0.6 mph at 40–50 °F and ~0.4 mph at 50–60 °F. *(SNIPPET-ONLY; `blogs.fangraphs.com` is WebFetch-blocked and was not opened.)*

The within-pitcher framing is better than almost anything in this corpus — and it does not save it. **Games at ≤40 °F in MLB are overwhelmingly April games.** April is the bottom of the season's velocity ramp. The comparison set — "the same pitchers' other games" — is mostly May through September. So the estimate contains the ramp, and the ramp has the same sign as the claimed cold penalty.

**(b) The warm end.** A FanGraphs analysis of starters 2007–2011 reports season-start to season-end velocity rising **~0.6 mph**, with the largest single step March/April → May, and the highest months **August and September**, not July. *(SNIPPET-ONLY.)* FanGraphs itself names three candidate explanations and declines to choose: ramp-up with time, warming weather, **survivorship bias**.

Survivorship is severe and under-appreciated here. Pitchers who lose velocity get optioned, shut down or relieved; the September pool is the subset that held its stuff. **The seasonal curve therefore cannot be used to extract a temperature effect in either direction, and the peak being in September — when it is cooling — is the tell.**

> **BIOMECHANIST:** Both estimates are "temperature + calendar," and in both the calendar pushes the same way as temperature. Neither is evidence for a thermal lever. There is no study in existence that estimates a temperature–velocity effect with the calendar held fixed.

### The design that removes both confounds, and what it costs

**Within-pitcher, within-calendar-month, across-game temperature contrast.** Compare a pitcher's hot starts to his own cool starts *inside the same month*. Pitcher fixed effects remove the arm; month fixed effects remove the ramp and survivorship together, because the pool is identical on both sides.

Detection cost, computed in-cycle at α = .05 two-tailed, 80% power, two arms (**the per-start velocity SD is bracketed because no start-to-start fastball-velocity SD has ever been published for an 85+ arm — F-586's input is an assumption this corpus carries, not a measurement**):

| per-start SD | Δ = 0.30 mph | Δ = 0.50 mph | Δ = 0.95 mph |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.8 mph | 223 starts | 80 starts | **22 starts** |
| 1.0 mph | 349 starts | 126 starts | **35 starts** |
| 1.2 mph | 502 starts | 181 starts | **50 starts** |

**Re-estimating the headline cold claim cleanly needs 22–50 starts.** MLB produces ~4,860 starts a season and publishes game temperature. **The clean test has not been run because nobody asked, not because the data is short.** Even the hardest cell — 0.3 mph at SD 1.2 — is ~500 starts, i.e. two months of one season.

⚠️ **A college program cannot run this.** A full staff produces ~60–75 starts a season and the within-month constraint cuts that to a handful per cell. **This is an MLB-public-data analysis. Do not try it on your own roster and do not believe anyone who says they did.**

## 3. THE ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS — AND THE RESULT OF THE DAY

### 3.1 The threshold ladder, and where a starting pitcher sits on it

Passive-heating work gives an ordered set of core-temperature thresholds *(SNIPPET-ONLY; tandfonline 10.3109/02656736.2010.519371 and PubMed 37678507 were NOT opened)*:

- **~37.8 °C** — voluntary force production begins to fall
- **~38.3 °C** — motor cortical excitability begins to drop
- **~38.5 °C** — suppressed cortical motor drive during high-intensity tasks (exertional heating to 39.5 °C)
- **~38.8 °C** — complex cognitive performance declines, alongside neuromuscular function

> **ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY:** Note what the dose is in every one of those studies — **continuous exercise or continuous passive heating.** A starting pitcher throws roughly 15–20 minutes of actual work spread across two hours, with a passive half-inning between every work bout and a dugout he sits in. **Metabolically, the starter is the lowest-strain position on the field.** The catcher is the highest; the position-player left fielder is higher than the pitcher. The honest prior is that a starter's core temperature in a hot game lands somewhere near the bottom of that ladder, not the top — and nobody has ever measured it, in any game, at any level.

That prior is not a dismissal. It is the **F-631/F-650 rule in a thermal form**: *ask what the sample's actual exposure dose was before importing its magnitude.* The heat literature's dose is continuous work. A starter's is intermittent with long passive recovery. Magnitudes do not port across that gap.

### 3.2 ⭐⭐ THE ONE MEASURED ACCURACY DECREMENT IN THE ENTIRE HEAT LITERATURE WAS MEASURED ON AN EXTERNALLY PACED TASK

This is the cycle's genuinely new idea and it cuts against the folklore, not for it.

**First, the exception worth noting.** For most of six weeks this corpus has logged "zero accuracy outcomes" in topic after topic (Dispute #27 — the running tally in `library/open-disputes.md` reached its **TWELFTH** venue on 2026-10-10; `FINDINGS.md`'s inline numbering stopped at the **EIGHTH**, F-518). **Heat is not another one.** The heat literature *does* contain a measured accuracy decrement, and it is a large one:

**Wittbrodt et al. 2018, *Physiological Reports*, doi 10.14814/phy2.13805.** n = **13** healthy adults (23.6 ± 4.2 y), counterbalanced 150-min trials at **45 °C / 15% RH**, with water replacement vs without (**~2.8–3.0% body-mass loss**) vs seated rest control. Visuomotor task accuracy **69.7 ± 13.5%** (heat + dehydration) vs **85.1 ± 7.0%** (control), **P < 0.01**; 6–8% slower response time; more commission and omission errors. *(SNIPPET-ONLY, through two review paraphrases — the paper was NOT opened. A review table lists n = 12 against the abstract's 13, an unresolved discrepancy. Participants were not blinded to hydration status.)*

**Now the feature that matters.** The task was a **22-minute PACED visuomotor task.** Externally timed. The participant responds when the machine says.

And that is precisely the distinction the hyperthermia neurophysiology literature draws. *(SNIPPET-ONLY; PMC12321350 and PMC13232702 were NOT opened.)* A core-temperature rise of ~1.5 °C **did not change brain activity during self-initiated movement**, but **did** change it during **externally triggered** movement; a companion review states that voluntary self-paced movements are unaffected while externally triggered movements requiring prefrontal control are impaired.

> **ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY:** **A pitch is the most self-initiated movement in sport.** Nothing cues it. The pitcher comes set and goes when he decides to go. If the self-initiated / externally-cued dissociation is real, then the single largest accuracy decrement in the heat literature was measured on a task class the pitcher **does not perform** — and the folklore sentence "heat fogs your command" has no mechanism behind it.

### 3.3 ⭐⭐ EXCEPT THAT THE PITCH CLOCK CONVERTS THE PITCH INTO THE OTHER CLASS

**And this is the part nobody has stated.**

A pitch used to be purely self-paced. Under a pitch timer — which NCAA baseball and MLB both now run — it is not. A pitcher working at 3 seconds remaining is still self-initiating. A pitcher working at 0.5 seconds remaining is being **externally cued by a clock**, under time pressure, with a called-strike penalty for failing.

If §3.2's dissociation holds, the pitch clock **moves the pitcher's task from the heat-resistant class into the heat-vulnerable one** — and it does so *selectively*, only for the pitchers who work close to the limit.

**That is a falsifiable prediction, and it is testable on public data today, for free:**

- **Statcast publishes a Pitch Tempo leaderboard** (F-334 — with the caveat F-334 already registered, that its definition excludes most of a pitcher's pitches).
- Game temperature is published.
- Split pitchers by tempo pressure (how near the limit they work). Split games by temperature. Look at late-inning location SD or called-strike rate.
- **Prediction:** the heat × command interaction is concentrated in the **high-tempo-pressure** group and is near zero in the fast-working group. A main effect of heat with no tempo interaction **falsifies** the mechanism.

> **BIOMECHANIST:** I will sign the design and not the mechanism. The prediction is specific, pre-registered here, and cheap — that is the right shape. But it rests on **two snippet-level studies in non-throwers** and the interaction could be produced by three other things (fatigue accumulating faster in heat, hurried mechanics, dehydration). It is a **hypothesis with a free test**, and it goes in the vault as `MECHANISM`, not as a finding about pitchers.

## 4. THE OTHER CHANNELS — ALL THREE LAND IN OUTCOME CLASSES THE VAULT ALREADY FORBIDS

**(a) Cooling (pre-cooling and per-cooling).** Bongers et al. 2015, *BJSM* — 28 studies, overall effect on exercise performance **+6.7 ± 0.9%, ES 0.43**. Hohenauer et al. 2018 — 29 studies, external pre-cooling vs no cooling on **peak power output**, Hedges' **g = 0.49 [0.33, 0.64]**. *(Both SNIPPET-ONLY.)* Outcomes are cycling and running endurance and lower-body peak power.

**F-004 forbids the conversion.** Jump height does not predict fastball velocity (r = 0.07, NS). A percentage of lower-body power **cannot** be turned into mph by this corpus or anyone else. **A cooling vest's 6.7% is not 6.7% of anything a pitcher sells.**

**(b) Heat acclimation.** Chalmers et al. 2014, *Sports Medicine* (PMID 24817609), systematic review, 8 studies: **peak anaerobic power efforts have not been demonstrated to improve** after short-term heat acclimation; aerobic tests did benefit; authors flag small evidence base and varying risk of bias. A separate trial found peak power unchanged in cool conditions after heat training; another reported ~2% peak power after ~10 days. *(All SNIPPET-ONLY.)*

> **ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY:** This is the cleanest negative in the topic and it is mechanistically unsurprising. Heat acclimation's adaptations are **plasma volume expansion, earlier and higher sweat rate, lower core temperature at a given workload** — cardiovascular and thermoregulatory. **None of them is a rate-of-force-development adaptation.** There is no channel from plasma volume to a ballistic arm action. Heat acclimation is a tolerance intervention, not a performance intervention, and for a sport whose outcome is peak power it is the wrong tool.

**(c) Hypohydration.** The evidence near 2% is genuinely mixed and should be reported that way:
- ~2.4% body-mass loss, n = 30, randomized: **prefrontal haemodynamics altered, motor learning NOT impaired** (Springer, doi 10.1007/s00221-022-06424-5; SNIPPET-ONLY).
- Low-to-moderate hypohydration, n = 10 crossover: **no significant cognitive decline** except Wisconsin card-sort accuracy (PMC8912814; SNIPPET-ONLY).
- GSSI review: decrements "more likely when hypohydration levels are > 2% body mass loss, but there is significant inter-individual variability."
- The clear decrement is Wittbrodt's **2.8–3.0% with heat stress added** — §3.2.

**The 2% number is a soft threshold borrowed from team sports, and the clean motor-learning test at 2.4% came back null.** F-395's warning stands and is now sharper.

## 5. THE 2% GUARDRAIL IS NEARLY UNREACHABLE FOR A STARTER — UNLESS HE DOESN'T DRINK

Derived in-cycle. **90 kg arm, 2% = 1.80 L deficit. Exposure 2.5 h (≈25 min pre-game bullpen + ≈2.0 h outing and dugout).** Net deficit as % body mass:

| sweat rate | drink 0.5 L | drink 1.0 L | drink 1.5 L | drink 2.0 L |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0.59 L/h** (vendor MLB spring-training figure) | 1.08% | 0.53% | −0.03% | −0.58% |
| **0.83 L/h** (GSSI pooled baseball, n = 1,303) | 1.75% | 1.19% | 0.64% | 0.08% |
| **1.20 L/h** (heat-elevated bracket) | **2.78%** | **2.22%** | 1.67% | 1.11% |
| **1.50 L/h** (heat-elevated bracket) | **3.61%** | **3.06%** | **2.50%** | 1.94% |
| **2.00 L/h** (upper bracket) | **5.00%** | **4.44%** | **3.89%** | **3.33%** |

⚠️ **The sweat-rate inputs are the weakest numbers in this brief.** 0.83 L/h comes from a **commercial blog's summary** of a GSSI pooled dataset and the original was not opened; 0.59 L/h comes from a **biosensor vendor's marketing page** (which also claims 806 mg electrolytes/h and that outfielders lose 20% more fluid than infielders — unverifiable). **Neither was measured in heat and neither separates pitchers from position players.** The 1.2–2.0 L/h rows are a bracket imported from intermittent team sport, not a baseball measurement.

**What the table says anyway, and it is robust across the bracket:**

1. **At pooled baseball sweat rates, a starter cannot reach 2% in one outing at any realistic drinking volume.** Even half a liter keeps him under.
2. **In genuine heat he blows through it even drinking two liters.** The row that flips the table is the sweat rate, and it is exactly the row nobody has measured in a baseball player in heat.
3. **The binding variable is drinking behaviour, not sweat rate** — and **a starting pitcher's actual in-game fluid intake has never been published, at any level.** He is the one athlete on the field with a private, unobserved two-hour window and a cooler ten feet away.
4. ⭐ **The within-game risk is therefore mostly an artifact of attention. The real exposure is cumulative** — the pitcher who shows up to a Friday start already 1% down from Thursday's lift in May starts the game at the top of the table, not the bottom.

## 7. STEP 3 — THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS, ANSWERED AND TRANSLATED

### Q1 (coach → biomechanist): "My guys *swear* they throw harder in July. Is that real?"

**Answer:** Unknown, and the number you have heard is not evidence. The +0.6 mph seasonal rise is three effects braided together — training ramp, warming weather, survivorship — and FanGraphs itself declined to separate them. The highest month in that sample was **September**, which is cooling. **Nobody has ever held the calendar fixed.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"You do throw harder in July than in February, and it's mostly because it's July — five months of throwing, not the thermometer. We can't separate those yet and nobody can."*
> **The drill is:** none. This is a thing not to coach.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing on video — this one fails on a spreadsheet, as a coach attributing a training adaptation to the weather and then easing off in the cold.
> **How we know it's working:** we don't chase this. If someone claims the clean estimate, ask one question: *did you hold the calendar month fixed?* If not, they measured the season, not the heat.

### Q2 (coach → anatomist): "Is the cooling vest worth a roster spot?"

**Answer:** Not on the evidence available. Its best result is **+6.7% on endurance and g = 0.49 on lower-body peak power**, and **F-004 forbids converting lower-body power into mph** (jump height vs velocity r = 0.07, NS). There is no measured channel from a cooling vest to a fastball.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The vest is a comfort tool. Use it if it makes the dugout tolerable. It is not a performance intervention and I'm not going to pretend it is."*

> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing. This fails as a budget line.
> **How we know it's working:** you can't know, at your sample size — see Q4.

### Q3 (coach → both): "What about hydration? Everyone says 2%."

**Answer:** The 2% number is borrowed from team sports, it is soft, and the cleanest motor test at **2.4%** came back **null**. The clear decrement needed **2.8–3.0% plus 45 °C heat stress in n = 13 on an externally paced task**. **And at pooled baseball sweat rates a starter cannot reach 2% in a single outing if he drinks anything at all.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The game isn't where you get dehydrated — the week is. Show up to Friday already topped up. If you drink a bottle every other half-inning you are not going to hit the number people are warning you about."*
> **The drill is:** **the scale, pre- and post-outing, with bottles logged** — see §8.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing on video. It looks like an empty bottle rack after a 92-degree Saturday.
> **How we know it's working:** when the post-outing deficits stop landing above 2%, and more to the point, when you finally *have* the number, because nobody in baseball does.

### Q4 (coach → biomechanist): "Could I just test this on my own staff this spring?"

**Answer: No, and this is the most useful thing in the brief.** Detecting a 0.5 mph temperature effect needs **80–181 starts** inside one calendar month. A full college staff produces **60–75 starts a season, all months combined.** You are short by a factor of three to ten before the within-month constraint even bites.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"We are not going to learn anything about weather from our own radar gun. Anyone who tells you their program figured out the heat effect is reading noise."*

> **How we know it's working:** when nobody on your staff quotes a within-program weather effect again.

## 8. WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK WITH AN 85+ ARM

**Weigh the starter in and out, and log what he drinks.** Nude or same-clothing weight 20 minutes before the pre-game bullpen; same scale, same protocol, within 10 minutes of coming out. Log every bottle he takes, in milliliters.

- **Cost:** a scale the program already owns (F-630's morning-weight protocol already put one in the pitchers' locker room) and one manager with a clipboard.
- **What it buys:** his **sweat rate in his actual conditions** and his **actual deficit** — two numbers that **do not exist in the published literature for any pitcher at any level**, and which this brief had to bracket across a 3.4-fold range because of it.
- **The decision it supports:** if he comes off at >2%, the fix is behavioural and immediate (more bottles, start earlier). If he comes off at 0.6%, the hydration conversation is over for the season and you stop spending attention on it.
- **How he'd know it worked:** ⚠️ **the honest version — it does not need a performance outcome and must not be sold as one.** This is a measurement that replaces a guess, not an intervention. **Six to eight outings** gives a stable per-athlete sweat rate. Do **not** regress velocity on it: with a per-start SD near 1 mph you would need the start counts in §2's table, which a program does not have.

⚠️ **What NOT to do this week:** buy cooling vests for pitchers, start a heat-acclimation block, or change anything based on the thermometer. The first two have no demonstrated channel to a ballistic outcome; the third has no clean estimate in existence.

## 9. STEP 4 — CROSS-EXAMINATION

### Challenge 1 — BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST: *"The self-initiated / externally-cued dissociation is two snippet-level studies in non-throwers and you have built the headline on it."*

**Claim challenged:** that hyperthermia spares self-paced movement and impairs externally cued movement, therefore spares pitching.
**Why I doubt it:** n is unknown in both, neither was opened, the "voluntary movements are unaffected" line comes from a **review's** characterization, and the measured outcome is **brain activity**, not performance. A movement-preparation EEG difference is not a command decrement.
**What would settle it:** one study with a *performance* outcome on a self-paced versus externally paced accuracy task under matched hyperthermia. Darts self-paced vs darts on a 3-second clock, n = 30, core temperature clamped.

**ANATOMIST'S RESPONSE — CONCEDED, in part, and the finding is re-graded.** The mechanism is registered as `MECHANISM`, confidence **low-medium**, and the brief's language is changed from "therefore spares pitching" to "therefore the one measured decrement does not obviously transfer." **The claim I defend is narrower and survives: the single largest accuracy decrement in the heat literature was measured on a paced task, and that is a property of the measurement, not an inference.** Whether a pitch is in the protected class is open — and it is **Dispute #69**.

### Challenge 2 — COACH → BIOMECHANIST: *"You've used calendar confounding to delete both estimates. Isn't 'everything is confounded, run nothing' just a way of never saying anything?"*

**Claim challenged:** §2's dismissal of both the cold and warm evidence.
**Why I doubt it:** every field estimate in baseball is confounded by something. If that standard retires findings, the corpus retires everything. And pitchers really do report that cold hands change the ball.

**BIOMECHANIST'S RESPONSE — DEFENDED, with one concession.** The concession: the **finger/hand sensation** channel at genuinely cold temperatures is a separate mechanism from the velocity estimate, it is not calendar-confounded, and it connects to **F-431/F-432** (friction, normal force, spin) rather than to velocity — the corpus should not have let §2 shadow it.

The defense: this is **not** a general objection to confounding. It is the specific observation that **the confound and the claimed effect have the same sign in both directions**, which is the condition under which an estimate carries no information at all. And critically, **I did not stop at deletion — I priced the fix at 22–50 starts on free public data.** That is the opposite of "run nothing." A finding this corpus can kill *and* replace for the cost of one Statcast query is a good day.

### Challenge 3 — ANATOMIST → COACH: *"Your 'what to do this week' is a measurement with no outcome. By this corpus's own standards, is that a recommendation at all?"*

**Claim challenged:** §8's weigh-in protocol.
**Why I doubt it:** the program's rule is that every recommendation needs a measurable check with the sample size to detect it. §8 explicitly refuses to attach a performance outcome. That looks like the rule being waived for a recommendation the coach liked.

**COACH'S RESPONSE — DEFENDED, and the distinction is worth adding to the house style.** There are two kinds of recommendation and this corpus has only ever formalized one. An **intervention** changes the athlete and owes a powered outcome. A **measurement** changes what the coach knows and owes only **reliability** — how many observations until the number is stable. §8 is the second kind, it is labeled as such, and it carries its reliability figure (6–8 outings).

The hazard the challenge is really pointing at is real and is accepted: **a measurement with no outcome becomes an intervention the moment someone acts on it.** That is why §8 states the decision rule *in advance* (>2% → behavioural fix; <1% → stop spending attention) and why it explicitly forbids regressing velocity on the result. **Registered as a standing distinction, not just a defense of today's item.**

## 10. REVISITING CARRIED-FORWARD DISPUTES — DID TODAY MOVE ANYTHING?
