## Headline

**Six verified corrections landed today, four of which reverse a conclusion. Those come first, because a correction to a recommendation the program is already coaching off is worth more than any new idea.**

1. **Stride length — the #2 recommendation — has been experimentally manipulated by two independent groups and neither found a gain.** One found the pitcher's *own* stride was significantly faster than a lengthened one. **A long stride is a marker of a hard thrower, not a lever that makes one.** (§4)
2. **The Driveline ≥88 mph figure is +0.65 mph, not +1.35.** The population-matched ceiling is **harsher** than the library said. (§2, §9)
3. **The changeup finding was INVERTED in the library.** Velocity *separation* is Rosen's main argued mechanism; what has no relationship to whiffs is *absolute* changeup velocity. (§9)
4. **"No controlled training study at 85+" was too strong.** Two clear the floor — including **the first positive controlled velocity result in a sample above 85 mph**, a Korean 12-week rotational/med-ball trial. (§9, §10)
5. **The MLB 1,163 figure is pitcher × pitch-type × season pairs, not pitcher-seasons, and its mean is internally inconsistent.** (§9)
6. **⚠️ Recommendation #1's evidence is not a "removal experiment."** Gdovin 2025 is *"Limiting Access to Resistance Training Equipment During the Off-Season"* — **an uncontrolled 8-week pre-post with NO control group**, and its mph magnitude is paywalled and unread. This confirms **Dispute #14**, which I argued on design grounds *before* knowing the design. (§9)

**And a finding about the research process itself, which I think is the most important thing in this document: none of the six was a fabrication.** Every one was a real source, read slightly wrong — and **all six erred in the same direction, toward more confidence than the source supported.** (§11) **Five further items were never reached by verification at all and are logged as outstanding in §9b — the library is not fully vetted.**

## 2. THE HARD PROBLEM — an 88 mph arm who wants 93

### 2.1 What I actually do

The evidence base does not cover him. It never has. So I stop pretending it does and I do three things instead.

**First, I give him the base rate on day one, out loud, before he pays me anything.**

In the best population match that exists — **58 high-level athletes entering at ≥88 mph**, mean age 23, mean 67 days of training at the best-resourced facility in the sport (Driveline, 4 Apr 2019) — the group went **89.6 → 90.3 mph. Mean gain +0.65 mph.** **41.95% gained ≥1 mph. 18.39% LOST more than 1 mph.** At the major-league level, only **12.1% of pitcher × pitch-type season pairs gain ≥1 mph and 2.5% gain ≥2 mph** (MLB Data Warehouse 2024, n = 1,163).

> ### ⚠️ CORRECTED 2026-08-13 — and the correction makes this HARDER, not easier.
> The library carried **+1.35 mph** for this cohort. **The real figure is +0.65 mph**, verified two ways against the article: its summary table (89.6 → 90.3) and its own binned distribution (which reconstructs to +0.63). **The best-resourced program in the sport moved 88+ mph arms about two-thirds of a mile an hour**, over roughly nine weeks, with nearly one in five going backwards.
>
> **This is the single most important number I will say to an 88 mph athlete, and I was about to say it wrong by a factor of two.** Any facility quoting "3–5 mph" to an 88 mph arm is off by five- to eight-fold against the best-matched public evidence that exists.

**88 to 93 is a five-mph ask. Against a population-matched mean of two-thirds of a mile an hour per block, that is not a season's project — it is a multi-year one, or it does not happen at all.** I say that in the first meeting.

**Second, I classify him before I program anything — and I classify him more than once.**

The velocity report's §6.7 procedure is right: force-plate test, compute predicted-minus-actual, and sort into engine-limited (actual > predicted) versus delivery-limited (actual < predicted).

**My addition, and it matters: the prediction carries a ±2.7 mph error bar, which is larger than a realistic annual gain. A single test cannot classify anybody.** I want three separate test sessions across 4–6 weeks before I believe a classification. If his predicted-minus-actual gap flips sign across three tests, he is not classifiable and I default to the delivery-limited program because it is cheaper and faster.

Three buckets, three different programs:

| Bucket | Signature | Program | Clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Engine-limited** | Tests below his velocity, consistently | Mass, absolute power, reactive strength | 12–24 months |
| **Delivery-limited** | Tests above his velocity | Mechanics, efficiency, underload throwing | 3–6 months |
| **Ceiling-limited** | Tests *at* his velocity, already long-striding, 25+, short levers | **The mph are not coming.** Arsenal, extension, slot, command | Now |

**The third bucket is real and I will name it.** Roughly 18% of the population-matched cohort *lost* velocity. Some of those men were not undertrained.

**Third, here is the actual program, in priority order, with an honesty label on every line.**

1. **Lift, year-round, absolute loads.** [Best-evidenced practice with the weakest causal claim — see §5, V2.]
2. **Add lean mass if he has frame for it.** Body mass r = 0.58, lean mass r = 0.52 — the strongest single physical correlates of velocity in the literature. **Zero intervention studies.** I say that out loud: *"this is the best bet anyone has and nobody has ever tested it."*
3. **Reactive strength / stiffness.** Drop jump R² = 0.30 in 53 professionals, the one jump quality that survives into a pro sample.
4. **Check the two "free" mechanical levers — stride %BH and pelvis at foot contact.** [Downgraded today. See §4.]
5. **Underload throwing, 3–5 oz, 6–10 weeks, radar-gated.** Best-supported implement claim; the mechanism (arm speed without measured kinetic penalty) and the outcome data both point at underload, not overload.
6. **Extension.** [See §3, Q10 — the cheapest "mph" in the document.]

### 2.2 What I say to him

This is the script, and I would say it in these words:

> "Here's the truth. Every velocity number you have ever been quoted was measured on somebody slower than you. There is not one published training study where the average guy in the room threw as hard as you already throw. Not one. So anybody who quotes you a number is extrapolating from fifteen-year-olds.
>
> What I *can* tell you is what happened to fifty-eight high-level arms who walked in at 88 or better, at the best facility in the sport, over about nine weeks. As a group they went from 89.6 to 90.3. **That's seven-tenths of a mile an hour.** Four in ten gained a full mile. **Nearly two in ten went the other way.** That's with everything working.
>
> So I'm not going to promise you 93. Here's what I'll promise instead. In twelve months, you and I will both know whether you're an engine problem or a delivery problem — that's the thing nobody bothers to find out, and it's the whole game. I'll give you the three things with the best evidence behind them, and I'll tell you which ones are guesses, because two of them are.
>
> And I'm going to spend the other half of your time somewhere else. Because if you get to 90 with a changeup that misses bats and command that's inside seven inches, you are a better pitcher than the guy who got to 93 and doesn't have those. That's not a consolation prize. That's the actual job."

> **⚠ CORRECTED 2026-08-17 —** The script's second sentence — *"There is not one published training study where the average guy in the room threw as hard as you already throw. Not one."* — **is too strong and should not be said to an athlete in these words.** Two controlled studies clear the 85 mph floor: **Ake 2016** (n = 56 NCAA D1 SEC, baseline **87.25 / 86.80 mph**, still null) and **Lee, Choi & Jeon 2026** (n = 29 Korean collegiate, baseline max **86.2 mph**, the first positive controlled velocity result above 85). The honest version is narrower and still does the same work: *there are two, both small, one null and one not randomized, and every large gain you have been quoted came from a sample 10–20 mph slower than you.* What remains genuinely zero: **any controlled trial in professional pitchers with velocity as an outcome.** The 89.6 → 90.3 / +0.7 mph figures in the rest of this script are correct. See `library/velocity-development.md` §1 and F-226 in FINDINGS.md.

**The mechanism that keeps belief alive: replace the velocity promise with a diagnostic promise and a portfolio.**

Belief does not die because you told an athlete the truth. Belief dies when he is promised five miles an hour, gets one, and has to choose between "I'm broken" and "you lied." **Both of those conclusions end the relationship and one of them ends his career.** The 18% loss rate must be said on day one, because if you say it after he loses, you are a fraud, and he will be right about that.

**Second thing I give him, and it is not a consolation prize:** roughly **40% of professional pitchers hold their velocity essentially flat from age 21 to 38** — losing only ~0.3 mph total (Petti & Zimmerman, FanGraphs). The default trajectory at this level is *down*: starters begin losing velocity in their mid-20s and shed ~1 mph/yr from 29. **Holding 88 for six more years puts him in the top two-fifths of professional pitchers at the thing that ends most careers.** That is a real, trainable, career-length outcome, and almost nobody frames it as a target.

### 2.3 "Keep lifting" as floor protection — is that coachable?

**No. Not as written, and I won't say it.**

Two problems. The first is motivational: *"this won't make you better, it'll stop you getting worse"* is the least compelling sentence available to a coach, and it will not survive contact with a 20-year-old at 6 a.m. The second is that I think the asymmetry it rests on is partly a study-design artifact (§5, V2).

**The translation I'd actually use:**

> **"Lifting doesn't buy mph. Lifting is what lets everything else buy mph."**

It is the substrate, not the intervention. And then I give him the honest correlational case, which is genuinely strong and genuinely motivating because it makes him a participant in an open question rather than a recipient of a hedge:

> *"Mass and absolute power are the two things that separate a 91 from a 95 in literally every cross-sectional study ever run — body mass correlates at .58, lean mass at .52, jump impulse at .71. What nobody has ever done is take a guy your speed, move those numbers, and see if his velocity moved. So we're going to measure both, in you, every eight weeks, for two years. You'll be the n."*

**Measurable check, with the realism required:** absolute IMTP net peak force, squat-jump absolute peak power in watts, drop-jump RSI, and lean mass by DEXA or BIA on the same device — every 8 weeks. Plot against his own release velocity.

**But the honest constraint, stated so nobody cheats:** the entire force-plate battery predicts velocity at R² = 0.54 with **±2.7 mph mean absolute error**, which is larger than a realistic annual gain. **Therefore you never, ever validate a strength block against a predicted velocity.** You validate a strength block against the strength quality. Velocity is a separate, slower, noisier readout on its own clock.

### 2.4 Stride length — how would you lengthen it, and how would you know it worked?

**This question is now harder than it was this morning, because the evidence turned against the premise.**

**What the manipulation studies say (full citations in §4):** three separate experimental manipulations of stride length exist. Velocity did not improve in any of them. In the one that used a modest ±10% change, the shorter stride was 0.8 mph *faster*, p < .001.

**What that kills:** the naive version — *"stride further and you'll throw harder."* Dead. That is exactly what those studies did, and it did nothing or went backwards.

**What it does not kill:** the possibility that an athlete who is short-striding *because of a constraint* will stride longer, and throw harder, once the constraint is removed. **An acute forced manipulation of a habituated stride is a disruption experiment, not a development experiment.** Telling a man to over-stride 25% in an 80-pitch simulated game tests his ability to absorb an insult; it does not test whether a longer stride, owned and integrated over twelve weeks, is worth anything.

**So how I would actually lengthen it:**

**Not by cueing it.** Never by cueing it. That is the experiment that failed, three times.

By **removing the constraint** and letting the longer stride emerge as the athlete's own solution:

| Candidate constraint | Why | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| **Drive-leg ankle dorsiflexion** | The push starts at the back ankle; restricted dorsiflexion caps how far the COM travels before the foot has to come down | **A 4-week band-mobility + balance intervention on D1 pitchers was run and defended at Nevada/Reno in spring 2026 with pre/post stride length. Results not published.** Get the thesis |
| **Incoming momentum ("body drift")** | COM velocity *before* peak knee height is the only COM window associated with velocity in 332 in-game D1 pitchers (Zappa 2026) — max COM velocity per se is not | [EMERGING] |
| **Trail-leg eccentric strength** | Determines whether he can hold a longer position rather than falling into it | [Mechanism only] |
| **Lead-hip abduction/adduction capacity** | Caps how far the lead leg can reach without the pelvis compensating | [Mechanism only] |

**Constraint drills, not cues:** step-back and walking-windup throws (raise incoming momentum so a longer stride appears as the *solution* to a task, not as an instruction); mound-to-flat contrast; downhill/uphill.

**And one finding that reframes the whole thing:** Crotin's own data shows the *shorter* stride cut mean heart rate by 11.1 bpm across an 80-pitch simulated game, with no velocity cost. **For a starter, stride length may be a stamina lever, not a velocity lever.** That is a genuinely useful, genuinely counterintuitive thing I did not know this morning.

### 2.5 How would I know it worked — rather than just moved his release point?

**This is the sharp half of the question and it has a clean answer.**

A longer stride does three things simultaneously: it lengthens stride, it increases extension, and it lowers release height. All three change *the pitch* without changing *the arm*.

**The discriminating measurement is: report RELEASE velocity, never perceived/effective velocity.**

Extension is baked into `effective_speed` by construction — `PV = release velocity × 54.17 / (60.5 − extension)`. If you evaluate a stride intervention on perceived velocity, you will "detect" a gain that is pure geometry. **Release velocity is unaffected by extension. It is the only clean outcome.**

Then track all three at once and partial them:

| Measure | Instrument | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Stride length, %BH** | Fixed 240 fps camera, calibrated reference in frame, same landmark, ≥10 pitches averaged | Did the thing you targeted actually move? |
| **Release velocity** | One radar, never mixed | Did arm speed move? |
| **Extension** | Trackman/Hawk-Eye | Did the geometry move? |
| **Release height** | Same | Did his VAA change as a side effect? |

**If release velocity is flat and extension rose, you moved his release point. Say so. That is not nothing — it is worth ~1.55 mph/ft of perceived velocity at 85 mph — but it is not what you claimed.**

**⚠ The measurement-error trap, and it is severe.** Single-camera markerless stride-length error is **5.75% of body height** (pitchAI validation) — and a **10% BH stride change is what's worth 2 mph.** *The instrument error is more than half the effect.* So: fixed camera position, calibrated reference, same operator, and **establish his own test-retest error before you claim a change.** If you cannot measure his stride to better than 2% BH repeatably, you cannot run this project.

**Sample size for the velocity outcome — the arithmetic, stated the way the command report states its 200-pitch number.**

Assume a between-session mean-fastball-velocity SD of ~1.0 mph *(this is an assumption — measure yours; a starter's session-to-session variation is what you need, not his within-outing SD)*. Paired, α = .05, 80% power:

- **1.0 mph change:** d = 1.0 → n ≈ (1.96 + 0.84)² / 1.0² ≈ **8 separately-dated sessions per condition**, ≥25 fastballs each.
- **2.0 mph change:** d = 2.0 → n ≈ **2–3 sessions.**
- **0.5 mph change:** d = 0.5 → n ≈ **31 sessions.** *You cannot detect half a mile an hour in a season. Stop trying.*

**The consequence nobody wants:** a velocity intervention needs roughly **eight dated sessions on one gun** before a 1 mph claim is anything but noise. That is the entire fall. And it is still cheaper than the command equivalent (~200 tracked pitches for 2 inches), which is why velocity development has commercially outrun command development — the same measurement-convenience artifact the stuff report names in its Finding 4.

## 4. THE STRIDE-LENGTH FINDING, IN FULL

Because this is the day's most consequential item and it contradicts a headline library recommendation.

**What the library currently says.** `velocity-development.md` §7 ranks stride length as recommendation **#2** — *"the best mechanical lever"* — on **Manzi JE**, Dowling B, et al. 2021 (*J Sports Sci* 39(23):2658–2664, PMID 34240663, n = 315 professional — ⚠️ *first author corrected 2026-08-13; the library had it as "Dowling 2021"*): **+0.9 m/s (+2.0 mph) per 10% of body height, no significant difference in elbow varus torque across stride-length quartiles.** Its Open Question #8 states: *"Nobody has lengthened a professional's stride and measured what happened to his velocity."*

**What the verified record actually shows — TWO independent experiments, plus one grey-literature third.**

| # | Study | Design | n / level | Manipulation | Velocity result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Ramsey DK, Crotin RL, White S (2014).** *Effect of stride length on overarm throwing delivery: a linear momentum response.* ***Hum Mov Sci* 38:185–196.** [PMID 25457417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25457417/) | Randomized crossover, 8-camera mocap 240 Hz, 2 force plates, radar; two 80-pitch simulated games, 72 h washout | 19 (15 collegiate, 4 elite HS) ⚑ ~73–81 mph | **±25% of self-selected stride** (differed p ≤ .001) | **Hand and ball velocity equivalent across all conditions** |
| **2** | **Matsuda T, Hirano A, Umakoshi Y, Kimura A (2025).** ***Front Sports Act Living*.** [PMID 40264933](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40264933/) | Instructed stride conditions | 20 college ⚑ | **±20%** | **Under 72.7 · NORMAL 75.8 · Over 72.7 mph. His own stride was significantly FASTER than both. Lengthening REDUCED velocity acutely** |

## 6. DISPUTES — revisited and added

### Dispute #10 (lower arm slot — universal or individual?) — **MOVED, still 🟡 NARROWED**

Two new inputs, both narrowing it further toward "individual":

1. **The effect is platoon-dependent.** Davy Andrews, FanGraphs, 27 Nov 2024, *"An Arm Angle Update That Ends With a Mystery"*: lower arm angles perform better against **same-handed** batters; higher arm angles better against **opposite-handed**. Same-sided correlations: arm angle→wOBA .13, →xwOBA .23; velocity −.18. Andrews flags an anomaly he cannot explain (every metric correlated better with xwOBA than actual wOBA) and says so plainly — credit for that.
2. **Slot changes propagate into spin efficiency and arsenal, not just VAA and torque.** Rosen, FanGraphs, 13 Apr 2026: Emerson Hancock dropped his arm angle **23° → 13°** and moved his landing crossbody; per Driveline's Spenser Davis the crossbody landing *"cut off his tendency to pronate."* Four-seam spin efficiency fell from 99%, which was **good** for him — it unlocked a glove-side breaking ball. 2.38 FIP, 30.6% K%.

**Narrowed conclusion: lower slot is a same-handed weapon that reshapes the whole arsenal, not a universal upgrade. Contraindicated for a pitcher facing mostly opposite-handed hitters, and possibly for a pitcher whose value is command (Manzi n = 338: the low-slot arms had worse command).** Slot is not a dial. It is a package, and the package includes his pronation tendency.

### Dispute #12 (velocity-optimal vs command-optimal) — **MOVED slightly, still 🔴 OPEN**

Nothing directly tests it. Two indirect nudges, both toward the "mirage" reading:

1. **The stride-length nulls weaken the entire "velocity-optimal delivery" construct.** If the single best-evidenced velocity lever produces nothing when experimentally moved, confidence that *any* of the cross-sectional kinematic coefficients are causal should drop — including trunk flexion at ball release (β = 1.829), which **is** the velocity side of this dispute and which the library already labeled "a marker, not a lever."
2. Andrews' platoon finding adds a fourth axis to the arm-slot square: slot has velocity, torque, command, **and platoon** consequences, pointing in different directions depending on who's hitting.

## 10. THE CORE-COILING STUDY — scouted properly, because it deserves it

**Lee CM, Choi W, Jeon BH (2026).** *The Effect of Core-Coiling Training for 12 Weeks on Skill-Related Physical Fitness and Performance in Collegiate Baseball Pitchers.* ***Asian Journal of Kinesiology* 28(1):19.** DOI [10.15758/ajk.2026.28.1.19](https://www.jkinesiol.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.15758%2Fajk.2026.28.1.19)

### What "core-coiling training" actually IS

It is not a new method and it is not Korean-specific. **Read the exercise list and it is a rotational medicine-ball and tubing program** — the thing American facilities have been calling "rotational power work" for fifteen years:

> Tubing Torso Rotation · One-Side Torso Rotation · One-Leg Torso Rotation · **Medicine Ball Side Throw** · **Medicine Ball Side Catch** · **Jump and Wall Ball Side Throw** · **Side Jumping and Wall Ball Throw** · Walking Lunge and Twist

**Dose:** 70-min sessions (10 warm-up / **50 main** / 10 cool-down), **4×/week × 12 weeks.** Intensity **60% 1RM wk 1–4 → 65% wk 5–8 → 70% wk 9–12.**

**That is a fully specified, runnable protocol.** The description is better than most peer-reviewed training papers manage, and a college program could execute it Monday.

### Results

n = 15 CCT vs n = 14 comparator, age ~20. Baseline **max velocity 86.2 / 85.9 mph** (clears the floor); **average velocity 84.0 / 83.5** (does not).

| Outcome | CCT | Comparator |
|---|---|---|
| **Max velocity** | **p = .003** | NS (p = .547) |
| **Average velocity** | **p = .011** | NS (p = .165) |
| Between-group ANCOVA | **p = .014 (max), .042 (avg)** | — |
| Agility | **p = .002** | NS (p = .131) |
| **Control / accuracy** | **No improvement** | **No improvement** |

### Verdict: PROMISING — and the one most likely to be over-quoted within a year

**Why it deserves attention rather than a dismissal:**
1. **It is the only positive controlled velocity result in a sample clearing 85 mph.** That is not nothing in a literature this empty.
2. **It converges with the mechanism.** Trunk power alone predicts ball velocity at R² = 0.731 (Aguinaldo & Escamilla 2019). Hip rotators do ~90% of the work of rotating the torso and supply ~30% of the power that reaches the shoulder (Roach & Lieberman 2014). Trunk torques are the primary energy *sources*; the shoulder and elbow are transfer elements (Naito 2021). **This trial trained exactly the segment the mechanism says generates.**
3. **It converges with the only other positive controlled non-implement trial** — Oyama 2026's 8-week lumbopelvic-hip block (⚑ HS, p = .014, and it also raised elbow varus moment). **Two countries, two populations, two independent controlled trials, same anatomical target.** That is the strongest convergence in the whole intervention literature, and it points at the trunk and hips — not the arm, not the implement.
4. **It partially rehabilitates rotational med-ball work**, which the library correctly called FOLKLORE on the grounds that the Szymanski literature is high-school *hitters* with *bat-speed* outcomes. That critique still stands. But the blanket "no such study exists in our population" no longer does.

**Why to hold it loosely — and the second one is disqualifying for the strong claim:**
1. **Not randomized.** No allocation concealment, no blinding, none described.
2. **⚠️ The comparator is close to a straw man.** The comparison group did a **machine-based general-strength circuit** — chest press, pull-down, shoulder press, arm curl, squat, leg press, leg extension, leg curl. **No rotational work. No plyometrics. No explosive lower-body work at all.** So this is *not* "rotational training beats strength training." It is **"12 weeks of explosive rotational and med-ball work beats 12 weeks of bodybuilding machines."** Given that this library's own §2.1 establishes that **absolute power and reactive strength** are the qualities that predict velocity — and the comparator trained neither — the comparison was rigged before it started, probably without anyone intending to rig it.
3. **It clears the floor on peak velocity only.** Average velocity 84.0 / 83.5 sits below it.
4. n = 29, one university, one squad. No kinematics, no kinetics, no stress outcome.
5. **Command did not improve in either group.** Worth saying out loud, because rotational-power marketing routinely implies it will.
6. Low-profile regional journal, not PubMed-indexed at time of review.

**How I would cite it:** *"one non-randomized 12-week trial, active comparator, collegiate, positive on peak velocity."* **Never** *"core training adds velocity."*

**What I'd actually do with it:** it does not change my program — I was already going to train the trail hip and trunk as the power source, on mechanism plus Oyama. What it changes is my **confidence label** on that block, from "mechanism only" to "mechanism plus two imperfect controlled trials." That is a real upgrade and it is worth exactly that much. **And it is now the recommendation that stride length was demoted below.**

**What would settle it:** the same trial with a real comparator — rotational med-ball work versus a *matched-dose explosive lower-body program* (jumps, bounds, absolute-power lifting), randomized, at 85+, with velocity and torque. **Until somebody runs that, we do not know whether the trunk is special or whether anything explosive beats leg extensions.**

## 11. WHAT FIVE CORRECTIONS IN ONE DAY IMPLIES

This is the part I think matters most, and it is uncomfortable.

**None of today's five corrections was a fabrication.** Not one. Sweep 1 was dominated by invented ASMI statements and phantom grip-strength correlations, and this program built a verification discipline around that threat. **Today's errors got through that discipline completely, because they are a different species.**

**Every one of them was a real source, correctly identified, read slightly wrong:**

| Error type | Example | Why the fabrication defense missed it |
|---|---|---|
| **A cross-sectional finding read as a causal lever** | Stride length: +2.0 mph/10% BH is a *real, correctly cited* number from a *real* n = 315 professional sample. The error was calling it a lever | Verifying the citation confirms the number. **The number is right. The inference is wrong** |
| **Two adjacent quantities collapsed into one** | Changeup: *absolute velocity* and *velocity separation* are different variables. Collapsing them inverted the conclusion | Both the source and the sentence are real. Nothing to catch |
| **A summary figure taken over the underlying table** | Driveline +1.35 vs the article's own 89.6 → 90.3 | Requires reading the table *and* recomputing the distribution — two steps past "does this source exist" |
| **A sampling unit misread** | "1,163 pitcher-seasons" vs pitcher × pitch-type × season pairs | The number is right. The denominator is not |
| **An absolutism doing rhetorical work it hadn't earned** | "Not one study at 85+" — Ake's baseline was *in the paper*, listed as "not reported" | Nobody checks a negative claim as hard as a positive one |
| **⚠️ A STUDY DESIGN INFERRED FROM A RESULT** | Gdovin's p < .001 read as a **"removal experiment."** The paper is *"Limiting Access to Resistance Training Equipment During the Off-Season"* — **an uncontrolled pre-post with no control group** | **Correct PMID. Correct n. Correct p-value. A design that does not exist.** This is the one that should frighten us — **the citation check passes perfectly and the claim is still false** |

**Three consequences I want adopted as standing rules.**

**Rule A — Verifying a citation is not verifying a claim, and this program has been treating them as the same thing.** A dated PMID next to a number tells you the number was published. It tells you nothing about whether the sentence wrapped around it is what the paper found. **Every one of today's six errors would have survived a PubMed check** — in every case the citation, the n, and the p-value were correct. The verification pass needs **two** further questions after "does this source exist":
- **"Does the source support this sentence, or a weaker one?"**
- **"What was the ACTUAL DESIGN, and is there a control group?"** — **never infer a design from a result.** Correction #6 is the case in point: a perfect citation, a real p-value, and a "removal experiment" that was an uncontrolled pre-post.

**Cross-reference:** this is precisely the failure the biomechanist has been warning about since Day 1 — cross-sectional coefficients being read as training targets. He was right, and the mechanism by which he was right is now documented five times over in a single day. `velocity-development.md` §4.7 lists downstream *consequences* under "do not chase" and then puts them at the top of its model tables anyway. **The document knows the rule and does not follow it.** (See §5, V3.)

**Rule B — Secondary sources are not the hazard. Our own prose is.** Every number today came from a legitimate primary source. The corruption happened in the *restatement* — in the compression from a paper into a table row into a recommendation. **Each compression step is a chance to lose a qualifier, and qualifiers are exactly what distinguishes "correlate" from "lever."** The practical fix: **any claim that has been promoted to a numbered recommendation gets re-read against the primary source before it ships**, because those are the ones that will be quoted to an athlete.

**Rule C — I did it too, in the direction I wanted.** I initially presented the Buffalo stride cohort as two independent studies. It is one experiment on 19 athletes reported across seven papers. **I nearly manufactured a false weight of evidence supporting the argument I was already making**, on the same day I wrote a cross-examination accusing my colleagues of exactly that. That is not a note of humility for its own sake — it is the reason Rule A has to apply to disconfirming evidence as hard as to confirming evidence. **The direction of an error is not evidence about its likelihood.**

**Rule D — the errors were not randomly distributed, and that is the finding.** All six moved the same way: **the library was more confident than its sources.** Random transcription error would have moved some of them toward *under*-claiming. **A one-directional error pattern is a systematic bias, and the most likely mechanism is that compression toward a usable recommendation is inherently a confidence-increasing operation.** Qualifiers are the first thing lost and they are precisely what separates "correlate" from "lever," "observed" from "removed," "absolute velocity" from "velocity separation."

**And the athlete-facing consequence, which is the whole reason any of this matters:** I was one day away from telling an 88 mph pitcher that a well-run block gets him about a mile and a half; that lengthening his stride was worth two more; and that the best-evidenced thing in his program was a lifting study that would stop him losing what he had. **The true numbers are two-thirds of a mile an hour, probably nothing, and one uncontrolled dozen with no control group and an effect size nobody has read.** He would have made a career decision on all three.
