## 1. Field sweep — headline

Good sweep. Six genuinely new items, one large hazard, one concession. Full log with sources and verdicts in `library/idea-scouting.md`. Top five:

1. **Effort-level dosing is real but the math is bad.** Fiegen 2023 / Wolf 2025 / Hyeamang 2026 converge on: a 25% cut in perceived effort buys ~13–19% less elbow torque and costs ~7.5–10% velocity. Half effort buys about a quarter less torque. Fleisig's 2026 *Sports Biomechanics* proposal to vary effort is strategically sound and mechanically oversold. **PROMISING for the strategy, DEBUNKED for the naive version.** Practical fix: submaximal days are radar-gated, not feel-gated.
2. **Reinold et al. 2026 (IJSPT) rebuilt interval throwing from 238,611 flat-ground throws (34 D1 pitchers).** The number to memorize: **elbow varus torque at 120 ft of long toss ≈ pitching off a mound.** Reclassifies long toss past ~120 ft from arm care to mound-equivalent load. **PROMISING** — most immediately useful item in the sweep.
3. **Skill is not less variability; it's better-organized variability.** Bloebaum (Driveline), UCM analysis, **43,650 pitches / 2,052 pitchers**. Elite arms confine variability to release-preserving directions (partial ρ = 0.22, p < 10⁻⁴⁷). Converges with Driveline's Feb 2026 command study (n=27): **repeatability matters at foot plant; adjustability matters everywhere else.** **PROMISING.** Neither library file covers command at all — biggest coverage gap in the program.
4. **Lower arm slot is now quantified on both sides.** ~4.23 N·m less varus torque per 10° at consistent velocity (n=66 elite college, OpenBiomechanics); MLB field data: −0.15 mph, +18.3 rpm, +2.14 run value for slot-droppers. **The best stress-benefit-per-velocity-cost lever in the library.** Contraindications named.
5. **⚠️ AI content-farm hazard.** Multiple high-ranking pages fabricated a "2026 ASMI consensus statement," a "USA Baseball 2026 coach survey," and precise fake statistics ("68% lower shoulder stress"), all attributed to real institutions. **None exist** — verified against ASMI's own publication list. **New standing rule: every institutional claim gets verified at the institution's publication list or on PubMed before it enters the library.**

### Sharpest thing that came out of it

**Question 4.** The 165–180° "max external rotation" number is a composite — true glenohumeral ER plus scapulothoracic posterior tilt plus thoracic extension plus trunk hyperextension plus forearm contribution. **More layback can come from the thoracic spine (adaptable, cheap) or from the anterior band of the IGHL (already plastically deformed in any 85+ arm, expensive). On video the two look identical.**

That is the single most consequential thing I learned today, because it means the most-coached position in pitching is a number that cannot tell you whether the athlete is getting better or getting looser. Combined with the fact that humeral retrotorsion — which sets the whole arc — was locked at skeletal maturity and is bone: **I will never again coach toward an MER target, and I will never stretch an elite arm into more external rotation.**

## 3. Cross-examination

### 3a. Answers to challenges put to me

**Biomechanist → "Which of the four 'free' torque reductions is actually modifiable without collateral velocity loss?"**

**Shoulder abduction at foot contact. Start there and only there.** It is a position at a landmark I can freeze on video, it lives in the stride — the only phase long enough to organize anything — and it has a felt cue and a working constraint. Ranked full answer in `open-disputes.md` #1. Later pelvis peak is modifiable only through its upstream causes (lead-hip IR, thoracic rotation, landing foot angle) — nobody can feel 15 ms. Shoulder abduction at BR is barely reachable inside a 30–50 ms window and the only route to it (trunk lateral tilt) is itself a positive torque contributor, so it's a wash. Elbow flexion at FC I refuse to cue — Dispute #2.

**Standing objection I want on the record:** "free" is a property of a regression coefficient, not of a bullpen. Four variables from a model explaining 40% of torque variance, cross-sectional, zero intervention trials, in an athlete where they all covary.

**Biomechanist → "Can we get force plates, since GRF is directly measured and torque is always modeled?"**

**Yes — and your case is stronger than you made it.** Dowling, Slowik & Fleisig (2026, *Sports Biomechanics*) now show that sampling rate and low-pass filter cutoff materially change computed pitching data (240–500 Hz sampling; 13.4 and 18.0 Hz the common cutoffs). Since inverse-dynamics torque rides on the second derivative of position, that is the mechanism behind our unresolved 64 vs 100 vs 120 N·m spread. **Adopt this as a rule: a torque value without its sampling rate and filter cutoff is not a number.**

**But directly measured ≠ important.** Bodyweight-controlled lead-leg GRF explains **4–6% of between-pitcher velocity variance.** Modeled torque predicts UCL surgery at HR 1.26 per 10 N·m. Precision and importance are different things, and force plates get sold as though they were the same. Also: your own file says elite braking-impulse norms in %BW·s **don't exist**, so we'd be buying an instrument to measure a variable with no reference values. Fine for within-athlete tracking, useless for evaluation. Say so on the purchase order.

**Anatomist → "Can mechanics decouple velocity from varus torque within an individual?"**

Here is the reconciliation I'd defend to an athlete: **R² = 0.957 describes a fixed delivery — along *this* curve, more velocity always costs more torque. The 28% efficiency gap describes different curves.** Coaching doesn't move you along your curve for free; it moves you to a different curve, and then you re-apply effort along the new one.

**But I won't pretend that's demonstrated.** Every piece of evidence for the 28% gap is between-subject and cross-sectional. It may be anthropometry, tissue quality, and humeral torsion — none coachable. And 60% of normalized torque variance is unexplained by kinematics. **Nobody has shown a within-athlete curve shift.** `open-disputes.md` #3.

**Anatomist → "Is there a dugout-measurable marker of posterior-cuff activation deficit that fires before command drifts?"**

**No, not a validated one.** Three candidates, all inadequate: between-innings HHD external-rotation strength (reliable instrument, no between-innings protocol, no MDC for this use, and the maximal contraction is itself a load); grip dynamometry (cheap and useful, but it's a forearm marker, not a cuff marker — right answer to a different question); slot drop on video (free, but it fires *after* command drift, which is what you asked us to beat).

**And I think the request may be malformed.** If the deficit is *central* voluntary activation, then any field test requiring a maximal voluntary contraction measures the same inhibited system with a cruder instrument. It will be noisier than the pitcher's own performance, which is already a 100-rep-per-outing readout. **The best available marker of a pitcher's arm may just be the pitcher.** I'd rather formalize the observation ladder — ATC charts arm-side miss rate and breaking-ball finish by inning — than chase a number that doesn't exist.

### 3b. Stress-testing the three biomech claims that contradict standard coaching

**Claim 1 — "Pelvis rotation gain vs velo, r = −0.07; the block does not whip the hips."**
**Verdict: cue retired, claim narrowed. 🟡**
I've killed the cue and I'm keeping it dead. But three problems before this hardens into library dogma: (a) a between-subject null in a population where *everyone already blocks* is a restricted-range problem, not proof of irrelevance; (b) the raw value is −0.07 and the bodyweight-controlled value is +0.10 — two signs on a variable this weak means the honest reading is "indistinguishable from zero," not "negative"; (c) it is **one industry R&D dataset, not peer-reviewed**, retiring a century-old model. **What would settle it:** replication in a second force-plate dataset, plus a within-athlete analysis — on a given pitcher's *harder* pitches, does pelvis rotation gain rise?

**Claim 2 — "Pros throw from a lower arm slot than HS, and it costs less torque."**
**Verdict: CORROBORATED, and stronger than when you wrote it — but not universal. 🟡**
Three independent corroborations found today: n=66 elite college (OpenBiomechanics) showing ~4.23 N·m less varus torque per 10° at consistent velocity plus better torque efficiency; MLB field data showing release points 2 in lower than 2016 and slot-droppers gaining +2.14 run value and +18.3 rpm at a cost of 0.15 mph; and ~4% torque reduction per 10°. **Concede the direction. Dispute the universality on three grounds:** it inverts at the HS level (lower slot → *higher* elbow flexion torque), and our population's floor sits astride that inversion; it requires wrist radial-deviation mobility to hold spin efficiency (Skenes tried and reverted); and **the most common way a coach "lowers a slot" is by adding trunk lateral tilt, which is itself a positive contributor to elbow varus torque** — executed wrong, this intervention costs torque instead of saving it. That is the failure mode that turns a good finding harmful in a bullpen.

**Claim 3 — "The textbook proximal-to-distal sequence was not observed on a single pitch (n=14); 2.7 sequences per pitcher."**
**Verdict: ⬜ I CONCEDE.**
I came in to attack this on sample size. It replicates: Scarborough et al. (2020, *Sports Biomechanics* 19(5)) — **n=22, 208 pitches, 14 distinct patterns, not one fully proximal-to-distal**, fewer than 10% of pitchers used a single pattern, and **variability was similar in HS pitchers and professionals** (so it isn't even a skill marker). Plus a further 30 pitchers / 249 fastballs → 17 patterns. Three labs, ~50 pitchers, 600+ pitches, same answer. "Fix your kinematic sequence" is retired.
**What survives, and I want it on the record:** the disorder is **distal** — arm/forearm/hand ordering. The proximal end holds: trunk-before-pelvis raises shoulder and elbow torque, and later pelvis peak associates with lower normalized torque. So the coachable claim narrows from "sequence your delivery" to **"don't let your chest beat your hips to foot strike"** — one specific, upstream, screenable failure. That one stands.

### 3c. My challenges to them

#### To the biomechanist

**Challenge B1 — Your "four free reductions" table is doing work that a 40%-R² cross-sectional model cannot support, and it will get repeated as prescription.**
The word "free" in a section header will survive every caveat you attached to it. Two of the four are effectively unreachable in a bullpen (shoulder abduction at BR sits inside a 30–50 ms window; elbow flexion at FC is arm path, not a dial), and all four covary in a real athlete. **What would settle it:** a partial-correlation or mediation analysis in the ASMI dataset — does elbow flexion at FC still predict torque after controlling for arm slot, arm-path length, and shoulder abduction at FC? If the coefficient survives, I fold. **And rename the section "the four lowest-cost hypotheses."**

**Challenge B2 — You recommend a technology stack without stating which measurements survive contact with a competing athlete.**
Your §7.6 stack is good. But the athlete who matters pitches Friday. A 240 fps two-camera setup gives me FC, MER, BR, stride length, knee behavior, foot angle — six things — and I have to tell him honestly that **his slot number, his separation number, and every torque number he's seen on Instagram are not measurements I can make.** Your file says that; your stack doesn't lead with it. **What would settle it:** a one-page "what you may and may not claim from this instrument" table at the top of §7, not the bottom.

**Challenge B3 — The trunk-flexion-at-release coefficient (β = 1.829, +8.3 mph per 10°) is in a velocity-predictor table where a coach will read it as a lever.**
You flagged it as a marker not a lever — in a caveat, three paragraphs below the table. Nobody reads the caveat. This is the single most dangerous number in the library, because "get out over your front side" is already the most over-coached cue in baseball and you've just handed it an 8-mph price tag. **What would settle it:** move the caveat into the table cell, or drop the effect size entirely.

#### To the anatomist

**Challenge A1 — Your between-start framework is written for a starter with a normal week and my athlete does not have one.**
Day 0 through Day +4 is physiologically sound and it assumes a five-day cycle, one high-intent exposure, and control of the calendar. My actual athlete is a draft-followed HS arm who throws Friday for his school, Sunday for his travel org, has a showcase on Wednesday, and is being watched by three organizations who each believe they own one appearance. **You named this hazard (§3.4) and then wrote a framework that presumes it away.** What I need is the *triage* version: given that the athlete WILL throw high-intent twice in four days, what's the least-bad ordering, and what's the marker that says stop? **What would settle it:** a decision tree for the compressed/uncontrolled schedule, not a template for the clean one.

**Challenge A2 — "Track him against his own baseline" is the right principle and you have not told me how to establish a baseline.**
ER strength, total rotational motion, elbow extension, perceived readiness — measured how often, by whom, at what time relative to throwing, and how many measurements before I have a baseline rather than a data point? Post-outing ROM is still down at 24 h, so a "baseline" taken on a random Tuesday in a dense stretch is not a baseline. Without a protocol this is unfalsifiable advice. **What would settle it:** a concrete minimum — e.g., "five measurements at ≥72 h post-outing across two weeks, same time of day, same tester, before you may call anything a baseline." Give me a number I can be wrong about.

**Challenge A3 — You call fatigue the highest-leverage variable and then give me no field test for it.**
Your own strongest mechanism — the flexor-pronator mass stops sharing load, so the ligament's share of a constant net torque rises — is the entire injury model, and it's labeled inference, not measurement. Meanwhile you note the infraspinatus activation deficit is *centrally* mediated, which means it's probably invisible to any voluntary field test. **So the most important variable in the system is the one we cannot measure.** I'd rather you said that in bold than left it distributed across four sections. **What would settle it:** the study in `open-disputes.md` #5 — interpolated-twitch activation plus a candidate field test at innings 1/3/5/7, with per-pitch command tracking. It's feasible today.
