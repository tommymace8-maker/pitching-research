## 3. THE RESEARCH — the arithmetic of pitch usage

All of §3 is derived in-cycle. No citation is required for any of it, and it can be reproduced from the scripts in §8.

### 3a. The optimum equalises MARGINAL value, not average value

**Set-up.** A pitcher chooses usage shares $p_i$ over pitch types $i = 1 \dots k$, with $\sum p_i = 1$. Let $v_i(p_i)$ be the run value per pitch of type $i$ **expressed to the hitter** — so lower is better for the pitcher. The single assumption is that a pitch degrades as it is used more, because the hitter's prior on seeing it rises: $v_i' > 0$.

The pitcher minimises expected runs per pitch:

$$V(p) = \sum_i p_i\, v_i(p_i)$$

Stationarity under $\sum p_i = 1$ gives, for every pitch actually used:

$$\boxed{\;v_i(p_i) + p_i\, v_i'(p_i) = \lambda\;}$$

**The bracketed term is the whole finding.** What is equal at the optimum is $v_i + p_i v_i'$, the *marginal* run value — the value of the pitch **plus the damage that throwing one more of it does to all the others of its type**. The *average* values $v_i$ are equal only if the wedge terms $p_i v_i'$ happen to be equal across pitches.

**Sanity check on the assumption.** If $v_i' = 0$ for all $i$ — no anticipation penalty — then $V$ is linear in $p$ and the optimum is a **corner**: throw the single best pitch 100% of the time. The interior mix every pitcher actually uses is therefore *evidence that $v_i' > 0$*. The anticipation penalty is not an add-on to the model; it is the only reason a mix exists at all.

**EVIDENCE: ESTABLISHED** (it is a constrained optimisation, not an empirical claim). **CAUSALITY: MECHANISM.**

### 3b. At a true optimum, the MORE-USED pitch should look BETTER on the leaderboard

Take the simplest anticipation model, linear in usage: $v_i(p_i) = a_i + b_i p_i$, with $b_i > 0$. Then the first-order condition is $a_i + 2b_i p_i = \lambda$, and substituting back:

$$v_i = \lambda - b_i p_i$$

**Average run values are equal if and only if $b_i p_i$ is equal across pitches.** With equal slopes $b$, the pitch thrown more often sits at a *lower* (better) average run value, by exactly $b(p_i - p_j)$.

Let $D$ = the anticipation penalty in runs per 100 pitches across a full 0→100% usage swing (so $b = D/100$). **$D$ is not known — it is bracketed:**

**How far apart average run values SHOULD sit at a true optimum (runs per 100, by which the MORE-USED pitch should look BETTER):**

| $D$ (R/100 over a 0→1 usage swing) | 60/40 split | 55/45 | 70/30 | 50/30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 0.40 | 0.20 |
| 2.0 | 0.40 | 0.20 | 0.80 | 0.40 |
| 4.0 | 0.80 | 0.40 | 1.60 | 0.80 |
| 8.0 | 1.60 | 0.80 | 3.20 | 1.60 |

**Read the row that matters.** ESPN's exemplar of a pitcher at equilibrium had his pitches "within **0.2 runs per 100** of the average of his others." At $D = 4$ and a 60/40 split, the *correct* optimum has them **0.8 runs per 100 apart** — four times the tolerance being treated as proof of optimality. A pitcher whose two pitches show *equal* run value at unequal usage is, on this model, **under-throwing his more-used pitch.**

**Worked two-pitch example.** Fastball intrinsically 1.0 R/100 worse than the slider, equal slopes at $D = 4$:

| FB usage | FB avg (R/100) | SL avg (R/100) | Total (R/100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30% | +2.20 | +2.80 | +2.620 |
| 40% | +2.60 | +2.40 | +2.480 |
| **43.8% (optimum)** | **+2.75** | **+2.25** | **+2.469** |
| 50% | +3.00 | +2.00 | +2.500 |
| 60% | +3.40 | +1.60 | +2.680 |
| 70% | +3.80 | +1.20 | +3.020 |

Note what the optimum row looks like on a leaderboard: **the fastball is the "worse" pitch (+2.75 vs +2.25) and is thrown less (43.8%) — and this is exactly right.** A Nash-Score-style reading would flag this pitcher as under-using his slider. He is not.

⚠️ **The honest limit, stated by the biomechanist before anyone else could:** this table is a *model output*, not a measurement. $D$ is unknown, the linear form is an assumption, and equal slopes across pitches is a further assumption that is probably false (a sweeper and a four-seam almost certainly do not decay at the same rate with usage). **What survives all of that is the sign and the structure, not the specific numbers**: at any interior optimum with any smooth $v_i$, average run values differ by $p_j v_j' - p_i v_i'$, and the more-used pitch is better whenever slopes are comparable. **Do not quote the cell values as norms.**

Per-pitch run value is a lottery: ~90% of pitches move run expectancy by a few hundredths of a run, and a small tail moves it by more than a full run. Building the distribution from approximate MLB outcome frequencies and delta-run-expectancy values:

|---|---|---|
| ball | .360 | +0.055 |
| called strike | .170 | −0.045 |
| swinging strike | .105 | −0.045 |
| foul | .185 | −0.025 |
| in play, out | .118 | −0.270 |
| single | .038 | +0.450 |
| double | .013 | +0.750 |
| triple | .001 | +1.050 |
| home run | .010 | +1.400 |

$\Rightarrow$ **SD ≈ 0.214 runs per pitch.**

| 100 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |

**Anchor these against reality.** A **college starter throws roughly 1,400–1,700 pitches in a full season, across his whole arsenal.** An MLB qualified starter throws ~3,000. So:
- A college pitcher's **primary fastball** might see 700 pitches in a season → SE ≈ **0.95 R/100**.
- His **third pitch** might see 200 → SE ≈ **1.77 R/100**.
- Even an **MLB ace's entire season of fastballs** (~1,200) sits at SE ≈ **0.72 R/100**.

**The Nash Score criterion of 0.2 R/100 is therefore between four and nine times smaller than the standard error of the numbers it is computed from, at every realistic sample size.** Jake Irvin's pitches being "within 0.2 R/100 of each other" is not evidence that he is at equilibrium. It is not evidence of anything. It is a coin landing near its own mean.

### 3e. The objective is quadratically flat — being wrong is cheap, and this is the finding a coach can actually use

Because $V$ is stationary at the optimum, the loss from a usage error is **second-order**. For the two-pitch linear model, $V(p) - V(p^*) = (b_F + b_S)(p - p^*)^2$. This flatness is **general** — it follows from stationarity, not from linearity, so it survives any smooth value function.

**Cost of a wrong mix, runs per 100 pitches / runs over a 1,600-pitch college season:**

| Usage error | D = 1 | D = 2 | D = 4 | D = 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 pp | 0.005 / **0.08** | 0.010 / 0.16 | 0.020 / 0.32 | 0.040 / 0.64 |
| 10 pp | 0.020 / **0.32** | 0.040 / 0.64 | 0.080 / 1.28 | 0.160 / 2.56 |
| 15 pp | 0.045 / 0.72 | 0.090 / 1.44 | 0.180 / 2.88 | 0.360 / 5.76 |
| 20 pp | 0.080 / 1.28 | 0.160 / 2.56 | 0.320 / **5.12** | 0.640 / 10.24 |
| 30 pp | 0.180 / 2.88 | 0.360 / 5.76 | 0.720 / 11.52 | 1.440 / **23.04** |

**Two coaching consequences, and they point opposite directions:**
1. **Small mix errors are nearly free.** Five points off optimal costs a tenth of a run to two-thirds of a run over a whole season. **Nobody should spend a single minute of a bullpen on a 5-point usage question.**
2. **Large mix errors are not free.** Thirty points off — a pitcher throwing a four-seam 65% when 35% is right, which is a completely ordinary error in college baseball — costs **3 to 23 runs a season.** At the top of that bracket that is a full win.

**The actionable regime is large errors only.** The middle does not exist.

### 3g. The one thing that IS measurable — and the level the inference belongs at

**Unmeasurable-on-an-individual is not the same as unreal.** Look again at the 20pp/D=8 row: 15,330–34,492 pitches per condition. At ~3,000 pitches per MLB starter-season that is **5 to 12 pitcher-seasons** — which, pooled across pitchers, Statcast delivers instantly. The 10pp/D=8 row needs ~245,000–552,000, or **80 to 180 pitcher-seasons** — still a routine query against a database holding millions.

**So the anticipation slope $D$ is a population parameter that is cheaply estimable league-wide and permanently unknowable athlete-by-athlete.** The correct architecture is: **estimate $D$ once from the league, apply it to the individual as a prior, and never try to learn it from him.** A pitcher's own run-value line should have no vote in his own mix.

**How to estimate $D$ from data that already exists** — this is the cycle's handed-forward study, and it needs no new instrumentation: within pitcher, within season, regress each pitch type's run value (or, better, its whiff-per-swing, which is far less noisy) on that pitch type's **trailing usage share**, using the natural variation in usage across starts and across times-through-the-order. Fixed effects for pitcher × pitch type. The slope is $b_i$. **This has almost certainly been done inside at least one front office and has not, as far as this corpus can tell, been published.**

## 4. THE ANATOMIST'S CONTRIBUTION — the slope has two signs, and that changes the answer

**The anatomist's objection, and it is the best thing produced in this cycle:**

> *"You have all assumed $v_i' > 0$ — that a pitch gets worse the more he throws it, because the hitter starts expecting it. That is one of two effects and you have ignored the other. A pitch thrown 5% of the time is a pitch the athlete throws maybe 70 times a season. He is not going to execute it. Motor programmes are dose-dependent and they decay. The fourth pitch is not bad because hitters sit on it — nobody sits on a pitch thrown 5% of the time — it is bad because he never throws it."*

This is correct and it is a genuine mathematical change, not a caveat.

**Write the slope as a net of two terms:** $b_i = b_i^{\text{ant}} - b_i^{\text{dose}}$, where $b^{\text{ant}} > 0$ is the hitter's anticipation penalty and $b^{\text{dose}} > 0$ is the pitcher's own execution improvement from throwing it more. At low usage $b^{\text{dose}}$ plausibly dominates, so **$b_i < 0$ in the low-usage region.**

**And that inverts the optimisation.** With $b_i < 0$, the contribution $a_i p_i + b_i p_i^2$ is **concave**, so it has no interior minimum — the solution is pushed to a **corner**. Formally: *in any usage region where the dose effect outruns the anticipation effect, the optimal usage of that pitch is either zero or substantially higher. There is no optimal small dose.*

**This is the single most usable result of the cycle.** It says the fourth-pitch decision is not "how much?" but **"commit or cut."** Sprinkling a pitch at 5–8% is, on this model, the one usage level that is provably wrong — it is the region where the pitch is under-practised *and* insufficiently threatening.

**⚠️ What is NOT known, and it is the pivotal quantity:** the crossover usage $p_c$ at which $b^{\text{dose}}$ stops dominating $b^{\text{ant}}$. The vault holds nothing on it. Nobody has measured it. **The 5–8% number in the paragraph above is the coach's intuition, not a finding**, and it is written here as intuition so a future cycle does not promote it.

**Second anatomist point, honest and short:** does a 15-point shift toward a breaking ball change tissue load? **UNKNOWN.** INDEX.md §5 lists *"Non-fastball workload accounting — whether a slider-heavy outing loads differently from a fastball-heavy one at equal pitch count"* as entirely uncovered. A mix change is a load change of unknown sign and magnitude. One line, per the brief: **this is the constraint that could veto a mix recommendation, and it is currently unpriced.**

## 5. TRANSLATE — the coach's four questions

### Q1 (coach → biomechanist): *"You just told me I can never measure whether a mix change worked. So why would I ever change one?"*

**Answer.** Because measurability and value are different things, and you have been conflating them. §3e says a 30-point usage error costs 3–23 runs a season; §3f says you cannot detect that you fixed it. **Both are true.** The mistake is thinking a decision requires a measurement — this one requires a *model*, estimated at the league level (§3g) and applied to your athlete as a prior.

> **What I tell the pitcher:** *"We're changing your mix on principle, not on evidence, and I'm telling you that up front. If your numbers look better in April I'm not going to credit this, and if they look worse I'm not going to blame it. Neither of those readings would be real."*
> **The drill:** none. This is a call sheet decision made in the office in February, not a bullpen intervention.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing. There is no video failure mode for a mix error — which is precisely why it survives in programs that coach off video.
> **How we know it's working:** *we don't, and we never will.* The only thing we verify is **compliance** — did the mix actually change? Pull the usage percentages off TrackMan after four starts. That is a proportion, not a run value, and 400 pitches pins a usage share to about ±2.5 points. **Verify the input; abandon the outcome.**

### Q2 (coach → biomechanist): *"You're telling me the pitch he throws MORE should look BETTER. Every analyst I know reads it the other way — 'his slider grades out best, throw it more.' Which of us is wrong?"*

**Answer.** They are, on their own model's terms — but the honest answer is that **the direction is right and the size is unknown.** §3b: at an interior optimum with comparable slopes, $v_i = \lambda - b_i p_i$, so the more-used pitch sits at the better average value by $b\,\Delta p$. Seeing a pitcher's slider grade 1 R/100 better than his fastball at 55/45 fastball usage is **consistent with an optimum**, not evidence of a mistake. The "throw your best pitch more" reading takes a leaderboard gap as a lever when it is a marker — **this is the stride-length error transplanted from biomechanics into analytics**, and it is being made by people who would never make it about stride length.

> **What I tell the pitcher:** *"Your slider's numbers being better than your fastball's numbers doesn't mean throw more sliders. If we threw more sliders the numbers would even up, and you'd be worse off, because the reason it looks that good is that they aren't looking for it."*
> **The drill:** none. This is a reading-comprehension fix for the coaching staff, not for the athlete.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a pitch-usage chart that has drifted 10+ points across a season toward whichever pitch had the good April.
> **How we know it's working:** you don't verify this one either. You verify that **nobody on the staff changed a call sheet off a run-value leaderboard.**

### Q3 (coach → both): *"How far off can a kid be before it actually costs him a game?"*

**Answer.** §3e, and it is a genuinely useful number. Below ~10 points off optimal, the cost is **0.3 to 2.6 runs across an entire 1,600-pitch season** — less than a single blown call. At 20 points it is 1.3 to 10 runs. At 30 points, 3 to 23 runs, and the top of that range is a full win.

> **What I tell the pitcher:** *"I'm not going to nitpick you five percent one way or the other on your mix — that's worth about half a run a year and I'd rather you were comfortable. But if you're throwing your heater two out of three and it should be one out of three, that's a real number, and we're going to fix that one."*
> **The drill:** a **call-sheet audit, once, in the office.** Pull his last full season's usage by pitch type and by batter hand. Look for anything more than 20 points from where the arsenal's shape says it should be.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a pitcher who has one pitch he trusts in a 2-0 count and one he trusts with two strikes, and nothing in between — the mix collapsed by count without anyone deciding it should.
> **How we know it's working:** usage percentages by count and by batter hand across ~400 tracked pitches. **Compliance again, not outcome.**

### Q4 (coach → anatomist): *"He's got a changeup he throws six percent of the time. Keep it or kill it?"*

**Answer.** §4 says **those are the only two options, and 6% is the one answer that is provably wrong.** In the region where the execution-dose effect outruns the anticipation effect, the objective is concave and the optimum is a corner. A pitch at 6% is under-thrown by its owner and not respected by the hitter — it collects the costs of both regimes and the benefits of neither.

⚠️ **The honest limit: the crossover point $p_c$ is unmeasured.** The *structure* of this argument is derivable; the *threshold* is not. Do not tell a pitcher that 6% is below a known line. Tell him it is in a region where the model says small doses don't work.

> **What I tell the pitcher:** *"Right now that changeup is a rumour. You throw it six times a game and you don't trust it, and neither do they. We're either going to build it into something you throw fifteen, eighteen percent of the time — which means you throw it every day and you throw it in counts that scare you — or we're taking it out of the arsenal and you're a two-pitch guy who's really good at two pitches. What you can't do is keep it as a rumour."*
> **The drill:** if committing — the changeup goes into **every** catch-play and every bullpen, and it gets called in 1-1 and 2-1, not just 0-2. If cutting — it comes out of the game plan entirely and the reps go to the primary.
> **On video the failure looks like:** the tell every coach already knows — the arm slows, the front side flies, the finish is different from the fastball. But see **F-256: that tell is FOLKLORE with a plausible mechanism, universally repeated and never measured.** It is legitimate to look at; it is not legitimate to claim as known.
> **How we know it's working — and this one IS affordable.** Do **not** track the changeup's run value; §3d says that needs thousands of pitches. Track its **execution consistency against his fastball's**, using the vertical release-angle SD the corpus already uses for command (F-171, F-186). Comparing two within-pitcher SDs, α = .05, 80% power:
>
> | The changeup's release-angle SD is this much worse than the fastball's | Pitches of EACH type needed |
> |---|---|
> | 2.00× | 34 |
> | 1.75× | 51 |
> | 1.50× | **96** |
> | 1.40× | 140 |
> | 1.30× | **229** |
> | 1.20× | 473 |
> | 1.15× | 805 |
>
> **~100 changeups and ~100 fastballs — three or four bullpens — will tell you whether he executes that pitch 50% worse than his fastball.** If he does, the pitch is a dose problem and the commit path is live. If his release consistency on it is already near his fastball's, then it is not an execution problem and the "commit" argument loses its main support. **That is a real, affordable, in-season check on the one question that actually has an answer.**

## 8. REPRODUCIBILITY
