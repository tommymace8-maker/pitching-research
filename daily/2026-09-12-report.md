## 3. THE RESEARCH

### 3a. The margin model — the whole topic is one subtraction [DERIVED] → **F-316**

**M = T_runner − (T_plate + T_pop)**, and M > 0 is safe. Every claim anyone makes about holding runners is a claim about one of those three terms. **The pitcher owns one.**

Margin in seconds against an average 2.00 s pop time (runner and pop-time inputs are REPORTED brackets, §2f):

| T_plate → | 1.20 (slide) | 1.30 (quick) | 1.40 (average) | 1.50 (slow) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elite runner 3.30 | +0.10 | 0.00 | −0.10 | −0.20 |
| Good runner 3.45 | +0.25 | +0.15 | +0.05 | −0.05 |
| Average runner 3.60 | +0.40 | +0.30 | +0.20 | +0.10 |

**The entire operating range of the topic — best runner to average runner, slide step to slow delivery — spans 0.60 seconds, and the pitcher controls half of it.**

**And the conversion that makes it coachable [DERIVED]:** a runner arrives at second at 25–28 ft/s, so **1 foot of lead ≈ 0.036–0.040 s**, and **0.10 s of delivery ≈ 2.5–2.8 feet of lead.** The gap between a 1.45 and a 1.25 delivery is **about 5.4 feet of lead** — more than any number of pickoff throws will ever take off a good baserunner.

### 3b. A tenth of a second is worth 10–20 points of success rate [DERIVED] → **F-316**

P(safe) = Φ(M/σ_M). σ_M — the SD of the margin across jump, throw, tag and the pitcher's own variation — is **not known anywhere** and is bracketed 0.15–0.30 s.

Points of safe-rate per 0.1 s: **26.6 / 19.9 / 16.0 / 13.3** at the 50% point for σ_M = 0.15/0.20/0.25/0.30, and **19.7 / 14.8 / 11.8 / 9.9** at a realistic 78% operating point. **Call it 10–20 points per tenth.** That is a very large number by this corpus's standards — and §3d explains why you will never see it.

### 3c. The break-even success rate survives not knowing either input [DERIVED] → **F-317**

CS is built from this corpus's **own** F-270 table (an in-play out = **−0.270 R**) plus a runner-removal increment reconstructed at 0.15–0.20 R, giving CS ≈ −0.42 to −0.47. SB ≈ +0.15 to +0.20. **Neither was read from a page. Both are bracketed everywhere.**

**Break-even p\* = |CS|/(|CS|+SB). Across every combination in the bracket: 67.7% to 75.8%, centred at 71.5%.** Both inputs unverified, both moved ±15%, and the answer moves eight points. **This is an F-307-class result: a ratio that survives not knowing its own inputs. Say "about seventy percent." Never quote a decimal.**

**And the consequence nobody says out loud:** because league success sits *just above* break-even, **one stolen-base attempt is worth almost nothing to anyone.** EV to the offence per attempt at SB=+0.175 / CS=−0.44: **−0.040 R at p=.65; −0.010 at .70; +0.003 at .72; +0.040 at .78; +0.083 at .85; +0.114 at .90.** At league-average success an attempt is worth **four hundredths of a run** — less than a tenth of what a walk costs. The loudest event in the dugout is one of the cheapest on the field.

### 3d. ⚠️ THE ENDOGENEITY — the payoff is invisible in the column everyone reads [DERIVED] → **F-318**

§3b assumes the runner goes regardless. **He does not. He goes when the margin is favourable.** A pitcher who takes 0.2 s off his delivery therefore does not mostly produce *failed* steals — he produces **unattempted** ones. The runners who would have been thrown out never leave first base.

**So the improvement lands in the ATTEMPT column, not the SUCCESS column — and his success-rate-against may not move at all, or may RISE, because only the fastest runners still try.**

Fifth appearance of the same structure: F-094 (between vs within), F-269/F-299 (at an optimum the more-used option looks better), Dispute #20b (strike probability is a move in a game), F-309 (catcher assignment is not random, so it is bias not noise), and now this. **A pitcher's stolen-base-success-rate-against is a selected statistic and must never be used to evaluate him.**

### 3e. The third disengagement loses by 14–28× [DERIVED] → **F-322**

Under a two-disengagement limit (MLB since 2023; proposed as an NCAA conference experiment, §2c), **the third disengagement is a balk unless the pickoff records an out.** So:

- Out → worth a caught stealing, **+0.44 R** to the defence.
- Anything else → balk, runner to second, **+0.175 R** to the offence.
- **Break-even P(pickoff out) = 0.175/(0.44+0.175) = 28.5%**, and **27.3–29.4% across the entire run-value bracket.**

**Pickoff throws record an out on the order of 1–2%. The third disengagement loses by a factor of 14 to 28.**

> **THE CARD RULE: never make the third disengagement unless the pickoff is essentially certain.** Not "rarely." The required success rate is twenty times what a pickoff throw achieves. Same genre as F-283 ("never challenge on 0-2"), and the margin (14–28×) is comparable to F-313's 9.8×.

**And the ordinary pickoff throw is close to worthless too:** 1–2% × 0.44 R ≈ **+0.007 R**, against a throwing-error risk of the same order. **Direct EV ≈ zero.** The entire case for throwing over is the deterrence channel, and §3a prices it — **a foot of lead is 0.037 s** — but **nobody has published a lead-length response to pickoff attempts for any population.** (F-324, Dispute #24.)

### 3g. The literature, and what its sample actually was [REPORTED + DERIVED] → **F-323**

**There is essentially one peer-reviewed slide-step velocity comparison in circulation and it is nine mph below this program's floor.** PMID 22487194: **88 pitches, 10 pitchers (6 collegiate, 4 HS), mean age 17.60 ± 2.63**, **76.2 ± 5.3 mph** (stretch) vs **74.5 ± 4.7** (slide step). Four things follow, and they matter more than the number:

1. **SAMPLE MISMATCH — directional only.** *"The science says the slide step costs 1.7 mph"* is a statement about ten teenagers.
2. **It is nonetheless one of the rare MANIPULATED comparisons in this corpus.** Same pitchers, both deliveries, within-subject. Unlike stride length (F-043/F-044/F-045) or open pelvis (F-049), the variable was actually *changed* and the result *measured*. **Delivery time is a LEVER, not a marker** — the marker/lever rule is satisfied here in a way it almost never is. What is *not* established is the magnitude of its cost at 85+.
3. **We do not know whether 1.7 mph was tested, or what the paired SD was.** Two descriptive means. At n=10 with a between-subject SD near 5 mph, an unpaired test is nowhere near significance; a paired test might be. **A descriptive difference of unknown reliability. Do not quote it as a cost.**
4. **The paper's own reported result argues against its own headline.** It reports **no differences at front foot contact or ball release.** **F-088 says foot contact is where the delivery is decided.** If the two deliveries are indistinguishable at that landmark, this corpus's own framework predicts a small velocity cost.

**And the strongest prior points the same way — F-069.** Wind-up and stretch produce statistically similar velocity and kinematics in professionals, replicated (Fleisig 2024; Escamilla 2026 dirt-mound replication). **Removing the entire wind-up — a far larger kinematic subtraction than shortening a leg lift — costs a professional nothing.** The prior on "2–4 mph" in an elite arm should therefore be **low**, and the folklore figure should be called folklore.

⚠️ **The honest counter, which is Dispute #24:** wind-up → stretch changes what happens *before* the leg lift; slide step changes the lift *itself* and compresses the time into foot contact. Different operations. The F-069 prior may not transfer. **Nobody has measured it at 85+ — and the pitcher in front of you can, today, which is more than the literature offers.**

## 4. TRANSLATE — the coach interrogates the other two

**Q1 (to the biomechanist): You are telling me a tenth of a second is fifteen points of stolen-base success. I have never once seen a pitcher clean up his time and watch his caught-stealing numbers move. Which of us is wrong?**
Neither. §3d. The runner chooses. Your improvement is collected in attempts that never happen, and the attempts that remain are the ones from the fastest runners — so the success rate against you can stay flat or go **up** while you are getting better. **The success rate is a selected statistic.** And §3f says even if it *did* move, you would need ~400 attempts to see it: 13 to 40 seasons.
> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Nobody is going to steal fewer bases off you in a way you'll see in a stat line. What changes is how many even try. Your number is the stopwatch, not the scoreboard."*
> **The drill is:** the §5 bullpen — 25 from his normal stretch, 25 quick, two hand-timers on every pitch.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a long, slow, vertical leg lift with the hands travelling *up* before anything travels *forward*; and at 240 fps, a back hip that has not started toward the plate by the time the lift peaks (F-204, F-205).
> **Here's how we know it's working:** five timed pitches per condition detects 0.15 s. Five. Retime him once a week in his bullpen, ten pitches, and plot it.

**Q2 (to the anatomist): If I take away his leg lift, am I taking away velocity? Everybody in the game says two to four miles an hour.**
The folklore has no sample size behind it anywhere (§2f). The one peer-reviewed manipulation reports 1.7 mph in a sample averaging **76 mph and 17.6 years old** (§3g) and does not report whether that difference was even tested. **And your own corpus has the better prior: F-069 says wind-up and stretch are statistically indistinguishable in professionals, replicated twice — you can delete the entire wind-up from a pro and he throws the same.** Removing the last part of a leg lift is a smaller subtraction than that.
**The mechanism reason:** the leg lift does not make velocity. Velocity is made between foot contact and release — arm cocking is 100–150 ms, acceleration 30–50 ms (F-088). The lift positions the athlete to arrive at foot contact with momentum and a stretched front side. **A slide step that preserves what happens at foot contact costs nothing; a slide step that makes him rush and land early costs plenty.** That is why the grey literature's own best line — *"if you're losing four, the problem is sequencing"* — is the one folklore claim worth keeping. It is not a claim about the slide step; it is a claim about what he does with it.
> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The lift isn't where your velo comes from. Your velo comes from what happens after your front foot lands. We're changing what happens before that, and we're going to check whether it costs you anything — today."*
> **The drill is:** the same bullpen, radar on. **On video the failure looks like:** the front foot down *early* with the throwing arm still low and behind, the trunk opening before the pelvis has finished (F-107, the one supported sequence fault).
> **How we know it's working:** 23 fastballs per condition detects 1.0 mph. He will throw 25. **Under 1 mph, take it. Over 2 mph, don't.**

**Q3 (to the biomechanist): I have a guy who throws over four times an inning because the runner is "bothering him." What is that worth?**
Approximately nothing, and under a disengagement limit it goes sharply negative. §3e: a pickoff throw records an out about 1–2% of the time, worth ≈ **+0.007 runs**, against a throwing-error risk of the same order. **Direct EV ≈ zero.** And the **third** disengagement needs a **28.5% pickoff success rate to break even** — twenty times what he has. The whole justification for throwing over is that it shortens the lead, and **a foot of lead is 0.037 seconds**, which is real but unmeasured by anybody, anywhere (F-324).
> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Your two step-offs are ammunition, not a nervous habit. When they're gone, you're throwing to the plate. The third one is a free base unless you have him dead."*
> **The card rule:** never make the third disengagement unless the pickoff is essentially certain.
> **How we know it's working:** count disengagements per plate appearance in the game log. **This is a compliance check, not an outcome check** — the F-273 discipline exactly. You verify he followed the rule; you never try to verify the rule paid.

**Q4 (to both): I have a finite number of weeks. Where does this rank?**
**Fifth of five, on runs — and first of five, on certainty.** Per college season: 0-2 counts ~0.2 R; **the running game 0.4–1.7 R**; times through the order ~1.0 R; a sequencing tendency 0.8–6.4 R; half an inch of catcher framing ~8 R. **It is a middling prize.** But it is the only one on that list where you can (a) change the thing directly, (b) measure the change the same day, (c) measure the cost of the change the same day, and (d) hand the athlete a number he can feel. **Everything else on the list is a prize you can win without ever knowing you won it.**
> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"This is worth about a run a season. That's not nothing and it's not your career. The reason we're doing it Tuesday instead of arguing about it all spring is that it takes forty minutes and then we know."*

**Q5 (to the anatomist): Does a quicker delivery cost him anything in tissue?**
One line, per the standing scope rule: the manipulated study reports differences between deliveries at **maximum external rotation** — plane of elevation, elevation, axial rotation — while reporting none at foot contact or release. **That is the window where valgus load peaks (F-088, F-097), so a slide step that alters the arm's path at MER is a plausible load change and nobody has measured it at 85+.** Not a reason to avoid it; a reason not to install it in October and ride it for eight months untracked. Moving on.

## 5. WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK

**One bullpen. Two blocks. Both sides of the trade, with real confidence intervals, in forty minutes.**

1. **25 fastballs from his normal stretch.** Radar on. **Two hand-timers on every pitch** — first movement to ball in the mitt.
2. **25 fastballs from the quick delivery he would actually use in a game.** Same everything.
3. Compare the means. **5 timings settle the delivery question; 23 fastballs settle the 1-mph question. He throws 25 of each, so both are powered.**
4. **Decision rule:** under ~1 mph of cost → take it. Over ~2 mph → don't, and buy the run somewhere else. Between 1 and 2 → a real judgement call, and §3f's table tells you which way your own situation pushes.

⚠️ **Two timers, not one.** Hand timing carries 0.03–0.05 s of its own error, a meaningful fraction of a 0.15 s effect. Average two operators, or count frames at 240 fps (F-204, F-205).
⚠️ **Do not try to read command off this bullpen.** F-310 says a real command change needs 2.4 seasons. Log strike rate as a flag and conclude nothing from it.
⚠️ **Do not check whether it worked by looking at steals against him.** §3d and §3f. That number cannot answer the question in his career, let alone his season.

## 6. CROSS-EXAMINATION

Each agent challenged the others. **Manufactured consensus is a failure state.** Three challenges, one conceded, one defended, one genuinely open.

### 6.1 ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST: "your five-pitch detection number is a magic trick, and the magic is in σ"
**The challenge.** Five pitches to detect 0.15 s rests entirely on a within-pitcher SD of 0.08 s for time to plate, and **that number was not measured, it was assumed** — in a cycle that read nothing, exactly like f₀ in F-307 and the run-value SD in F-270. A pitcher whose delivery time varies by 0.15 s pitch to pitch would need 20+ per condition, and worse, *a pitcher who is deliberately varying his hold times — which is good pitching — has a large σ by design.* The headline claim of the cycle sits on an unverified input.

**The biomechanist's answer — CONCEDED IN PART, and the claim survives the concession.** The σ is indeed assumed, and the report now carries it bracketed: at σ = 0.05 / 0.08 / 0.10 s, detecting 0.15 s takes **2 / 5 / 7** pitches. **The conclusion is not sensitive.** To push the requirement to even 50 pitches per condition, σ would have to be **0.27 s** — larger than the entire span between an elite delivery and a slow one (§3a). **That is the difference between this input and f₀: f₀ scaled the answer linearly, and σ here is inside a square root against an effect that is enormous relative to any plausible value.** The conceded part is real and now stated: **the deliberate hold-time variation objection is correct and changes the protocol** — time him on pitches where he is *trying* to be quick, not on a mixed block. That is now in §5.

### 6.2 BIOMECHANIST → COACH: "you cannot call the running game 'the one measurable topic' and then admit the topic isn't measurable"
**The challenge.** §3f concedes that attempt rate needs 2–3 seasons and success rate needs 13–40. So the coach is selling a detection win on a **proxy** — the stopwatch — while the actual quantity of interest, runs allowed to the running game, is as invisible as everything else. **How is that different from F-282, where the corpus talked itself into a manufactured-count drill on a transfer assumption nobody has tested?**

**The coach's answer — DEFENDED, and the distinction is the point of the cycle.** F-282's weakness was that the *link* between the drill and the outcome was assumed (F-197: no bullpen-to-game command transfer study exists). **Here the link is not assumed, it is arithmetic.** §3a is a subtraction. Time to plate does not *proxy* for the margin — it is a **term in the margin**, with a coefficient of exactly 1.0, by the definition of the quantity. There is no transfer question between "he is 0.15 s quicker" and "the margin is 0.15 s smaller"; they are the same sentence. **What remains unmeasurable is how much the *opponent* concedes in response, which is a fact about opponents, not about the pitcher.** So the honest claim is narrower than the headline and is now written that way in F-320: **the wall disappears for the lever and not for the topic.**

### 6.3 COACH → ANATOMIST: "you used F-069 to kill the velocity worry. I think you are stretching it."
**The challenge.** F-069 says wind-up ≈ stretch in professionals. The anatomist used it as the prior for slide step ≈ stretch. **Those are not the same subtraction.** Wind-up → stretch removes a rocker step and a turn *before* the leg lift; slide step removes the lift *itself* and compresses the time into foot contact. If the lift's job is to deliver momentum and a stretched front side *at* foot contact — which is the anatomist's own mechanism in Q2 — then the slide step attacks the mechanism and the wind-up does not. **You are borrowing the authority of a replicated result for a different operation.**

**The anatomist's answer — CONCEDED IN PART, and it becomes Dispute #24.** The two subtractions are genuinely different and the corpus should not treat the F-069 prior as settling the slide step. **What survives:** the manipulated study reports **no differences at foot contact or release** between the two deliveries, which is direct evidence — in the wrong sample — that the slide step does *not* attack the mechanism. **What does not survive:** any claim that the slide step is free at 85+. **Nobody has measured it, in this population, ever.** Both agents then agreed on the practical resolution, which is the reason the cycle's recommendation is a measurement rather than a prescription: **the disagreement is settled for one athlete in one bullpen, and is not settled for anybody else at all.**
