### 1.1 Oshikawa et al. (2020) — pressure was MANIPULATED in elite college baseball players, and performance went UP

**Oshikawa T, Morimoto Y, Adachi G, Akuzawa H, Kaneoka K (2020).** *Changes in lumbar kinematics and trunk muscle electromyographic activity during baseball batting under psychological pressure.* **International Biomechanics 7(1):66–75.** doi `10.1080/23335432.2020.1811765`. PMCID **PMC8130725**, PMID 33998388. **READ IN FULL.**

**Design — and this is why it matters.** n = 14 male collegiate players, age 21 ± 1, height 173.4 ± 3.6 cm, weight 74.6 ± 5.3 kg, **13 ± 1 years of baseball experience, training 4 h/day 6 days/week on an elite collegiate team** (Waseda). Within-subject, three conditions:

1. **Non-pressure** — "perform five swings similar to their usual practice."
2. **Pressure** — reward varied by **±1 USD per ±1 km/h** of mean bat-tip velocity against their own non-pressure baseline.
3. **Emphasized pressure** — after being shown a **false result** claiming they had beaten baseline by 3 km/h, told that beating the pressure condition would raise the reward again.

**This is a MANIPULATION, not an observation.** The corpus almost never gets one.

**The manipulation check passed, hard.** STAI Y-1: **32.4 ± 6.4 → 45.4 ± 6.9 → 44.4 ± 7.1**; F(2,26) = 36.545, p < 0.001, **partial η² = 0.738**. These players were genuinely anxious.

**And they got BETTER, not worse:**

| Outcome | Non-pressure | Pressure | Emphasized | p | partial η² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bat-tip peak velocity (m/s)** | 30.4 ± 3.2 | 32.6 ± 4.7 | **34.1 ± 3.1** ᵃ | 0.003 | 0.367 |
| **Swing-phase duration (ms)** | 257.9 ± 48.7 | 219.0 ± 33.5 ᵇ | **212.5 ± 28.4** ᵇ | <0.001 | 0.609 |
| Peak lumbar flexion (°) | 27.9 ± 8.0 | 32.4 ± 9.4 ᵃ | 35.3 ± 9.8 ᵃ | <0.001 | 0.606 |
| ROM lumbar ext/flex (°) | 24.1 ± 7.1 | 29.8 ± 8.1 ᵃ | 31.2 ± 8.8 ᵃ | <0.001 | 0.692 |
| Peak ang. vel. lumbar extension (°/s) | 234.4 ± 69.4 | 278.0 ± 72.4 ᵃ | 285.4 ± 84.7 ᵃ | 0.003 | 0.450 |
| Peak ang. vel. lumbar flexion (°/s) | 170.9 ± 56.4 | 208.3 ± 63.0 ᵃ | 235.1 ± 97.1 ᵃ | 0.019 | 0.362 |

ᵃ significantly higher/larger than non-pressure; ᵇ significantly shorter than non-pressure.

**+3.7 m/s of bat-tip velocity is +12.2%. The swing phase compressed by 45.4 ms — 17.6%.**

**THE NULLS, which the paper reports against its own hypothesis and which matter as much:** peak lumbar *rotation* angle (lead 11.6 → 15.1°, p = 0.076; back 8.8 → 8.5°, p = 0.228), peak rotation *angular velocity* (lead p = 0.201, back p = 0.072), lateral-flexion ROM (p = 0.745), and **external and internal oblique EMG — no change under any pressure condition.** The authors' own explanation: EO/IO activity "was already extremely high in the non-pressure condition, and could not increase."

**What actually rose in EMG:** bilateral **lumbar erector spinae** in the swing and follow-through phases, emphasized-pressure vs non-pressure only (lead p = 0.009, back p = 0.019).

### 1.2 Aoyama et al. (2021) — the yips is NOT mostly an anxiety phenomenon, by its own trigger data

**Aoyama T, Ae K, Souma H, Miyata K, Kajita K, Kawamura T, Iwai K (2021).** *Difference in Personality Traits and Symptom Intensity According to the Trigger-Based Classification of Throwing Yips in Baseball Players.* **Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 3:652792.** doi `10.3389/fspor.2021.652792`. PMCID **PMC8424038**, PMID 34514382. **READ IN FULL.**

n = **107** college players, **First Division of the Tokyo Metropolitan Area Collegiate Baseball League** (the Japanese D1 equivalent), age 20.3 ± 1.3, 12.5 ± 2.1 years' experience. Anonymous questionnaire, August 2019.

**Classification by trigger of initial onset:**

| Group | n | % of all 107 | % of yips cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychologically triggered yips | 19 | 17.8% | **38.8%** |
| Non-psychologically triggered yips | 25 | 23.4% | **51.0%** |
| Pain-triggered yips | 5 | 4.7% | 10.2% |
| Non-yips | 55 | 51.4% | — |

**49 of 107 players — 45.8% — reported a yips history. Only 19 of those 49 (38.8%) had a psychological trigger at all.**

And the inside of the psychological group is not what the coaching folklore says either. Its triggers were: **"experience of throwing error" n = 11**, "anxiety about throwing" n = 5, "reprimand from others" n = 2, "fear-related throwing experience" n = 1. **The single most common psychological trigger is a physical event — a bad throw — not free-floating anxiety.**

The non-psychological group is stranger still: only **3 of 25** were triggered by a change in throwing mechanics. **22 of 25 had no identifiable trigger of any kind.**

Other read numbers: median initial onset **age 17** (i.e. high school, inside this corpus's population window); median duration 10 months (psych, IQR 1.5–37) vs 5.5 months (non-psych, IQR 2.25–11.5), n.s.; symptoms still present at survey 11/19 vs 9/25 (p = 0.149); acute vs gradual development 9/10 vs 5/20 (**p = 0.054** — a trend, not a result, toward psych-triggered cases being acute).

### 1.3 Aoyama et al. (2024) — yips throwers are not more anxious, and have no motor signature when asymptomatic

**Aoyama T, Ae K, Taguchi T, Kawamori Y, Sasaki D, Kawamura T, Kohno Y (2024).** *Spatiotemporal patterns of throwing muscle synergies in yips-affected baseball players.* **Scientific Reports 14:2649.** doi `10.1038/s41598-024-52332-9`. PMCID **PMC10834996**, PMID 38302478. **READ IN FULL.**

n = 22 college players (12 yips, 10 control), age 20.2 ± 1.2, experience 12.0 ± 1.5 y. 13 ipsilateral upper-extremity EMG channels, VICON at 250 Hz, **full-effort throws from the stretch to a bullseye 5 m away** — a distance the authors deliberately chose because their own earlier survey found symptoms far more likely at ≤10 m than ≥50 m. Muscle synergies by non-negative matrix factorization.

**THREE RESULTS, all of them useful and two of them nulls:**

1. **ANXIETY NULL.** State anxiety yips **34.1 ± 5.9** vs control **34.5 ± 5.3**, t(20) = 0.172, **p = 0.865**. Trait anxiety **45.2 ± 5.4** vs **43.5 ± 4.9**, t(20) = 0.753, **p = 0.460**. *The yips group was not measurably more anxious than the control group on either scale.*
2. **SYNERGY NULL.** Four muscle synergies extracted in both groups; **no significant difference in spatial or temporal pattern between groups.** Phase proportions also null: early cocking 81.9% vs 83.6% (p = 0.628), late cocking 14.9% vs 13.9% (p = 0.821), acceleration 3.5% vs 2.8% (p = 0.203).
3. **THE CONDITIONAL, which is the finding.** Only **3 of 12** yips players reproduced symptoms in the lab, despite the short-distance design chosen to provoke them. A blinded neurologist confirmed objective dystonic movement in all three. **Deviant synergy patterns appeared in 2 of those 3 — and in none of the 9 whose symptoms did not appear.** Players whose symptoms were not reproduced were motorically indistinguishable from controls.

Typing: 4 Type I (focal dystonia), 6 Type II (choking), 2 Type III (both).

Limitations the authors state: only 3 successful trials each (mean 6.2 attempts), to limit fatigue and arm risk; one of the three symptomatic players (Y-1) showed no distinctive synergy pattern.

## 2. THE THREE AGENTS

### 2.1 ANATOMY-PHYSIOLOGY — why pressure should raise power and lower precision, and it is the same mechanism

The Oshikawa result is not a surprise to a physiologist; it is the expected sign. An acute stress response is **sympathoadrenal**: circulating catecholamines, raised motor-neuron pool excitability, lowered recruitment threshold, raised firing rate, and a documented shift in the excitation state of the corticospinal tract. Everything in that list makes **maximal voluntary output go up**. This is the same axis that produces the "throw harder in the ninth" phenomenon every coach has seen and few can explain.

But the identical mechanism degrades precision, and for a reason that is structural rather than psychological. **Force output and force *variability* scale together.** Signal-dependent noise in motor output means that when you raise the drive, you raise the variance of the drive. A system that is running at a higher recruitment level with a higher firing rate is a system whose trial-to-trial endpoint scatter is larger. **You cannot get the velocity gift without the command tax — they are the same neural event viewed at two scales.**

Second mechanism, and this is the one the Oshikawa data actually shows: **the fast subsystems are already saturated and the slow ones are not.** The obliques did not increase under pressure, and the authors are right about why — they were already near ceiling in the non-pressure condition. What *did* increase was lumbar erector spinae activity and sagittal-plane range. **Pressure does not add output to the structures already maximal in a full-effort throw. It adds output to the structures that had headroom.** For a pitcher that predicts something specific: under pressure, the arm does not get more aggressive (it cannot; it is already at the tissue's limit at 85+), but the **trunk and the lower half do**, and that is a timing change, not a strength change.

Third: the yips data forces a reclassification. **The Aoyama 2021 trigger split (61% non-psychological, 22 of 25 non-psych cases with no trigger at all) plus the Aoyama 2024 STAI null is the profile of a task-specific dystonia, not of an anxiety disorder.** Focal task-specific dystonias — writer's cramp, embouchure dystonia, musician's dystonia — are disorders of **sensorimotor cortical representation**, characterised by degraded surround inhibition and blurred somatotopic maps after long periods of highly repetitive, highly stereotyped practice. The demographics fit: median onset **age 17**, after roughly a decade of daily repetition of one movement. The "anxiety" is downstream of the motor failure at least as often as upstream of it — consistent with the most common psychological trigger being **"experience of throwing error" (n = 11), i.e. the bad throw came first.**

**The anatomist's verdict:** one third to one half of the players carrying a "mental" label in a college clubhouse have a movement-system problem that a sport psychologist is not equipped to fix, and that a mechanics coach cannot see (§1.3 result 2). That is a triage failure, and triage is cheap.

### 2.2 BIOMECHANIST — what the numbers will and will not carry

**On Oshikawa (§1.1). I will defend the manipulation and attack the interpretation.**

Defend: a within-subject design with a 13-point STAI shift and partial η² = 0.738 on the manipulation check is a real manipulation in a genuinely elite sample (4 h/day, 6 d/wk). The corpus has very few of these. The **direction** — power up, timing compressed — is worth carrying.

Attack, and these are fatal to the magnitudes:

1. **THE ORDER CONFOUND IS TOTAL.** The authors state it plainly: *"the trials of each condition could not be performed randomly because the psychological pressure must be increased gradually."* Non-pressure always came first. So the monotone sequence of bat-tip velocity 30.4 → 32.6 → 34.1 m/s is **exactly what progressive warm-up produces** in a task preceded by an "optional" 10-minute warm-up. There is no counterbalanced arm and no way to separate the two. **The +12% is an upper bound on a pressure effect and is equally consistent with zero pressure effect.**
2. **INCENTIVE IS NOT ANXIETY, AND THE STAI CANNOT TELL THEM APART HERE.** The reward was tied *directly and explicitly to bat-tip velocity*. Being paid per km/h and swinging harder is a **motivation** result. The STAI rose, but it would rise under either account. The study is therefore not evidence that *anxiety* raises output; it is evidence that **an output-aligned incentive raises output**, which is not news, and which is the opposite of the game situation where the incentive is aligned to *outcome*, not to effort.
3. **THE TASK IS A DRY SWING.** No ball, no pitcher, self-paced timing, five repetitions, middle three analysed. The authors chose this deliberately for kinematic repeatability, and that choice removes the entire **precision** half of the task. **A study with no accuracy outcome cannot speak to choking, and choking is what a coach means by pressure.**
4. **n = 14**, and the power analysis targeted a partial η² of 0.14 — so the study was designed to detect only large effects, and the near-misses (lumbar rotation p = 0.076, back-side rotation velocity p = 0.072) are uninterpretable rather than null.
5. **It is BATTING.** The kinematic bridge to pitching is an analogy, not a measurement.

**On Bosshard & Gomez (§1.4).** The honest headline is not d = 0.23. It is **d = 0.14 after trim-and-fill, with a prediction interval of [−0.18, 0.65]**. A prediction interval that crosses zero means: *a competently run future trial has a material chance of finding this makes performance worse.* And the d = 0.45 for "mixed" interventions — the number the industry will quote, because it is the biggest and it describes what practitioners actually do — **rests on k = 5.** Quote the prediction interval or quote nothing.

**On Hase et al. (§1.5).** 57 of 62 studies observational. When the authors moderated by risk of bias, **the low-risk-of-bias studies produced the weaker association.** That is the classic signature of an effect that shrinks as measurement improves. Combined with CO reactivity failing to reach significance and CTI-before-performance at r = .08, the defensible statement is: *challenge/threat state is weakly associated with performance, mostly measured concurrently, and has been directly manipulated five times.*

**On Su et al. (§1.6).** Already dispatched in §1.6. The generalisable lesson is the one this corpus keeps relearning: **a control group that improves is a finding, and the abstract is where it goes to die.** Nothing was fabricated here. A real study reported a real table and then described it wrongly in the one sentence everybody reads. **Reading the table is not optional, and this is the second time in three cycles that reading the table changed the answer** (cf. F-353, F-354).

**And on restriction of range, which applies to all of it.** Every psychological correlate above was measured in samples spanning a wide skill range. In an all-85+ population, the anxiety-to-performance correlation will collapse the way every other correlation collapses (Luera 2020, r = .17–.29). **A pitcher who has survived to a D1 roster at 90 mph has already been filtered on the ability to execute while nervous.** The variance available for a mental-skills intervention to act on is smaller in this population than in any sample in any of these papers.

### 2.3 PITCHING-COACH — what survives to the bullpen

Four things survive, and the fourth is the only one I would spend a training block on.

**(a) Stop telling him pressure makes him worse.** It does not, for the thing he is most worried about. Every velocity-facing mechanism in §2.1 says the adrenaline is on his side, and the one manipulated study in an elite college sample found output **up 12%**. Guys throw harder in games than in bullpens; that is real, it is expected, and telling a pitcher he is going to fall apart in the eighth is manufacturing a problem the physiology does not have.

**(b) The tax is on command, and he should be told that explicitly, because it changes what he does with it.** "Pressure makes me worse" is unusable. **"Pressure buys me a mile and a half and charges me the corner"** is usable — it says *stop trying to paint, go get the middle-third strike, let the extra velocity do the work.* That is an in-game decision rule, not a feeling.

**(c) The pre-pitch breath is defensible, but not for the reason it is sold.** F-330 already established the seconds are metabolically almost free. The HRVB study (§1.6) shows breathing training moves **anxiety and breathing rate** — its best-supported results — and does **not** show it moves performance. So the honest pitch to a pitcher is: *this makes the inning feel manageable, and we do not have evidence it changes the pitch.* Sold that way it costs nothing and does not set up a broken promise. Sold as a performance lever it is a promise the literature cannot keep.

**(d) THE ONE THING I WOULD ACTUALLY DO THIS WEEK — triage the "mental" guy.** §1.2 and §1.3 together say that of the players in my bullpen carrying a "he's in his own head" label, **roughly three in five never had a psychological trigger, and as a group they are not measurably more anxious than everyone else.** The current response to those players — more mental-skills work, more talking about it, more confidence cues — is aimed at a mechanism that is absent in the majority of them. And §1.3 result 2 says I cannot find it on video: **when the symptom is not occurring, the motor pattern is indistinguishable from a healthy thrower's.** So filming a clean throw and studying it is wasted film. **You have to capture the episode, or you have nothing.**

## 3. THE COACH'S FOUR QUESTIONS

**Q1 — "He's 92 in the pen and 94 in the first inning. Is that adrenaline, and should I trust it?"**

*Answer:* the direction is supported and the magnitude is not. Oshikawa's manipulation produced +12% peak output in an elite college sample with a verified 13-point STAI shift — but the order confound (§2.2) means the honest range on a pressure effect runs from zero to that. **What is not in doubt is the physiology (§2.1): the sympathoadrenal response raises maximal voluntary output, and that mechanism does not care whether the task is a bat swing or a fastball.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The nerves are worth about a mile and a half to you. That's real, it's free, and it's yours. What they cost you is the edges."*
> **The drill is:** no drill — this is a measurement, not a cue. Radar the bullpen and radar the start.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing. There is no failure here. The failure is the *coach's* — talking a pitcher out of an advantage by warning him about pressure.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** see §3.1 below. **~16–23 fastballs per setting.** One bullpen and one start.

**Q2 — "Then why does he miss arm-side when it matters?"**

*Answer:* signal-dependent noise (§2.1) — raising the drive raises the variance of the drive. And Oshikawa gives the kinematic shape of it: the **swing phase compressed by 17.6%** while the obliques, already at ceiling, did not change. The analogous prediction for a pitcher is that **foot-contact-to-release shortens under pressure** while the arm cannot contribute any more than it already does. Earlier-than-usual rotation with the same arm is a timing mismatch, and a timing mismatch is a release-point problem, and a release-point problem is a command problem. **⚠️ This is an analogy from batting, it has not been measured in throwing, and it is the single most valuable thing anyone reading this could go measure.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Under pressure your lower half speeds up and your arm can't. Give it somewhere to go — middle-third, and let it run."*
> **The drill is:** high-leverage bullpen with the target moved to the middle third, not the corner. Manufacture the leverage (F-282's manufactured-count block is the existing template); do not manufacture a smaller target.
> **On video the failure looks like:** at 240 fps, foot-contact-to-release shorter in the manufactured-leverage set than the routine set, with the trunk arriving early and the arm in the same place it always is. **Compare the two sets on the same day, same camera position.**
> **Here's how we know it's working:** honestly — *within one season, we probably don't.* See §3.2.

**Q3 — "The kid who can't throw it back to the pitcher. Mental guy, right? Send him to the sport psych?"**

*Answer:* **maybe, and the point is that you are currently guessing.** 45.8% of a Japanese D1-equivalent roster reported a yips history; **61% of those cases had no psychological trigger**, 22 of 25 non-psychological cases had **no trigger at all**, and the yips group's state and trait anxiety were statistically indistinguishable from controls (p = 0.865, p = 0.460). Meanwhile the most common *psychological* trigger was **a bad throw (n = 11)**, not anxiety (n = 5) — the motor event came first.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing about his head, until I know. *"Let's find out when it happens and what it looks like. Not why."*
> **The drill is:** a **trigger and distance audit**, one page. Every occurrence for two weeks: distance, intent level, who was watching, game or practice, and whether it was acute onset or crept in. Aoyama's own design says to bias the sampling to **short distances (≤10 m)** and full intent — that is where symptoms reproduce.
> **On video the failure looks like** — *and this is the finding* — **like nothing at all, unless you catch the episode.** Aoyama 2024: nine of twelve yips players were motorically identical to controls when asymptomatic. **Film of a clean throw is not diagnostic. Stop collecting it.**
> **Here's how we know it's working:** we have a classification, and a referral that matches it. Acute onset traceable to a specific throwing event, symptoms tied to being watched → sport psychology. Gradual onset, no trigger, short-distance-and-low-intent specific, visible involuntary movement → **that is a task-specific dystonia presentation and it is a neurology referral, not a confidence problem.** Median onset age 17 means you will see this in freshmen.

**Q4 — "Breathing work and the 'treat nerves as excitement' thing. Worth a block?"**

*Answer:* **worth five minutes, not a block.** Arousal reappraisal's best randomized evidence is **d = 0.23 falling to d = 0.14 after correcting for publication bias, with a prediction interval of [−0.18, 0.65]** — and **not one motor-skill subgroup existed to analyse**, because there were not enough motor-skill studies to make one. It is an effect established on maths exams and public speaking. HRVB moved anxiety and breathing rate in college players and **did not** move hitting once you read the control column.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"The breath is for how the inning feels. I'm not going to tell you it adds a strike, because nobody has shown that."*
> **The drill is:** keep it inside the between-innings routine he already has (F-358: take the break, warm up at the end of it). It costs nothing there.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a pre-pitch routine that has visibly compressed by the fifth inning — which F-336 already identified as a **same-night-detectable fatigue tell**, at **31 charted pitches**. That is a better use of the same observation.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** it isn't, at the individual level, and you should not pretend otherwise. Track the thing that *is* detectable — clock margin (F-336) — and let the breathing ride along.

### 3.1 The detection arithmetic — the velocity half IS measurable this week

Two-sample, α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power: **n per group = 2(1.96 + 0.84)²σ²/Δ² = 15.68 σ²/Δ².**

Within-pitcher fastball velocity SD is bracketed by this corpus at **σ = 0.8–1.2 mph** (F-289 — still unpublished anywhere, still sitting in every program's radar log). To detect a **Δ = 1.0 mph** bullpen-to-game difference:

| σ (mph) | Fastballs needed **per setting** |
|---|---|
| 0.8 | **10** |
| 1.0 | **16** |
| 1.2 | **23** |

**One bullpen and one start.** This is the second instrument in the whole corpus that answers inside a week — the first was the running game (F-320). **The arousal-velocity effect is individually measurable and nobody measures it.**

### 3.2 …and the command half is not, and this is the recurring shape

Same formula, zone rate as the outcome, p ≈ 0.5 so σ² = 0.25:

| Δ (percentage points of zone rate) | Pitches needed **per setting** |
|---|---|
| 10 pp | **392** |
| 15 pp | **174** |
| 20 pp | **98** |

**A 10-point zone-rate difference between bullpen and game needs ~392 taken-plus-thrown pitches in each setting.** He does not throw that in a season of bullpens at matched intent. And this is the *optimistic* version — it ignores the catcher contamination F-309 sized, and it ignores that bullpen and game pitches differ in count, hitter presence and selection.

**So the asymmetry, stated plainly and for the fifth time in this corpus: the thing pressure gives you is measurable in one week; the thing pressure costs you is not measurable at all at the individual level.** F-287 (times through the order), F-295 (the transition matrix), F-307 (location scatter) and F-320 (the running game) are all the same shape. **Any coach who tells you he has *seen* a pitcher's command degrade under pressure has seen roughly 30 pitches and a memory.**

## 4. CROSS-EXAMINATION

**① BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST.** *"Your signal-dependent-noise story is doing a lot of work and it is not in evidence anywhere in what we read today. Not one of these six papers measured throwing accuracy under a pressure manipulation. You are importing a motor-control principle from laboratory isometric tasks and asserting it holds at a 95 mph release. What would settle it: a within-subject bullpen with manufactured leverage and TrackMan release-point scatter as the outcome — release-point SD, not zone rate, because §3.2 says zone rate cannot pay for itself."*

**ANATOMIST — CONCEDES THE EVIDENCE, DEFENDS THE PRIOR.** Correct that nothing today measured it. Signal-dependent noise is about as well-established as anything in motor control, but the biomechanist is right that its magnitude at maximal-effort whole-body throwing is unmeasured, and that "well-established in general" is exactly the move this corpus keeps catching other people making. **Downgraded to MECHANISM, and the accuracy claim in §2.1 should be read as a prediction, not a finding.** The release-point-SD design is accepted as the settling test and goes to §6.

**② COACH → BIOMECHANIST.** *"You dismantled Oshikawa on the order confound and then told me to keep the direction. Which is it? Because if the +12% is warm-up, then my answer to Q1 — 'the nerves are worth a mile and a half' — is me telling a pitcher something you just told me is unsupported."*

**BIOMECHANIST — CONCEDES, AND THE ANSWER TO Q1 MUST BE REWORDED.** The coach has caught a real inconsistency. What survives Oshikawa is **the sign, and only weakly**. The *strong* support for "pressure raises output" is the physiology (§2.1) plus the universal coaching observation, neither of which is a controlled measurement. **The correct thing to tell a pitcher is "the nerves are not going to take velocity away from you" — a statement about the sign, and nothing about the size. "A mile and a half" is a number this corpus has not earned and should not be said out loud.** §3.1 is how he would earn it for his own athlete, which is the whole point of putting the arithmetic there.

**③ ANATOMIST → COACH.** *"You want to send the gradual-onset, no-trigger, involuntary-movement player to neurology. On what? Twelve yips players, three of whom reproduced symptoms, two of whom showed a synergy deviation. That is a referral pathway built on n = 2. And a 45.8% prevalence figure from an anonymous self-report questionnaire on one Japanese league is not a prevalence figure — it is a response rate to a question about a word."*

**COACH — CONCEDES THE n = 2 AND DEFENDS THE TRIAGE.** The synergy result is n = 2 and I will not build anything on it. But the referral does not rest on it. It rests on **the trigger split (25 of 49 non-psychological, 22 of those with no trigger at all) and the STAI null (p = 0.865 / p = 0.460)**, which are the whole samples, not the subgroup. And the anatomist's framing has it backwards on cost: **I am not diagnosing anything. I am declining to send a player with no psychological trigger and unremarkable anxiety to an intervention aimed at psychological triggers and anxiety.** The prevalence caveat is conceded and written into F-368 — self-reported yips history on one league is not an elite-American-baseball prevalence estimate.

**④ COACH → ANATOMIST (second challenge).** *"You called this a task-specific dystonia. Writer's cramp in a throwing arm. If that is right, the standard of care includes botulinum toxin and sensorimotor retraining, and I have just handed a nineteen-year-old a neurological diagnosis on the strength of an analogy. What stops me?"*

**ANATOMIST — CONCEDES THE OVERREACH.** The dystonia framing is a **hypothesis with a matching demographic profile (onset ~17, ~decade of stereotyped repetition) and a matching symptom profile (task-specific, distance-specific, intent-specific, involuntary)**, and Aoyama's own Type I/II/III scheme already carries it. **It is not a diagnosis and no coach should voice the word to a player.** The correct coaching act is narrower than what §2.3(d) implied and §5 records the correction: *refer to a physician and say what you observed — do not name a condition.* Redrafted that way in F-370's COACHING field.

**⑤ BIOMECHANIST → COACH.** *"Your §3.1 table is the best thing in this brief and you have quietly changed what it measures. Bullpen-versus-game is not a pressure manipulation. It differs in warm-up state, adrenaline, mound, hitter presence, catcher, ball, time of day and what he is trying to do. You have sixteen fastballs' worth of power to detect a difference you cannot attribute."*

**COACH — CONCEDES, AND IT SHARPENS THE RECOMMENDATION.** Fair, and it is the same confound that killed Oshikawa. **The fix is within-outing, not bullpen-versus-game: high-leverage versus low-leverage fastballs inside the same starts**, which holds warm-up, mound, ball and day constant and lets leverage vary. Accumulating 16–23 high-leverage fastballs takes a few starts rather than one, and it still lands inside a season. **F-374 is written to the within-outing version, not the bullpen version.** *This exchange changed the recommendation, which is why it is here.*

### Manufactured consensus check
**Not reached, and one disagreement is genuinely open.** The anatomist and the biomechanist do not agree on whether the command tax is established or predicted. The anatomist conceded the grade and kept the prior; the biomechanist accepts the mechanism and denies it has a magnitude. **That is an unresolved dispute, not a compromise, and it goes to `open-disputes.md` as Dispute #27.**

## 8. TOMORROW — three questions handed forward

1. **Does foot-contact-to-release actually compress under leverage in a thrower?** Oshikawa found a **17.6% swing-phase compression** in batting. The pitching analogue is unmeasured anywhere, it is the mechanism behind the entire command-tax argument, and **it needs a high-speed camera and two manufactured-leverage bullpens, not a lab.** Currently the cheapest unanswered question in the topic.
2. **What is the release-point SD difference between high- and low-leverage fastballs in the same outing?** §3.2 says zone rate cannot pay for itself; **release-point scatter is a continuous measure with far more information per pitch**, and it is already in every TrackMan log. *Fifth entry in the pattern of F-264, F-289, F-295 and F-307: already in every program's own data, and nobody has published it.*
3. **Can the arousal-reappraisal effect be shown on ANY motor skill?** §1.4 could not form a motor-skill subgroup because the studies do not exist. Until one does, the industry's most confident mental cue rests on maths exams. **Recorded as a gap in the literature, searched and confirmed absent within the k = 44 of a 2024 meta-analysis of RCTs.**
