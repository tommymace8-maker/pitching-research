## 1. THE ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS — DOES THE TISSUE STORY SURVIVE?

**The mechanism everybody sells.** The pitch is a ground-up kinetic chain: the drive leg produces horizontal
impulse, the lead leg brakes it in ~50–80 ms (F-645), the pelvis and trunk rotate, the arm is the last
segment. Sprinting and jumping train horizontal and vertical impulse. Therefore sprint and jump training
should raise velocity. The argument is coherent and the industry treats it as settled.

**Three places the tissue story is weaker than the diagram.**

**(1) The impulse the pitch needs is small, brief and horizontal — and the lifts people prescribe are none of those.**
Registered already: forward centre-of-mass velocity on a pull-down (with a running start the rules forbid)
correlates with in-game velocity at **r = 0.164, n = 332 D1** (F-740/F-741 topic). If adding a *running
start* buys that little between hard throwers, the marginal value of a faster 30 m is not obviously large.
**MECHANISM, and it argues against the lever, not for it.**

**(2) Sprinting and pitching load the lead leg in different directions.**
Sprint acceleration is sagittal and cyclic; the pitch's lead-leg block is a single frontal/transverse-plane
braking event. The corpus already has the frontal-plane dissociation registered (F-009 — the
lateral-to-medial jump is the only field test correlating with throwing velocity across all four throwing
conditions and in both hands; Lehman 2013, JSCR 27(4):902–908). **If the frontal plane is the one that
carries signal, linear sprint training is the wrong plane.** EMERGING.

**(3) Body mass is doing work in every one of these correlations, and the anatomist has to concede it.**
F-001 (body mass r = 0.58) and F-005 (power per kg **r = 0.19 NS** vs absolute power r = 0.43, p = .014,
n = 33 D1). A heavier athlete sprints with more *momentum* and less *speed*. Any lower-body metric expressed
in absolute terms partly re-measures mass. **This is the lens for F-783, the day's cleanest confound.**

## 2. THE BIOMECHANIST PASS — THE INTERSECTION, AND WHAT IS ACTUALLY IN IT

### 2.1 The headline: the intersection contains TWO trials, and neither is on this population

Fourteen searches, six extended. What exists, in full:

**(a) McEvoy KP & Newton RU (1998), "Baseball throwing speed and base running speed: the effects of ballistic
resistance training," *J Strength Cond Res* 12(4):216–221.** n = **9 training vs 9 control, professional
baseball, age 24 ± 4**, 10-week pre-season, 3 sessions per 2 weeks. Intervention: **explosive squat jumps and
bench throws at ~30–50% 1RM**. **Throwing speed +2.0 ± 1.5% (p ≤ .05); control "no change."** → **F-777.**

This is the best-designed object the topic has and it still cannot answer the question, for four reasons:
1. **Lower body is not isolated.** Squat jumps were bundled with bench throws. The design cannot attribute
   anything to the legs. This is the same confound as every other "positive" program in the registry.
2. **The outcome is a THROW, not a PITCH.** "Throwing speed" in an 18-man professional squad of mixed
   positions is not a mound fastball. Baseline velocity is **unretrievable** — the corpus's standing
   complaint (F-742: zero sample mean velocities in the entire long-toss literature) repeats here.
3. **±1.5% on a +2.0% mean.** At 90 mph that is +1.8 mph with an SD of 1.35 mph across 9 men. Several
   improved nothing.
4. **The significance pattern is the pre-post pattern.** "Training group improved (p ≤ .05); control showed
   no change" is **two within-arm tests**, not a group × time interaction. → **F-779.**

**(b) Makni E, … Hammami R, et al. (2024), *Biology of Sport* 41(4):293–304, PMID 39416498 / PMC11474988 —
"Limb-belt resisted sprint training improves physical fitness and ball-throw velocity in pubertal handball
players."** n = **27, age 14.4**, randomised to **belt-resisted sprint (n = 9) / vest-resisted sprint (n = 8) /
normal sprint (n = 8)**, 6 weeks in-season. Belt group improved **all** tests (moderate-to-large); vest group
improved sprint, standing long jump, five-jump test and **throw velocity** (small-to-large); **the normal-sprint
control improved change-of-direction only.** Across players, **change in throw velocity correlated with change
in sprint time and jump ability, r = 0.40–0.69, p < 0.01.** → **F-778.**

**This is the only study in existence in which sprint training was manipulated and a throwing-velocity outcome
measured, in any sport.** It is also **triple-mismatched**: 14-year-olds, handball, **425 g ball** — Dispute #85's
transfer question, which yesterday's cycle recommended promoting to a standing constraint. And its effects are
reported as **qualitative magnitude labels within arms**, so the between-arm contrast the abstract implies is
not a between-group effect size. Pre-post pattern again.

### 2.4 Two genuinely new objects on lower-limb fatigue — and they contradict F-359

**Chang E-Y, Yeh H-C, Ho C-A, Lau H-T, Wang P-H, Lin W-H, Ho C-S (2026), "Monitoring pitching load and

**So the literature now says three different things about whether pitching fatigues lower-body jump output:**
no (F-359, underpowered), yes-with-stable-velocity (2025, n = 12), yes-with-velocity-loss-only-at-D2

### 2.5 The marker side, and a reliability number the corpus has been asking for

**Navratil J & Pearson J (2026), "Relationship between countermovement jump metrics and fastball velocity in
Division II collegiate pitchers,"** Winona State research-and-creative-achievement conference abstract,
`openriver.winona.edu/rca/2026/schedule/99`. **n = 23 D2 pitchers**, 3 CMJs with **10 s rest**, average
fastball velocity tracked across a fall season. Reported: **"no strong correlation between average fastball
velocities and CMJ force plate metrics,"** and **ICC = 0.36 for Peak Relative Braking Force and 0.36 for Net
Positive Impulse.** → **F-781.**

**⚠️ THIS IS A CONFERENCE ABSTRACT WITH NO RETRIEVABLE SAMPLE OR TABLES. It is not evidence for a magnitude
and is registered as a LEAD.** Two reasons to be careful beyond that: 10 s between CMJ reps is a *within-session*
reliability estimate, which is not the between-day reliability a coach needs; and the abstract's own framing
conflates an ICC with a correlation.

**But the arithmetic it licenses is free and source-independent.** If ICC = 0.36, then
SEM = SD·√(1 − 0.36) = **0.80 SD**, and MDC₉₅ = 1.96·√2·SEM = **2.22 SD**. A metric whose minimal detectable
change is **more than two between-athlete standard deviations cannot track an individual athlete at all.**
If that ICC survives peer review, Peak Relative Braking Force and Net Positive Impulse are unusable for
monitoring — which is precisely the gap the corpus has flagged repeatedly (no ICC/SEM/MDC for pitcher
force-plate metrics from any source).

**And it is the fifth landing of the restriction-of-range prediction (F-750).** Lehman 2013's lateral-to-medial
jump predicted throwing velocity at **R² = 0.322 (r ≈ 0.567)** in a mixed college sample (n = 42). F-750's rule
predicts r ≈ 0.30–0.44 on a higher, narrower staff. **A D2 pitching staff reporting "no strong correlation"
at n = 23 is consistent with exactly that** — though at n = 23 the confidence interval is wide enough that
this is a *directional* landing, not a confirmation, and the thin source makes it weaker still.

### 2.6 F-783 — the cleanest confound of the day, and it needs no source

A Japanese study (n = **31 college pitchers**, Rapsodo, sprint times 10 m → 400 m) reports that ball speed
correlates **positively with MOMENTUM and KINETIC ENERGY over 10/30/50/100 m**, **negatively with running
SPEED over 200 m and 400 m**, and positively with **body weight, BMI, lean body mass, muscle mass and SMI.**

**Read the algebra before reading the conclusion.** Momentum = m·v. Kinetic energy = ½m·v². Both are
**mass-weighted sprint quantities**. The same paper reports that **body mass itself correlates with ball
speed**. So "sprint momentum predicts ball speed" is, to a first approximation, **F-001 restated with a
stopwatch attached** — the same move F-005 already caught in the opposite direction ("dividing power by body
mass deletes the term carrying the signal"). The honest version of the finding is: *heavier pitchers throw
harder and also carry more momentum down a runway.* It is **not** evidence that getting faster over 10 m
raises velocity.

**The negative 200/400 m correlation is the interesting half and points the same way:** the pitchers who
throw hardest are the *worst* longer-distance runners, which is what you expect of bigger, more powerful
athletes — and it is a second, independent reason not to read any of this as a sprint-training instruction.

## 4. THE COACH'S PASS — FOUR POINTED QUESTIONS, ANSWERED, THEN TRANSLATED

### Q1 to the BIOMECHANIST: "I have a 91 mph junior who runs a 7.1 sixty. Do I buy him velocity by making him faster?"

**Answer: unknown, and nobody has ever tested it on anyone like him.** The entire licence for that sentence is
(a) a cross-sectional sprint-ratio marker in a **79.5 mph tryout sample** (F-012, adjusted R² = 0.230), (b) a
mass-weighted sprint correlation that is largely body mass restated (F-783), and (c) one trial in
**14-year-old handball players** (F-778). **Zero manipulations in baseball. Zero at 85+.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "Getting faster is good for your body and good for your draft profile and
> I cannot promise you one mile an hour for it. Nobody has ever tested it on an arm like yours."
> **The drill is:** keep the sprint work — 2× per week, 6–8 × 20–30 m, full recovery, off-season — and keep it
> **because it is cheap, it is low-risk, and it holds general athleticism**, not because it is a velocity
> intervention. **On video the failure looks like:** sprint work crowding the throwing day it sits next to —
> the same-session interference moderator is the one real finding in that literature (F-690).
> **How we know it's working:** his 10 m split, not his velocity. **Do not credit velocity to it.**

### Q2 to the ANATOMIST: "If the lower half matters, why does every positive trial bundle it with upper-body work?"

**Because the field programs it that way.** F-782: 17 of 21 exercises upper-body, 4 lower. McEvoy 1998 paired
squat jumps with bench throws. Escamilla 2012's three arms were Throwers Ten, Keiser and plyometrics — all
shoulder-dominant. **The answer is not that the legs don't matter; it is that nobody has ever run the
experiment that would isolate them.** ESTABLISHED as a structural fact about the literature.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "The leg work is in your program because of how you're built, not because
> a study moved your fastball with it." **The failure mode on video:** a pitcher who has added squat numbers
> and still leaks at the lead leg — strength arriving without a block.

### Q3 to both: "Is the lateral-to-medial jump worth testing, given it's the one that correlated?"

**Yes as a test, no as a target — and expect it to shrink on your staff.** F-009/Lehman 2013 is the only field
test that correlated across all four throwing conditions in both hands, at **R² = 0.322 (r ≈ 0.567)** in a
**mixed college sample, n = 42**. On an 85+ staff, F-750's restriction-of-range rule predicts **r ≈ 0.30–0.44
(R² ≈ 0.09–0.19)** — and the D2 abstract reporting "no strong correlation" (F-781) is consistent with that.
**CROSS_SECTIONAL. It has never been manipulated.**

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** "This jump tells me something about how you're built sideways. It is not
> a thing to chase." **The drill is:** test it, don't train it — one session, 3 attempts per leg, record best.
> **On video the failure looks like:** a big right-left asymmetry on the drive side.
> **How we know it's working:** you don't. **There is no measurable check, and that is the finding.** With
> R² ≈ 0.09–0.19 in our range, detecting that a jump gain carried into velocity needs ~**50–80 athletes**,
> not one staff. F-774's arithmetic applies unchanged.

### Q4 to the BIOMECHANIST: "Chang 2026 says the jump test catches fatigue my radar misses. Do I start jump-testing between outings?"

**Not yet, and the reason is the reliability number, not the fatigue claim.** The fatigue claim has three
incompatible answers in the literature (Dispute #89). But even taking Chang 2026 at face value, **F-781's
ICC = 0.36 implies MDC₉₅ ≈ 2.22 SD** for two of the exact metrics people monitor. A test that cannot detect
a change smaller than two between-athlete SDs cannot tell you whether *this* pitcher is fatigued today.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing — this is a staff decision, not a cue.
> **What I'd actually do:** before monitoring anyone with a force plate, spend **one afternoon** establishing
> your own between-day ICC — 8–10 arms, 3 CMJs, two separate days, same time of day. **That is the cheapest
> decisive measurement available to this program right now and it needs no literature at all.** If your own
> ICC comes back near 0.36, stop; if it comes back above 0.85, the metric is usable and Chang 2026 becomes
> worth acting on. **Sample size to detect a 2-inch command gain is ~200 tracked pitches (standing rule); the
> jump-reliability audit is far cheaper than that and should come first.**
