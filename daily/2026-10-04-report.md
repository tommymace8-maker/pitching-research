# STEP 1 — THE RESEARCH

## 1.1 The calendar is not the lever. Cumulative pitch load is — by 2–3×.

**F-575.** Bradbury & Forman (2012), *J Strength Cond Res* 26(5):1181–1187, PMID 22344048. MLB starting pitchers, 1988–2009, fractional-polynomial multiple regression on next-game ERA.

| Load term | Effect on next-game ERA |
|---|---|
| Each pitch thrown in the **preceding game** | **+0.007** |
| Each pitch averaged over the **preceding 5 games** | **+0.014** |
| Each pitch averaged over the **preceding 10 games** | **+0.022** |
| **Rest days** | **"weakly associated with performance"** |

**The ratio is the finding, not the absolute scale.** The rolling 5-game and 10-game load terms carry **2× and 3.1× the per-pitch coefficient of the last outing.** Translated into the only sentence a coach needs: *what you did two and three weeks ago is worth more than what you did on Friday, and the number of days between Friday and today is worth close to nothing.*

🚨 **BIOMECHANIST'S WARNING, AND IT APPLIES TO ANYONE WHO QUOTES THIS PAPER.** The model is explicitly **fractional polynomial**, i.e. non-linear in the load terms. The per-pitch coefficients are therefore **marginal effects at some point on a curve, not slopes you may multiply out.** "100 pitches → +0.70 ERA next start" is arithmetic the paper does not license, and the full specification needed to do it correctly is behind an egress block. **Quote the ratio. Do not quote a product.**

`CAUSALITY: CROSS_SECTIONAL` — an observational panel over 22 seasons. Nobody assigned pitch counts or rest. Managers remove tiring pitchers early, which **biases the immediate-load coefficient toward zero** (the biggest counts go to the pitchers having the best days) and biases the cumulative term the other way (durable pitchers accumulate load *and* are good). Neither direction is corrected for in anything retrievable.

## 1.2 The rest-day null, with a number attached

**F-576.** Two independent estimates, both pointing at nothing:

- **Peer-reviewed (Bradbury & Forman, above):** each additional rest day ≈ **0.015 ERA improvement, not significant.** Skipping a starter in a five-man rotation — **four extra days** — buys **0.06 ERA.**
- **Non-peer-reviewed (community.fangraphs.com, "The Effect of Rest Days on Starting Pitcher Performance"):** Modified-FIP premium **0.239 on four days' rest, 0.144 on five, 0.291 on six.**

**The coach's reading: four extra days of rest is worth six hundredths of an ERA. That is not a lever; that is a rounding error you can see from space.**

⚠️ **The non-monotonic 4 → 5 → 6 pattern is almost certainly noise and must not be quoted as "five days is optimal."** No confidence intervals, no n, no peer review are retrievable; a U-shape across three adjacent bins with unreported error bars is the single most common way a null gets sold as a finding. The honest statement is that **the two sources agree the effect is small and disagree about its shape, which is what you expect when the effect is zero.**

## 1.3 ⭐ The one place rest *does* move velocity is the reliever — and it is still a marker

**F-577.** *The Hardball Times*, "Do relief pitchers suffer from pitching back-to-back days?" (tht.fangraphs.com). **~300,000 PITCHf/x pitches.** Author and year **not recoverable** through the search layer.

| Usage | Fastball velocity |
|---|---|
| **Back-to-back days** | **−0.5 mph** vs ≥ 1 day rest |
| **Three straight days** | **≈ −1.5 mph** |
| **One or two days' rest** | reported optimum |

**This is the only place in today's sweep where the calendar visibly moves a physical output, and the population is dead-on — MLB relievers, 93–98 mph, well clear of the 85 floor.**

🚨 **AND IT IS A MARKER, NOT A LEVER, FOR a reason that is unusually clean.** Nobody randomised who pitched three days in a row. A manager chooses. The selection runs in both directions at once and they do not cancel: he uses the arm that *looks* fresh (which inflates the back-to-back group's velocity, biasing the estimate toward zero), and he burns the third straight day when the pen is already empty and the game is out of hand (which deflates it). **"Give him a day and he gets half a tick back" is an inference the design cannot support.** What the design *does* support is a monitoring rule: a reliever on his third straight day is, on average, a 1.5-mph-slower pitcher than the one on the card.

## 1.5 The side session is not a game, and nobody has measured what it is

**F-579.** The direction is certain and the magnitude is folklore.

- **MEASURED (corpus, F-464):** the mound is worth **≈ +2.0 mph over flat ground**, randomised within athlete at regulation distance — one of the very few true manipulations in this entire corpus. (Sample mean 76.5 mph: **off-population, directional only.**)
- **MEASURED (Driveline flat-ground dataset):** each athlete threw flat-ground throws **≈ 10 mph slower than on the mound** — but that is a *throwing program* comparison across distances and intents, not a matched-intent mound-vs-flat contrast, and it is not the same quantity as F-464's +2.0.
- **FOLKLORE (Blewett; PRP Baseball; coaching blogs, no data anywhere):** *"a 100%-effort bullpen is ~92% of in-game max; an 88–91 guy pens at 84–87."*

**The number everybody uses — how much softer a between-start side is than a game — has never been measured.** The two measured quantities bracket nothing useful: one is the mound's *existence* (+2.0 mph), the other is a long-toss program (−10 mph). The thing in between — same mound, same pitcher, Tuesday side versus Friday first inning — **is one afternoon of radar logging that no published source has done.**

## 1.7 🚨 AND THE RECOVERY LITERATURE REPEATS YESTERDAY'S STRUCTURAL ERROR, EXACTLY

**F-581.** The best-designed study this sweep found is a real intervention:

**"Acute and Delayed Recovery Effects of Percussive and Ice Therapies on Shoulder Strength and Sensorimotor Function After Baseball Pitching"** — *JSCR* 2026, PMID 42172289, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000005507.
**n = 16 collegiate pitchers.** Simulated five-inning game (**75 pitches**). Within-subject, two treatments: **10-min percussive massage** vs **15-min icing** to the dominant shoulder. Measured at baseline, post-pitching, post-treatment, **and 48 h post-treatment.**
**Result: icing raised external-rotation peak torque at 120°/s and 300°/s at Post-48 (43.8 ± 11.4 and 41.7 ± 10.7 N·m) relative to post-pitching and post-treatment, and reduced soreness. Percussive massage did not.**

`CAUSALITY: INTERVENTION`. Controlled, within-subject, a real 48-hour window. **And the outcome is isokinetic torque and a soreness scale.**

## 1.8 The hidden-workload arithmetic — DERIVED IN-CYCLE

**F-584.** The corpus already holds **F-137**: bullpens, flat-ground, long toss, showcases and testing days load the same tissue, and game-day pitch counts systematically undercount true workload. Today supplies the arithmetic for the specific case.

From **F-211** (effort-level dosing, the corpus's own entry): per 25% reduction in perceived effort, elbow stress falls only **~13–19%** while velocity falls **~7.5–10%**. The relationship is **sublinear** — which runs in both directions. **Reducing intent buys you much less stress relief than it costs you in velocity; and *raising* intent on a submaximal session costs you almost the full stress of a maximal one.**

**Consequence, stated as arithmetic:** a 30-pitch side session taken from ~85–88% intent to ~95%+ intent does **not** become "a slightly harder side." By F-211's exchange rate it lands within roughly **10–15% of the per-pitch elbow stress of 30 game pitches.** ⚠️ **F-211's inputs are n = 10 high-school and n = 19 collegiate athletes, and the extrapolation past the measured effort range is mine, not the source's. This is a bracket, not a number.**

## 1.10 What it would take to know — DERIVED IN-CYCLE

**F-586.** Building on the corpus's existing power arithmetic (F-186; and the between-session velocity entry at an assumed SD ≈ 1.0 mph).

Paired comparison, α = .05 two-tailed, 80% power: **n ≈ 7.85/d² starts.**

| Effect you want to detect | d (at SD = 1.0 mph) | Paired starts needed |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 mph | 0.50 | **≈ 32** |
| 0.75 mph | 0.75 | **≈ 14** |
| 1.0 mph | 1.00 | **≈ 8** |

**A college starter makes roughly 15–17 starts in a season.**

> 🚨 **THEREFORE: one pitcher cannot evaluate a between-start protocol change at the half-tick scale in one season. Not "it would be hard" — the observations do not exist.** At 0.5 mph he needs about twice a season's worth of starts. He can detect a full tick in half a season, and a full tick is not what a schedule change buys.
>
> **The only design that fits a college program is at STAFF level: six starters × 15 starts ≈ 90 paired observations, with the protocol CROSSED OVER — alternating weeks, not a before/after — because a before/after over a season is confounded with in-season velocity drift, which this corpus has already registered as large (F-601-area body-mass work; velocities reported dropping as much as 10 mph across a season alongside 6–10 lb of weight loss, snippet-only).**
>
> ⚠️ The SD = 1.0 mph input is **an assumption the corpus has carried, not a measurement.** It is also **one query against Ole Miss's own radar log** — mean fastball velocity per start, per pitcher, SD across starts. **That single number would retire the bracket, and it is free.**

# STEP 3 — TRANSLATE (the coach interrogates the other two)

### Q1 — COACH → BIOMECHANIST: *"You've just told me rest days are worth 0.06 ERA. Do I stop caring what day it is?"*

**No, and the distinction is the whole topic.** The null is on the **calendar**, not on **load**. Bradbury & Forman's own coefficients say the 5- and 10-game rolling pitch load carries 2–3× the weight of the last outing (F-575). **The week is not a recovery interval you count in days; it is a slot in a three-week load average.** The calendar null is real and the load effect is real, and they are not in tension — they are the same result stated twice.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Nobody can show me that a sixth day makes your Friday better. What they can show me is that three weeks of heavy Fridays makes this Friday worse. We manage the month, not the week."*
> **The drill is:** nothing. This one is a scheduling decision, and the intervention is that I stop adding Tuesday work to buy back a day I didn't lose.
> **On video the failure looks like:** nothing visible on day +3. It shows up as a mid-April arm that looks like a March arm with the top 1.5 mph shaved off and a release point that drifts after pitch 60.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** rolling 10-outing pitch total per starter, plotted against his own mean fastball velocity per start, all season. **Not one start. The unit of analysis is the trend line, and it needs the whole season to have a slope.**

### Q2 — COACH → ANATOMIST: *"Day +1, his IR is still down. Is the Tuesday side session hurting him or helping him?"*

**Unknown, and I want to be precise about which part is unknown.** The tissue state is established: ROM and strength are measurably depressed immediately post-outing and **still depressed at 24 h** (F-126), and the ice/percussive trial shows external-rotation torque still recovering out to **48 h** (F-581). So day +1 is not a neutral canvas — that much is solid.

**What is not known is whether throwing into that state on day +2 or +3 does anything to day +5.** Nobody has run it (F-580). And the anatomist will concede something to the coach here that the corpus's own F-214 does not concede: **ArmCare's position — that underloading the arm may be the bigger physiological problem than throwing more — is not absurd**, and a template built purely on "tissue is depressed, therefore rest" has assumed the answer.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Your arm is measurably not back on Sunday. That's a fact. What Tuesday's pen does to next Friday is a guess — mine included — so we're going to keep Tuesday small and honest instead of pretending it's free."*
> **The drill is:** the side session stays, and it gets a **pitch count and an intent cap that are written down before he walks out there** — because the thing that actually varies is intent, and intent is the expensive variable (F-584).
> **On video the failure looks like:** a Tuesday side that has a hitter, a catcher framing, and a guy reaching back on 0-2. That is a game appearance wearing a tracksuit.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** his own day +1 and day +3 arm-strength readings, same device, same time of day, trending back toward his baseline across the season rather than stair-stepping down. ⚠️ **And I do not yet know this device's MDC, which means I do not yet know how big a drop has to be before it is real** — that is the egress-blocked IJSPT paper, and until I have it, I read the trend and refuse to react to any single reading.

### Q3 — COACH → BOTH: *"The Marlins put a hitter in the box on the side day. Do I copy it?"*

**The anatomist says no, not as described.** Not because game-likeness is wrong — it is probably right — but because **the article itself reports that the hitter raises intent beyond what the pitcher should be throwing between starts**, and F-584's arithmetic says the stress penalty for that is close to full freight. **The Marlins bought transfer with workload and the reporting does not say they accounted for it.**

**The biomechanist says the idea is the best-designed thing in today's sweep and still isn't evidence.** Two named pitchers developing new pitches is an anecdote. There is no control staff, no counterfactual, and the organisation changed several things at once.

**The coach's resolution, which is a narrowing rather than a copy:** take the **prescription**, leave the **hitter**. The genuinely transferable idea in the Marlins' program is not the batter — it is that **the side session has a written objective before it starts** instead of being thirty pitches of ambient mound time.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Tuesday has one job and you're going to tell me what it is before you throw. Not 'get my work in.' Something like: ten sweepers that finish below the zone to a right-handed hitter's back foot."*
> **The drill is:** every side session opens with a declared target and a declared pitch count, and gets scored. This is F-185/F-356's **declare-the-target** rule applied to the side session — **free, and the corpus already rates it the highest-value change available.**
> **On video the failure looks like:** no stated objective, so the pitcher defaults to the thing he's already good at, throws it at 95% because it feels good, and leaves the mound having paid game-pitch stress for zero skill.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** **miss distance in inches on the declared pitch type, scored every side, pooled across the season.** Per F-186 that is **~200 tracked pitches before a 2-inch improvement is claimable** — at 10 declared pitches a side, **that is 20 side sessions, i.e. most of a season.** One good Tuesday proves nothing and I will not let anyone tell me otherwise.

### Q4 — COACH → BIOMECHANIST: *"Half a tick. Can I see it on my own staff this spring?"*

**On one pitcher, no.** F-586: detecting 0.5 mph paired needs **≈ 32 starts** at SD = 1.0 mph; he has 15–17. **The data to answer the question will not exist at the end of the season.**

**On the staff, yes, if you cross it over.** Six starters × 15 starts ≈ 90 paired observations. **Alternate the protocol week to week per pitcher — never a first-half/second-half split**, because in-season velocity drift is large and would swallow the effect whole.

> **So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing — this one is for me, and the discipline it buys is that **I stop announcing that a change worked.**
> **The drill is:** alternating-week assignment, written down in February, not decided in April.
> **On video the failure looks like:** me watching one guy throw 93 on a Friday after a new Tuesday routine and telling the staff the routine works.
> **Here's how we know it's working:** we don't, for a season, and **that is the correct answer rather than a failure of the plan.** ⚠️ **First, free, and before any of it: pull last season's radar log and compute each starter's start-to-start SD of mean fastball velocity.** If it comes back at 1.4 instead of 1.0, every number in this table roughly doubles and the staff-level design fails too.
