# Daily Research — THE PRE-OUTING WARM-UP: what the bullpen before the first inning is actually for

**Date:** 2026-09-20 · **Cycle 18** · Findings **F-413 → F-424**
**New library file:** `library/pre-game-warmup.md` · **New disputes:** #33, #34
**SIX PRIMARY TEXTS READ IN FULL** (sixth consecutive reading cycle).

**Topic selection.** `priming` and `pregame` both return **0 hits** in `FINDINGS.md`. `warm-up` returns 31, and every one of them is the **between-innings** break (`library/between-inning-break.md`, F-351→F-364) or the post-outing stretch. The **pre-outing** warm-up — the 20–30 pitches a starter throws in the bullpen before the first inning, and everything he does in the 45 minutes before that — is uncovered. It is also the single routine in the program that every pitcher performs before every outing and that nobody has ever tested.

---

## 0. THE ONE-PARAGRAPH RESULT

Three physiological channels have been proposed for the pre-game routine — **stretching**, **ball-weight priming**, and **post-activation performance enhancement**. All three have now been checked at the source. The ball-weight channel is a **genuine manipulated null on a throwing population** (F-413). The PAPE channel is a **genuine manipulated null in three randomised crossover trials**, and the direction is *negative*: jump height was **highest after the general warm-up and lower at every point afterwards** (F-415). The stretching channel's headline null is real but sits on a **61-mph recreational sample** (F-414). Meanwhile the one thing about the pre-game bullpen that is **solidly measured** is its **size**: 27.2 ± 9.4 pitches in high school, and **45.4% of a professional pitcher's entire season volume is "hidden"** (F-418) — a proportion that is **invariant from high school to MLB**. So the pre-game routine is large, unmeasured, variable by a factor of two between pitchers, and **nobody has ever manipulated its volume and measured what came out.** That is the gap this cycle opens.

## 1. THE ANATOMY–PHYSIOLOGY PASS — what a warm-up can and cannot do to tissue

The mechanistic case for a warm-up rests on four things, and they do not have equal standing:

| Channel | Mechanism | Time course | Does it plausibly reach a pitch? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Muscle temperature** | Q₁₀ effects on cross-bridge cycling rate, reduced viscous resistance | rises over 5–15 min of activity, decays with τ of order 300–1800 s (corpus §2, `between-inning-break.md`) | Reaches **lower-body power**. Whether that reaches ball velocity is the corpus's oldest open question — **F-004, F-358, F-359 say no.** |
| **Phosphagen / metabolic readiness** | PCr availability | half-time 20–30 s (F-132) | **Irrelevant pre-game.** A rested pitcher is saturated. This channel only bites *within* an outing. |
| **Post-activation potentiation (myosin RLC phosphorylation)** | Ca²⁺-sensitivity increase after a conditioning contraction | peak 3–10 min, coexists with fatigue | **See F-415. In three RCTs it did not beat a general warm-up.** |
| **Motor calibration / release-point convergence** | not a tissue property — a *skill* property | unknown; nobody has measured it in throwing | **The only channel not yet falsified, and the only one nobody has measured.** |

**The anatomist's position:** the first three channels are all *capacity* channels, and this corpus has spent eighteen cycles establishing that this population is **not capacity-limited at the margin** in ways that a 30-minute routine can move. A pitcher who already throws 90 is not going to find another mph in a muscle that is 1 °C warmer. The fourth channel — calibration — is the one where a warm-up could plausibly be doing real work, and it is the one nobody has instrumented.

**Adaptation timeline note:** nothing in a pre-game routine is an adaptation. Every effect discussed here is acute and gone within the hour. This matters for the marker/lever rule in an unusual way — **acute crossover designs are the one place this literature is genuinely well-designed**, because you can randomise a warm-up within-subject on consecutive days. The problem is not the designs. The problem is the populations and the outcome variables.

## 2. THE BIOMECHANICS PASS — six papers, read at the source

### 2.1 ⭐ The ball-weight priming channel — MANIPULATED, ON A THROWING POPULATION, NULL (F-413)

**Shin Y-A, Choi W-H (2018).** *Effects of weighted baseball throwing during warm-up on ball velocity and upper extremity muscle activation in baseball pitchers.* **J Exerc Rehabil 14(3):436–444.** PMCID **PMC6028211**, PMID 30018931, DOI 10.12965/jer.1836146.073. **Read in full 2026-09-20** via `pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com`.

n = 12 pitchers, Korean (Jangan HS, Suwon + Dankook University), age **20.2 ± 1.64**, career **10.8 ± 2.17 yr**, all overhand, no injury history. Within-subject, three warm-up conditions: **light ball (LB)**, **regular ball (RB)**, **overweight ball (OB)**, each followed by maximum-effort pitching with a **regular** ball from a full mound at 18.44 m. Radar: Applied Concepts Sport Radar SP78585.

**Table 2 — ball velocity (km/h, mean ± SD):**

| Warm-up condition | Velocity during warm-up | **Max-effort with RB after** |
|---|---|---|
| LB → RB | 121.00 ± 4.05 | **121.58 ± 4.03** (75.55 mph) |
| RB → RB | 119.83 ± 4.45 | **120.92 ± 4.36** (75.14 mph) |
| OB → RB | 85.91 ± 7.16 | **121.75 ± 3.19** (75.65 mph) |

**Post-hoc on the max-effort column: F = 0.138, p = 0.871.** Total spread across the three conditions: **0.83 km/h = 0.52 mph.** Authors: *"the ball velocity during TBME with an RB subsequent to WU did not show significant differences according to the weight of the baseball; thus, using weighted baseballs does not appear to have a significant acute effect on the ball velocity."*

⚠️ **POPULATION: 121 km/h = 75.1 mph. Ten mph below this corpus's floor. SAMPLE MISMATCH — DIRECTIONAL ONLY.**

**What it does establish, and it is not nothing:** this is a **within-subject manipulation of the exact variable coaches argue about**, with a **null that is 0.5 mph wide** and an F of 0.138 — i.e. the between-condition mean square is about one-seventh of the error mean square. The warm-up ball changed the *warm-up* velocity enormously (OB threw 53 mph, LB threw 75 mph — a 22 mph spread, p < 0.001) and changed the **outcome by nothing.** The manipulation worked; the transfer did not.

**What it does NOT establish:** a precise bound. The within-subject SD is not reported, so the minimum detectable effect cannot be computed. With n = 12 and a crossover, the study is powered for something in the neighbourhood of 0.8–1.3 mph, not 0.3.

**Secondary result worth keeping:** EMG showed **real, significant differences in muscle activation by ball weight during the warm-up** (upper trapezius, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, serratus anterior, deltoid, lat, triceps — all p < 0.05 somewhere). So the warm-up ball demonstrably changed *what the arm did*, and the ball still came out the same speed. **That is the strongest form of a null: the manipulation is verified at the muscle and absent at the outcome.**

### 2.2 The stretching channel — the headline null is a 61-mph sample (F-414)

**Williams M, Harveson L, Melton J, Delobel A, Puentedura EJ (2013).** *The Acute Effects of Upper Extremity Stretching on Throwing Velocity in Baseball Throwers.* **J Sports Med (Hindawi) 2013:481490.** PMCID **PMC4590899**, PMID 26464880, DOI 10.1155/2013/481490. **Read in full 2026-09-20.**

n = 27 males, age 25.1 ± 2.4. Randomised crossover, counterbalanced (9 subjects started with each condition), three separate test days. Conditions: **static stretch**, **PNF**, **no stretch** — targeting the shoulder internal rotators (pec major, subscapularis, lat). 10 max-effort pitches before, 10 after.

**This is the paper every "stretching is fine before you throw" claim traces back to. Here is its sample, in the authors' own words:**

> *"We were precluded from using professional or collegiate-level baseball players and therefore sought young healthy males with varying levels of baseball throwing experience who were not currently playing at a competitive level."*

And its own limitation: *"A third of the subjects who participated in the study had 0–5 years of baseball throwing experience."*

**Table 2 (units = km/h):**

| Condition | Average velocity | Peak velocity |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-static | 92.4 (17.49) | 97.5 (17.27) |
| Post-static | 90.6 (17.45) | 96.1 (16.85) |
| Pre-PNF | 92.2 (17.48) | 97.4 (16.82) |
| Post-PNF | 90.8 (17.11) | 96.2 (16.32) |
| Pre-control | 93.5 (17.04) | **98.8 (17.30)** |
| Post-control | 92.4 (16.72) | 98.2 (17.09) |

🚨 **Peak velocity in the best condition: 98.8 km/h = 61.4 mph. Average: 58.1 mph. The SD is 17 km/h = 10.6 mph on a mean of 92 — a coefficient of variation of 19%.** This is not a pitching sample. It is a sample of men who can throw a baseball.

**Results:** no interaction for average (F(2,50) = 0.534, p = 0.589) or peak (F(2,50) = 0.058, p = 0.944). No main effect of time (average F(1,25) = 3.075, p = 0.092; peak F(1,25) = 0.065, p = 0.800).

**⚠️ THE ODD RESULT THE AUTHORS COULD NOT EXPLAIN.** The main effect **for condition** *was* significant (average F(2,50) = 5.267, p = 0.008; peak F(2,50) = 4.342, p = 0.018) — subjects threw harder on their control days **both before and after** the intervention. In a counterbalanced crossover, a **pre-intervention** difference between conditions is not an effect of the intervention; it is noise, order, or a day effect. The authors say so: *"the significant findings of main effect for stretching groups are difficult to explain."* The magnitude is 1.1–1.3 km/h = **0.7–0.8 mph**, and the radar gun is a **Bushnell Velocity Speed Gun, accuracy ±1.0 mph.** *The paper's only significant finding is smaller than its measurement error.*

**The on-population study exists and I could not read it.** Haag SJ, Wright GA, Gillette CM, Greany JF (2010), *Effects of acute static stretching of the throwing shoulder on pitching performance of NCAA Division III baseball players*, **J Strength Cond Res 24(2):452–457**, PMID 20072054, DOI 10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c06d9c. **journals.lww.com, pubmed and ovid are all egress-blocked.** From search snippets only: it reports **no effect on velocity or accuracy** in D-III pitchers. ⚠️ **SNIPPET-ONLY. Its sample size and its sample's mean velocity are both unknown to this corpus.** It goes to the verification queue.

### 2.3 ⭐ The PAPE channel — three RCTs, and it does not beat a warm-up (F-415)

**Rappelt L, Held S, Wiedenmann T, Micke F, Donath L (2024).** *Post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) protocols do not further increase jumping performance beyond warm-up effects: findings from three acute randomized crossover trials.* **Front Physiol 15:1447421.** PMCID **PMC11350113**, PMID 39206386, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2024.1447421. **Read in full 2026-09-20.**

Three separate randomised crossover trials, **n = 18 + 28 + 20 = 66**, all with 1RM familiarisation:
- **Study I:** isometric squat 3 s @ 130% 1RM vs 6 s @ 65% 1RM (Squat-1RM 146 ± 19 kg — a genuinely strong sample)
- **Study II:** squat 3 × 3 @ 85% 1RM vs local quadriceps electromyostimulation
- **Study III:** squat vs chest press, 4 reps @ 80% 1RM

**The design innovation is the one that matters:** CMJ was measured **after the general warm-up (PRE)**, **after the muscle-specific warm-up (POST_WU)**, and then at 2/3/6/7/11 min after the PAPE stimulus. Most PAPE studies have no PRE-warm-up anchor at all, so they cannot separate potentiation from the warm-up that delivered it.

**Result. The highest jump in the whole protocol was at PRE — after the general warm-up, before anything else.**

- Study I: significant effect of time (F(2,85) = 3.67, p < 0.05, ω²p = 0.06). PRE vs POST_2 significant (p = 0.035). ISO-PAPE-130: 0.392 ± 0.064 → 0.388 ± 0.059 m. ISO-PAPE-65: 0.394 ± 0.067 → 0.383 ± 0.057 m. **No interaction (p = 0.182), no condition effect (p = 0.102).**
- Study II: large time effect (F(4,243) = 47.8, p < 0.001, ω²p = 0.43). **PRE > POST_WU (p = 0.009) > POST_3, POST_7, POST_11 (all p < 0.001), and it stays down.** No interaction (p = 0.746), no condition effect (p = 0.214).
- Study III: **nothing significant at all.**
- Test–retest ICC(3,1) = 0.938–0.968 — the instrument was good.

Authors: *"the CMJ jumping height slightly decreased from the general to the specific warm-up and subsequently remained at this significantly lower level, regardless of the condition."* And: *"Prior to tasks requiring explosive strength, general and sport-specific warm-up strategies should be used."*

**⚠️ THE HONEST CAVEAT, WHICH THE ABSTRACT UNDERSELLS AND THE INDUSTRY WILL OVERSELL IN THE OPPOSITE DIRECTION.** Every post-hoc SMD in this paper is **trivial**: 0.03, 0.05, 0.06, 0.09, 0.10, 0.12, 0.16, 0.18. The abstract's "≤8.2 ± 4.6%, SMD ≤0.39" is the upper edge of a range whose body is near zero. So the correct sentence is **not** "priming makes you worse." It is: **"after a general warm-up, nothing else you do moves the needle, and if it moves it at all it moves it down."**

### 2.4 The upper-body PAPE meta-analysis the industry quotes is the exact quantity Rappelt falsifies (F-416)

**Krzysztofik M, Wilk M, Stastny P, Golas A (2021).** *Post-activation Performance Enhancement in the Bench Press Throw: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.* **Front Physiol**, PMCID **PMC7844331**, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2020.598628. **Read in full 2026-09-20.**

11 studies, **174 resistance-trained men** (age 25.2 ± 2.1; BP relative strength 1.31 ± 0.14 kg·kg⁻¹). Search to August 2020.

- **Overall PAPE effect on bench-press throw: ES = 0.33, p < 0.01 — "small."**
- Bench press @ **60–84% 1RM: ES = 0.43** (best); ballistic–plyometric 0.29; **≥85% 1RM: 0.23**; >100% 1RM: 0.22; concentric-only: 0.11.
- **Single set (0.37) > multiple sets (0.29).**
- Rest interval, **below** 85% 1RM: 5–7 min (0.48) > 0.15–4 min (0.40) > ≥8 min (0.36).
- Rest interval, **above** 85% 1RM: 5–7 min (0.30) ≈ 8 min (0.29), but **0.15–4 min = −0.13 (negative).**

🚨 **READ THE METHODS SENTENCE.** *"Meta-analyses of standardized mean effect size (ES) between **pre-CA mean and post-CA mean** from individual studies were conducted."* **The pooled ES = 0.33 is a within-condition pre-post difference.** It is not a comparison against a control arm that kept warming up. **That is precisely and exactly the quantity Rappelt et al. designed their three trials to decompose — and when you put a general-warm-up anchor in front of it, it goes away.** The meta-analysis and the RCTs are not in conflict; the meta-analysis is measuring warm-up and calling it potentiation.

### 2.5 The "throwing" in the throwing-PAPE literature is a seated chest pass (F-417)

**Stastny P, et al. (2023).** *Antagonist activation exercises elicit similar post-activation performance enhancement as agonist activities on throwing performance.* **BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil**, PMCID **PMC10044369**, DOI 10.1186/s13102-023-00657-9. Read in full 2026-09-20.

n = 13 **professional Czech female softball players**. A priori power: 12 needed for ES = 0.4. Outcome: **a 2 kg medicine ball chest throw, performed seated, legs at 90°, head/shoulders against a wall, trunk against a chair.** Bench press CA: 5.72 ± 0.5 → 5.91 ± 0.4 m/s.

⚠️ **This is a constrained bilateral chest press projected into flight.** It shares no kinematic feature with a pitch: no stride, no pelvis–trunk sequence, no external rotation, no single-arm ballistic acceleration. It is in the search results for "PAPE throwing velocity" and it is not a throwing study in this corpus's sense. **SAMPLE AND CONSTRUCT MISMATCH — not directional, not usable.**

Two studies, both read in full, at opposite ends of the level ladder, and they agree.

## 3. THE DERIVATION — the pre-game thermal gap, using the corpus's own model (F-419)

The corpus already owns a calibrated cooling model (`library/between-inning-break.md` §2, F-356): an exponential decay fitted so that **ΔT(900 s) = 1.5 °C**, converted at **~3% loss of lower-body power per 1 °C**.

The between-inning break is 120 s. **The pre-game gap is not.** The starter's last bullpen pitch and his first game pitch are separated by lineup exchange, announcements, the anthem, the walk in — and for the **visiting** starter, by the **entire top of the first plus a changeover.** MLB 2025 nine-inning games average **2:38**; across ~17.5 half-innings that is **~9.0 min per half-inning including its ~2:05 break.**

Reading the corpus's own decay at longer intervals:

| Elapsed | τ = 300 s | τ = 600 s | τ = 1800 s | **Range** | **≈ power cost @ 3%/°C** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **120 s** (the between-inning break — corpus value) | 0.52 | 0.35 | 0.25 | 0.20–0.52 °C | 0.6–1.6% |
| **480 s** (8 min) | 1.26 | 1.06 | 0.89 | **0.89–1.26 °C** | **2.7–3.8%** |
| **600 s** (10 min) | 1.37 | 1.22 | 1.08 | **1.08–1.37 °C** | **3.2–4.1%** |
| **1200 s** (20 min) | 1.55 | 1.67 | 1.86 | **1.55–1.86 °C** | **4.7–5.6%** |

**DERIVED IN-CYCLE. So the pre-game gap is worth roughly two to four times the between-inning break on the thermal channel.** The corpus's verdict on the between-inning break was *"the margin is not close — don't have this argument"* (F-357). **Four times "not close" is still not close, and it dies at the same step it always dies at:** the quantity being moved is **lower-body power**, and F-004 (jump height vs velocity **r ≈ 0.07**), F-358 (the re-warm-up meta moves jump and not sprint) and F-359 (**117 maximum-effort pitches moved no squat-jump variable at all**) say that quantity does not reach a fastball in this population.

**⚠️ AND THERE IS A DAMPER THE MODEL DOES NOT INCLUDE.** Both starters get **8 warm-up pitches on the game mound** immediately before the first inning. Those pitches are themselves a re-warm-up, and F-362 already found that *when* a pitcher throws his warm-ups inside a break barely matters. So the derived 3–4% is an **upper bound on an upper bound.** This is the substance of **Dispute #34**.

## 5. TRANSLATION — the coach's four questions

### Q1 (Coach → Biomechanist): "Every one of these nulls is on a 61-mph guy or a 75-mph guy. Why should I believe any of it applies to a kid who runs it up to 94?"

**Answer: you shouldn't believe the magnitudes, and you should believe the direction — for one specific reason.** Restriction of range (Luera 2020, n = 149 pros, r = .17–.29) says correlations *shrink* in hard-throwing samples. It says the same about intervention effects that work through capacity: an 85+ arm is further along every capacity curve, so an acute capacity nudge has **less** room, not more. **A null in a 75-mph sample is, if anything, conservative for a 92-mph sample.** The one place this reasoning fails is if the mechanism is *skill* rather than capacity — a better thrower might get more out of calibration, not less. Which is exactly why the surviving hypothesis in this cycle is a command hypothesis.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** nothing. This is a staff-level conclusion, not a cue.
**The drill is:** stop adding things to the pre-game routine that are sold as velocity.
**On video the failure looks like:** a pitcher doing eight extra "priming" reps at 7:02 for a 7:05 first pitch, having already thrown his 27.
**Here's how we know it's working:** you can't know from one outing. See Q4.

### Q2 (Coach → Anatomist): "Fine, but the overweight ball *feels* like it wakes the arm up. Twelve guys in Korea doesn't tell me it doesn't."

**Answer: the EMG says you're right about the feeling and wrong about the effect.** Shin & Choi measured the muscles, not just the gun. The overweight ball **significantly changed activation in the upper trap, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, serratus anterior, deltoid, lat and triceps** — the arm genuinely did something different. And the ball came out at 121.75 km/h instead of 120.92. **The sensation is real and it is not the mechanism.** This is the cleanest available demonstration that "it feels like it's working" and "it is working" are separate measurements in this domain.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"If the heavy ball makes your arm feel ready, keep it — it isn't hurting you. Just don't expect it to show up on the gun, and don't add pitches to get it."*
**The drill is:** unchanged; keep whatever he already does.
**On video the failure looks like:** the pen creeping from 25 pitches to 40 because the "priming" got bolted on top of the old routine instead of replacing part of it.
**Here's how we know it's working:** it isn't a velocity intervention, so don't measure it with a gun. It's a readiness routine. Measure it with the pen count.

### Q3 (Coach → both): "Give me the actual number. How many pitches should he throw in the pen?"

**Answer: nobody knows, and I want to be explicit that this is a real unknown rather than a hedge.** The only measured value is **27.2 ± 9.4 (high school starters)**. The only professional datum is a season total. **There is no dose-response study of warm-up throw number against subsequent velocity or command in baseball — and I checked handball and tennis too, where the acute-warm-up literature is much denser, and there isn't one there either (F-423).** The coaching consensus number (15–20) is folklore and is ~40% below the only measurement of what pitchers actually do.

**What the evidence does license, negatively:** Rappelt says that after a general warm-up, additional specific work bought nothing and trended down. Shin & Choi says the *content* of the last few throws doesn't matter. Together those point toward **"enough, then stop"** rather than **"more"** — but that is an inference across a transfer gap, and I am labelling it **WEAK**.

**So what I tell the pitcher is:** *"Your pen is done when you've located your three pitches, not when you've hit a number."*
**The drill is:** chart it (Q4).
**On video the failure looks like:** the last five pen pitches being repeats of a pitch he already located, thrown because the routine says five more.
**Here's how we know it's working:** Q4.

### Q4 (Coach → Biomechanist): "Then what do I actually measure, and how long until I know anything?"

**The pre-game bullpen has a standard deviation of 9.4 pitches. That is a natural experiment already running in your program, for free, and nobody has ever read it out** (F-421).

**THE AUDIT.** For every starter, for the rest of fall and into the spring, record three numbers: **(a) pre-game bullpen pitch count**, **(b) first-inning first-fastball velocity**, **(c) first-inning strike%** — and, if TrackMan is running, **(d) first-inning location SD in inches.** Nothing else. It is one line per outing.

**Detection, done honestly.** Two-sample, α = .05, 80% power, n per group = 15.7 σ²/Δ². Using the corpus's working within-pitcher release-speed SD of **1.0 mph** (F-289):

| Effect you want to detect | Outings per bucket | Total outings |
|---|---|---|
| **1.0 mph** | 16 | **32** |
| **0.75 mph** | 28 | **56** |
| **0.5 mph** | 63 | **126** |

**A 14-arm staff throwing ~15 outings each is ~210 outings a season.** So a **median split on pen volume detects 0.5 mph inside one season at staff level** with pitcher fixed effects — and detects 1.0 mph inside **one starter's two seasons**. This is genuinely executable and it requires no equipment you do not have.

**For command**, the corpus's ~200-tracked-pitch rule applies: a first inning is ~15–20 pitches, so **~12 outings per bucket** gets you there. **Command is the cheaper measurement here, and it is also the channel the mechanism section says is the only one still alive.**

⚠️ **Two confounds to design around, stated up front.** (1) **Bullpen volume is not random** — a pitcher who feels bad throws more. So the naive regression is contaminated in the direction that would *manufacture* a negative result. Use the residual after regressing pen count on pitcher identity and days' rest, or restrict to pitchers whose pen count varies for scheduling reasons. (2) **First-inning performance is confounded with facing the top of the order** — the corpus already holds this in **F-287** (lineup-slot composition bias). Velocity is immune to it; **strike% and outcomes are not.** Use velocity and location SD, not ERA.

### BONUS DESIGN — the quasi-random instrument (F-420)

There is a cleaner instrument hiding in the schedule. **The visiting starter's gap between his last bullpen pitch and his first game pitch is longer than the home starter's by exactly one half-inning — the top of the first — and the length of that half-inning is quasi-random with respect to his own state.** It depends on the opposing pitcher and your own offence, not on him.

**Design:** for visiting starts only, regress first-inning first-fastball velocity and first-inning location SD on the **duration of the top of the first**. Within-pitcher. This is F-363's long-half-inning design moved to the one interval in the game that is longest and that every starter experiences.
**Why it is better than home-vs-away:** home/away is confounded with travel, sleep (F-410), crowd and umpire. Top-of-the-first duration is not.
**What the thermal derivation predicts:** a 4-minute top of the first vs a 16-minute one is ~0.9 °C ≈ 2.7% of lower-body power ≈ **essentially nothing on velocity if F-004 is right, and something on release scatter if the calibration channel is real.** **The two hypotheses make opposite predictions on which outcome moves.** That is a real test.

## 6. CROSS-EXAMINATION

### ① BIOMECHANIST → COACH and → the corpus itself: "You have now concluded, two cycles running, that a null on velocity is secretly a finding about command. Is that a discovery or a house style?"

**The charge, stated fairly.** On 2026-09-19 the sleep cycle concluded: *a short night does not take his fastball, it takes his strike zone* (F-406). Today the warm-up cycle concludes: the physiological channels are dead on velocity, so **the surviving channel is motor calibration** — command again. **Two consecutive topics, two different literatures, same escape hatch.** The pattern is suspicious because command is **the corpus's least-measured outcome**, which makes it the cheapest place to relocate a hypothesis you don't want to abandon. Stride length and extension both died because a plausible story outran a measurement; "it's really a command effect" is a plausible story with **no measurement behind it in either cycle.**

**COACH'S DEFENCE — PARTIAL, AND THE CHARGE IS PARTLY CONCEDED.** The sleep case had something today's does not: Craven 2022 actually measured **target-accuracy tasks** and found −20.9% against −6.26% for power (F-400). The construct match was arguable, but there was a measured accuracy outcome. **Today there is no measured accuracy outcome anywhere.** Not one of the six papers measured where the ball went. Williams measured velocity; Shin & Choi measured velocity and EMG; Rappelt measured jump height; Krzysztofik measured bench-throw velocity; Zaremski and Erickson counted pitches. **So the command channel today is an inference from the absence of everything else, which is the weakest possible support.**

**RESOLUTION: CONCEDED in substance, and the finding is downgraded accordingly.** F-419's command claim is labelled **WEAK, not EMERGING**, and the phrase used in the report is **"the only channel nobody has falsified,"** not "the channel that matters." **→ Dispute #33, OPEN.** The discriminating evidence is cheap and named: Q4's audit measures velocity and location scatter **simultaneously**, so the first program to run it settles whether this is a discovery or a habit. ⚠️ **Flagging this for the next three cycles: if a third topic ends by relocating to command without a measured accuracy outcome, the pattern is method, not physiology.**

### ② ANATOMIST → BIOMECHANIST: "You are treating Rappelt's trivial SMDs as if they were a demonstration that priming is counterproductive. They are not."

**Conceded, and the report was edited before publication.** Every post-hoc SMD in Rappelt is 0.03–0.18. The abstract's 8.2% is the ceiling of a bracket, not the estimate. **The defensible claim is "PAPE did not beat a general warm-up in any of three RCTs, with no condition effect and no interaction in any of them" — a null, not a reversal.** The negative direction is noted and explicitly not leaned on. Recorded as a correction within the cycle.

### ③ BIOMECHANIST → ANATOMIST: "Your thermal table is the corpus's own model extrapolated to four times the interval it was calibrated at. That is not free."

**Partially conceded.** The model was fitted to a single anchor, ΔT(900 s) = 1.5 °C, so reading it at 480–1200 s is **interpolation inside the calibration span**, not extrapolation — that part of the objection fails. But the τ bracket (300–1800 s) is itself a guess, and the fact that the three τ values *cross over* between 600 s and 1200 s (τ=1800 gives the smallest ΔT at 600 s and the largest at 1200 s) shows how weakly the bracket is pinned. **The honest statement is that the range 0.9–1.9 °C is a bracket whose width reflects ignorance of τ, not measurement error.** And the whole quantity is then multiplied by a transfer coefficient the corpus believes is near zero, so **the precision of the thermal number is not the binding constraint on the conclusion.** Recorded in `library/pre-game-warmup.md` §3.

### ④ COACH → BIOMECHANIST: "Your 'quasi-random instrument' isn't random. A long top of the first means my offence is hitting, which means the starter I'm measuring has a lead."

**Excellent objection, partially conceded, and it changes the analysis rather than killing it.** Top-of-the-first duration is correlated with **(a)** the opposing starter's command that day and **(b)** the game state the visiting starter inherits. (a) is exogenous to him. (b) is not: pitching with a 3-run lead may change effort (and F-365 says pressure raises peak output in elite college players, which would work *against* the thermal prediction). **Fix:** include runs scored in the top of the first as a covariate, and — better — use **pitch count of the top of the first** rather than runs, since a long scoreless half-inning (walks, fouls, mound visits) carries the time cost without the lead. **Recorded as a design amendment, not a retraction. → Dispute #34.**

### ⑤ ANATOMIST → COACH: "You wrote 'stop adding things to the pre-game routine.' The evidence supports 'adding things doesn't help.' Those are different sentences and the second one is not an instruction."

**Conceded. This is the marker/lever rule turned on my own recommendation, and it lands.** Nobody has manipulated *removal*. Rappelt manipulated *addition* against a warm-up control and found nothing; **subtraction below the general warm-up is untested and there is a real floor down there.** The recommendation is therefore reworded to: **"do not add pre-game work in pursuit of velocity"** — a null-supported prohibition on *addition*, which is what the evidence licenses — and explicitly **not** "cut the pen." **The audit in Q4 is what would license anything about cutting.**
